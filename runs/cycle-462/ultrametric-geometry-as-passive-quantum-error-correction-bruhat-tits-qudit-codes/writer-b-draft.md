# Ultrametric Geometry as Passive Quantum Error Correction: Bruhat–Tits Qudit Codes and the Non-Archimedean Bound (Revised)

## Abstract

Active quantum error correction (QEC) dominates the overhead of scalable quantum processors: repeated syndrome extraction, low-latency decoding, and large ancilla counts inflate hardware cost and energy consumption, and a passive alternative that eliminates measurement cycles would reduce system complexity. The ultrametric geometry of the $p$-adic numbers satisfies the strong triangle inequality $\|x+y\|_p\le\max(\|x\|_p,\|y\|_p)$, which forces balls in the associated Bruhat–Tits tree $T_{p+1}$ to be either nested or disjoint; whether this property can be turned into a genuine quantum error-correcting code with provable parameters is an open question. We define a ball code on $T_{p+1}$, prove a nesting lemma, and derive the exact code size $n_t=1+(p+1)(p^{t}-1)/(p-1)$ and a conditional distance $d=2t+1$ that holds given a branch-symmetric encoder we do not construct. Under independent depolarising noise at physical error rate $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$ we compute analytically a passive logical failure probability $P_{\mathrm{pass}}=9.980015\times10^{-6}$ for the flagship $p=3$, $t=1$ block ($n_1=5$ qudits), a suppression factor of $100.2$ over an unencoded qudit, and $P_{\mathrm{pass}}^{(2)}\approx6.73\times10^{-7}$ for $t=2$; correcting an arithmetic error in the prior version, the projected advantage over an active distance-3 baseline with measurement error $\varepsilon_m=\varepsilon$ is a factor $\approx3.99$, not $4.08$. All quantitative results are derived step by step, limitations and falsification criteria are stated, and the central claim remains conditional on constructing an explicit encoder.

## 1. Introduction

Scalable quantum computing is presently blocked by the overhead of active quantum error correction, which requires repeated syndrome extraction, low-latency decoding, and large ancilla resources. This overhead directly limits the size of computation that near-term hardware can run and inflates the thermodynamic cost of error-correction cycles; the physical-to-logical qubit overhead of surface-code-style schemes has been estimated, as an explicit assumption, in the range $10^{2}$–$10^{4}$ [11]. If a hardware platform could store logical information in a structure that *passively* blocks error propagation, the need for active cycles would disappear, reducing both hardware complexity and energy consumption, and the design variable would shift from ancilla budget and decoder latency to the static layout of the qudits themselves.

The non-Archimedean (ultrametric) geometry of the $p$-adic numbers provides a candidate structure. On the field $\mathbb{Q}_p$ the norm satisfies
$$\|x+y\|_p\le\max\bigl(\|x\|_p,\|y\|_p\bigr),$$
which implies that any two balls are either nested or disjoint. The combinatorial skeleton of this geometry is the Bruhat–Tits tree $T_{p+1}$, a homogeneous tree in which every vertex has degree $p+1$. By placing qudits at the vertices and encoding a logical qudit into a ball $B_t(v_0)$, we obtain a geometry in which local errors cannot coherently add across distinct branches. Hecke operators, which average over the $p+1$ forward neighbours of each vertex, serve as a natural gate set that respects the tree locality.

This paper is a post-publication revision of the version of this work published as [9]. The revision does three things. First, it corrects an arithmetic error in the active-baseline comparison of [9]: the active logical failure probability is $P_{\mathrm{act}}\approx3.98\times10^{-5}$, not $4.07\times10^{-5}$, and the passive advantage is a factor $\approx3.99$, not $4.08$ (Section 4.4). Second, it reconciles a numerical divergence between the binomial computation performed here and the value $P_{\mathrm{pass}}=5.992003\times10^{-6}$ reported for the same model parameters in [11]; we adopt the directly computed binomial value and state the convention behind the choice (Section 4.3). Third, it strengthens the conditional character of the distance claim: Proposition 1 is proved *given* a branch-symmetric encoder, and the explicit encoder remains the single most important open task, as [9] itself already flags in its Discussion.

No simulation, measurement, or hardware experiment is performed or reported in this paper; every quantitative claim is either an exact combinatorial identity, an analytic computation shown step by step, or a projection whose assumptions are stated explicitly.

## 2. Background and Related Work

We review the literature that directly informs the construction, citing specific points each source establishes.

**Continuous-time QEC [1].** Continuous-time quantum error correction treats both noise and correction as processes continuous in time, based on continuous weak measurements and feedback, and analyses them from the point of view of the subsystem principle, under which protected information resides in a subsystem $\mathcal{H}^{A}$ of the system Hilbert space and correctability means the existence of a CPTP recovery map $\mathcal{R}$ with $\mathrm{Tr}_{B}\{(\mathcal{P}^{AB}\circ\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E})(\sigma)\}=\mathrm{Tr}_{B}\{\sigma\}$ [1]. Its Outlook section notes that in the quantum-jump model the code-space fidelity reaches a quasi-equilibrium value usable to estimate performance, and that optimal CTQEC protocol construction remains open [1]. Our scheme shares the motivation of reducing discrete measurement rounds but seeks a geometry-based containment that eliminates any feedback operation entirely.

**Entanglement-assisted codes [2].** Entanglement-assisted quantum error-correcting codes extend the stabilizer formalism by allowing the sender to append pre-shared ebits before the encoding unitary, and their conclusion states that this allows construction of quantum codes from classical linear codes without imposing the dual-containing constraint, while entanglement can increase either the rate of communication or the number of correctable errors [2]. EAQECCs still rely on active syndrome measurement, so they do not address the passive-correction goal; our codes are not entanglement-assisted, and the protection mechanism is purely geometric.

**Qudit algorithms [3].** Efficient implementation of quantum algorithms on multilevel systems is supported by a transpilation pipeline whose Discussion establishes that qudit-based transpilation complexity is polynomial in qubit number and linear in gate number, with a greedy mapping finder of $\mathcal{O}(n^{3})$ complexity for qudit dimensions $4\le d\le7$; the same work demonstrates a $6$-qubit circuit on four $4$-level qudits with considerably fewer two-particle operations than a straightforward qubit implementation [3]. This motivates qudits of dimension $p$ as natural carriers for the tree-based code and indicates that qudit control tooling exists to attempt the encoder of Section 4.2.

**Classical-to-quantum coding surveys [4].** The survey establishes the shared skeleton of classical and quantum coding: both add redundancy, encode invertibly, and check a syndrome, and its Conclusions state that both quantum and classical schemes "add ancillary qubits/bits and measure a syndrome to protect information," with modern quantum code construction based on stabilizer codes [4]. This supplies the active, syndrome-based baseline family against which Section 4.4 compares, and makes precise what a passive scheme must eliminate: the syndrome-measurement step itself.

**Foundations of QEC [5].** The foundational treatment shows that general noise and relaxation of two-state systems can always be understood as a combination of Pauli operators acting on the system, and recalls the Knill–Laflamme framework as the general theoretical description of requirements for quantum error-correcting codes [5]. We adopt exactly this picture: our error model decomposes noise into Weyl (generalized Pauli) operators $X^{a}Z^{b}$ on single-vertex qudits, and our correctness proof targets the Knill–Laflamme conditions.

**Qudit-capable QEC expositions [6].** The exposition of quantum error correction defines a code as a subspace of a larger Hilbert space designed so that the most common errors move the state into an error space orthogonal to the code space [6]. This orthogonality picture is precisely how the disjointness of distinct balls in $T_{p+1}$ is used in Proposition 1: a weight-$(t+1)$ error moves amplitude into a ball orthogonal to the code ball, making it detectable.

**Operator-algebra QEC [7].** The stabilizer formalism for operator algebra QEC (OAQEC) generalizes Gottesman's stabilizer formalism and Poulin's OQEC, and its introduction records that OQEC "brought together traditional QEC with passive notions such as decoherence-free subspaces and noiseless subsystems, and led to the advent of subsystem codes" [7]. OAQEC therefore already contains passive protection as a special case, and its error-correction theorem characterizes correctable Pauli error sets for codes defined by stabilizer, gauge, and logical operator data [7]. Our ball codes could be embedded in this framework once an explicit encoder is constructed; OAQEC is the natural analytical lens for that construction.

**Ultrametric barrier analysis on Bruhat–Tits trees [8].** This QNFO paper derives, for the same tree geometry, energy barrier heights $B(3)=8$ and $B(4)=16$, logical error bounds $p_L(3)=6.5536\times10^{-4}$ and $p_L(4)=4.295\times10^{-7}$ at a per-vertex error probability $p_{\mathrm{err}}=0.40$, thermal bounds including $p_L(5)\le1.27\times10^{-14}$, and a cooling-budget projection of a per-site gap $\Delta\ge1.727\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ to reach $10^{-12}$ logical error at depth 4 [8]. Its summary table gives the barrier as $B(d)=2^{d}$ for odd $p$ and illustrates with $p=5$, $t=2$: $n_2=37$, $d=5$, $B=2^{5}=32$, and $p_L=0.40^{32}\approx1.5\times10^{-7}$ [8]. These are concrete companion benchmarks for the analytic failure probabilities computed here, obtained by a barrier-counting method rather than the binomial weight-counting used in Section 4.3.

**The prior version of this work [9].** The published version of this paper states the same code parameters $n_1=5$, $n_2=17$, $n_3=53$ for $p=3$, the conditional distance $d=2t+1$, and the passive failure probabilities $9.98\times10^{-6}$ ($t=1$) and $6.73\times10^{-7}$ ($t=2$), and its Discussion already flags that Proposition 1 is conditional on a branch-permutation-symmetric encoder that it does not construct [9]. The present revision keeps everything [9] establishes and corrects its active-baseline arithmetic (Section 4.4).
