# Passive Fault Tolerance via Bruhat–Tits Tree Codes: Ball-Code Logical Error Rates, Energy Barriers, and the Cooling Wall of Active Quantum Error Correction

## Abstract

Large-scale quantum computation is currently gated by active error correction: stabilizer codes such as the surface code require physical-to-logical qubit overheads on the order of $10^{2}$–$10^{4}$, continuous syndrome measurement, and millikelvin refrigeration whose thermodynamic cost grows as the operating temperature falls. This paper examines a proposed alternative in which logical qudits are encoded on balls of the Bruhat–Tits tree $T_{p+1}$, the incidence geometry of the $p$-adic numbers, so that the ultrametric strong triangle inequality confines errors to single branches and correction is passive. The specific gap addressed is quantitative: prior statements of the scheme assert a depolarizing threshold near $75\%$ and a removal of the cooling wall, but neither figure has been derived. We formalize the ball-code model, derive the exact code sizes $n_t = 1 + (p+1)(p^{t}-1)/(p-1)$, and compute, with full arithmetic, passive logical failure probabilities of $9.980 \times 10^{-6}$ per epoch for the flagship $p=3$, $t=1$ block (5 qudits, distance 3) and $6.729 \times 10^{-7}$ for the $t=2$ block (17 qudits, distance 5) at physical error rate $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$, suppression factors of $100.2$ and $1486$ over unencoded qudits. Under the conjectured barrier $E_{\text{barrier}}(d) = 2^{d}$, thermal activation gives $p_L \le e^{-8} = 3.355 \times 10^{-4}$ at depth 3. The Carnot coefficient-of-performance ratio between 4 K and 10 mK operation is $405$. The $75\%$ threshold remains an open conjecture; the encoder existence problem is the binding constraint.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum processors today face a scaling wall that is thermodynamic as much as it is architectural. Fault tolerance in every leading architecture — surface codes, color codes, and other stabilizer schemes — is active: errors are detected by repeatedly measuring syndrome operators and corrected by classical decoding, with physical-to-logical qubit overheads that published estimates place between $10^{2}$ and $10^{4}$ [10] and that the Alpha Pi project summarizes as roughly $1000{:}1$ for surface codes [11]. Syndrome extraction must run continuously, at low latency, and on hardware held near 10–20 mK, because the measurement and control layer itself must be error-free enough not to overwhelm the code [4]. The consequence is that the refrigeration plant, not the qubit count alone, becomes a dominant cost: cooling power scales inversely with the thermodynamic (Carnot) efficiency, which collapses as the operating temperature falls, and the Alpha Pi analysis argues that active correction additionally hits a signal-processing ceiling beyond which no classical filtering removes fundamental quantum back-action [11]. Cloud quantum-service providers feel this directly as higher subscription cost and shorter runnable circuits; hardware teams feel it as dilution-refrigerator capacity, wiring, and heat-load budgets.

A design that suppressed logical errors passively — by geometry rather than by measurement — would change three decisions at once. It would remove the syndrome-measurement layer and its ancilla overhead, which traditional fault-tolerant error correction schemes require in numbers proportional to the maximum stabilizer weight [6]; it would relax the refrigeration requirement from the millikelvin regime toward the 4 K regime reachable by pulse-tube cryocoolers, where the Carnot coefficient of performance is roughly $405$ times larger than at 10 mK (computed in Section 4.6); and it would convert the scalability question from an engineering race against decoherence into a checkable geometric claim. The proposal under examination, due to the ultrametric quantum error correction program [9, 10, 11, 12], asserts exactly this: encoding on the balls of a Bruhat–Tits tree creates an energy barrier $E_{\text{barrier}}(d) = 2^{d}$ against logical error, confines error propagation to single branches by the ultrametric strong triangle inequality, and — in its strongest form — admits depolarizing error thresholds near $75\%$, far above the roughly $1\%$ figure the proposal attributes to surface codes.

The gap this paper closes is quantitative and critical rather than promotional. The $75\%$ threshold and the full removal of the cooling wall are assertions; what can be derived today is narrower and still useful: exact code parameters, exact passive failure probabilities under stated noise assumptions, the barrier scaling and its thermal-activation consequences, and the thermodynamic ratio that quantifies how much of the cooling wall passive operation would remove. We derive these numbers with fully shown arithmetic, we state which claims remain conjectural, and we carry the falsification criteria of the source program [9, 10, 12] into a form a laboratory test could reject.

The contributions are:

- Derivation of the exact ball-code sizes $n_t = 1 + (p+1)(p^{t}-1)/(p-1)$ for $T_{p+1}$, giving $n_1 = 5$, $n_2 = 17$, $n_3 = 53$ for $p=3$ and $n_1 = 4$, $n_2 = 10$, $n_3 = 22$ for $p=2$ (Section 4.1).
- Exact computation of the passive logical failure probability $P_{\mathrm{pass}} = 9.980 \times 10^{-6}$ per protection epoch for the $p=3$, $t=1$ block at $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$, a suppression factor of $100.2$, and $P_{\mathrm{pass}}^{(2)} = 6.729 \times 10^{-7}$ for the $t=2$ block, a suppression factor of $1486$ (Sections 4.2–4.3, results in Section 5).
- Derivation of the thermal-activation bounds $p_L(3) \le e^{-8} = 3.355 \times 10^{-4}$ and $p_L(5) \le e^{-32} = 1.266 \times 10^{-14}$ under the conjectured barrier $E_{\text{barrier}}(d) = 2^{d}$ with $k_{\mathrm{B}}T = 1$ (Section 4.4).
- Computation of the Carnot coefficient-of-performance ratio $405$ between 4 K and 10 mK operation, quantifying the fraction of the cooling wall that passive correction removes under fixed heat load (Section 4.6).
- Verification of the classical majority-vote threshold mechanism at $p = 0.4$: suppression through $0.173670$, $5.551 \times 10^{-3}$, and $2.904 \times 10^{-10}$ over three concatenated levels, confirming that the recursive suppression on which the passive scheme rests is genuine below threshold (Section 4.5).
- A critical statement of the quantization gap: the $75\%$ depolarizing threshold and the quantum confinement of superposed error paths (Hypothesis Q of [9]) remain unproved, with explicit falsification criteria (Sections 6 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The geometric setting of this paper is the Bruhat–Tits tree, and the arithmetic of its subgraphs is well developed in the number-theoretic literature. Work on the missing branches of the Bruhat–Tits tree studies the set $S(\Omega)$ of maximal orders in the local matrix algebra $\mathfrak{A} = \mathbb{M}_{2}(k)$ containing a given order $\Omega$, a set realized as a sub-tree of the Bruhat–Tits tree called the branch of the order, and uses these branches to study the selectivity problem and normalizers of Eichler orders [1]. That work fixes the graph conventions this paper adopts — vertices with a symmetric neighborhood relation, full subgraphs, and vertex valency — and its proof machinery walks ghost paths and stems inside the tree, computing distances between branch structures case by case [1]. The relevance here is foundational: the branch decomposition of [1] is the same tree geometry whose nesting and disjointness properties the passive code exploits, and the valuation-theoretic classification of ramified units and uniformizers [1] underlies the qudit alphabet structure used in Section 3.

Against this geometric proposal stands the entire active fault-tolerance paradigm. Homological product codes provide a method for implementing a universal set of fault-tolerant logical gates by mapping between different encoded representations of a logical state so that complementary classes of transversal gates become available, and the construction preserves sparsity of stabilizer generators, which the authors connect to the quantum LDPC program [3]. The construction is explicitly active: CSS codes are defined from chain complexes with boundary operators $\delta$ satisfying $\delta^{2} = 0$, and the homological product $\partial = \delta_{1} \otimes \mathbb{1} + \mathbb{1} \otimes \delta_{2}$ builds the code space [3]. The passive scheme of this paper removes the measurement layer entirely, so the comparison in Section 6 is between the gate flexibility that homological products buy [3] and the syndrome-free operation that the tree geometry promises.

Recent work has attacked the overhead of active schemes from the protocol side. Low-overhead transversal fault tolerance for universal quantum computation demonstrates that logical operations can be transversal and interleaved with a single syndrome-extraction round instead of $d$ rounds, using partial decoding and mid-circuit commitment, with circuit-level simulations of a $\lvert\overline{S}\rangle$ distillation factory based on the $[[7,1,3]]$ Steane code at fixed circuit noise $p = 0.1\%$ [4]. The same work notes that transversal operations and correlated decoding were recently found highly effective in experiments with reconfigurable neutral-atom arrays [4]. This matters for the present proposal in two ways: it shows the active-overhead baseline is a moving target that any passive scheme must beat numerically, and it identifies neutral-atom tweezer arrays as the natural platform for the minimal experiment proposed in Section 7.

On the ancilla side, the flag technique achieves fault-tolerant error correction on capped color codes with few ancillas by detecting high-weight errors arising from a few faults using a small number of flag ancillas, and the work observes that distinguishability of fault sets depends on the number of flag ancillas, gate ordering, and the choice of measured generators [6]. The same introduction records that the Shor error-correction scheme requires as many ancillas as the maximum weight of the stabilizer generators [6]. The passive scheme's selling point is the limit of this trajectory: zero syndrome measurements and therefore zero ancillas, which Section 4 prices into the heat budget.

The broader fault-tolerance literature supplies both analogies and methodological discipline. The orthogonal fault tolerance model was proposed to manage and reason about multiple fault tolerance mechanisms that co-exist in dynamically adaptive systems, where the interaction of co-evolving mechanisms is the key challenge [2]; a passive geometric layer coexisting with any residual active layer is precisely such a multi-mechanism design, and feature interaction between the layers is a risk Section 6 takes seriously. In cloud systems, Byzantine faults are highlighted as especially dangerous because they often go undetected at the initial stage and can propagate to other virtual machines before detection, keeping mission-critical applications off the cloud [5] — the same propagation-without-detection failure mode that the ultrametric confinement claim is designed to exclude. Decentralized validation for non-malicious arbitrary fault tolerance in Paxos hardened an active-replication framework and validated it by fault injection: 100 message-fault injections and 100 application-fault injections were each detected with zero errors, and the summed coverage of protocol-deviation faults reached $97.6\%$ [7]. That injection methodology — inject faults, count escapes — is the template for the falsification experiment of Section 7. Finally, SignSGD demonstrates fault tolerance to blind and Byzantine adversaries in distributed learning: the aggregation of signed gradients maintains accuracy as the number of blind adversaries increases, with a theoretical bound verified in practice on logistic regression and feed-forward networks [8]. This is the closest classical analogue of the passive mechanism: robustness conferred by an aggregation structure (there, sign majority; here, tree-ball majority) rather than by detection and retransmission.
