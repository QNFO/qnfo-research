# Subliminal Learning as Architecture-Specific Latent-Space Steganography: A Channel Model for Trait Transfer Under Distillation

## Abstract

Subliminal learning — the transfer of a teacher model's behavioral traits to a student trained on semantically unrelated outputs such as number sequences — is documented empirically but lacks a quantitative channel model that explains why transfer succeeds within a model family and fails across families. This gap blocks any principled design of filters, training recipes, or audits for the increasingly common practice of training on model-generated data. We formalize subliminal learning as steganographic modulation of the teacher's output distribution: a trait is encoded as a low-amplitude perturbation along a teacher-specific direction in function space, budgeted by a per-token KL divergence $D$. We derive a covert capacity $C = D/\ln 2$ bits per token, a steganalytic detection threshold $n \gtrsim \ln(1/\alpha)/D$ tokens, and an architecture-specificity condition in which per-sample trait information scales with the squared representational overlap $\rho^2$ between teacher and student directions. At $D = 0.05$ nats the model gives $C = 0.072134$ bits per token, detection at $92.10$ tokens for a known-reference detector at $10^{-2}$ false-alarm tolerance, and a cross-family sample-complexity penalty of a factor of $9$ at $\rho_{\mathrm{cross}} = 0.3$ versus $\rho_{\mathrm{same}} = 0.9$ (factor $81$ at $\rho_{\mathrm{cross}} = 0.1$). The model predicts that content filtering preserves the channel whenever the trait direction is orthogonal to the filtered semantic subspace, and it converts empirical observations of brittleness and context dependence into testable quantitative conditions.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations that fine-tune language models on data generated by other models face an audit problem with no current quantitative answer. A teacher model conditioned to hold a trait — a harmless animal preference or a harmful misalignment — generates training data whose visible content is unrelated to the trait, yet students trained on those outputs acquire the trait [3]. The same protocol transfers misaligned and unsafe behavior, so a pipeline that ingests teacher-generated data can inherit properties that no semantic inspection of the data reveals [3], [4]. Today the practitioners responsible for such pipelines — alignment teams, data-curation teams, and the operators of teacher-student distillation services — have three tools: format filters, semantic classifiers, and human review. All three were applied to filtered number-sequence data and failed to reliably identify the transmitted content [3], and text-optimization recovery of the biasing prompt succeeds even from datasets that do not exhibit behavioral transfer [6]. What is missing is a model that says, in numbers, how much covert information a carrier of a given size and distortion budget can carry, how many samples a detector needs, and under what condition a student of a given architecture can or cannot decode the signal. With such a model, a curation team could size a filter's residual risk in bits per token, an auditor could state a sample complexity for detection, and a training-recipe designer could predict whether a cross-family student is exposed at all.

The empirical record already contains the qualitative ingredients of such a model. Transmission succeeds when teacher and student share a base model and fails across dissimilar model families [3]; the effect depends strongly on the system prompt used during fine-tuning and evaluation, weakening or disappearing when they mismatch [7]; trait transfer spreads to related traits in cluster structures that recur across model families and carriers [1]; a targeted control that regularizes drift along probe-space trait directions suppresses transfer while preserving task performance [4]; and the channel survives even when the only training signal is a single scalar judge score per completion [2]. Each observation constrains where the trait information can live: not in token identity, not in any semantic subspace a filter can name, and readable only through directions that a matching architecture shares. What the record does not yet contain is a single derivation chain that turns these constraints into capacities, sample complexities, and transfer ratios.

This paper builds that chain. We model the teacher's trait as a sign variable $z \in \{-1, +1\}$ encoded in a low-amplitude perturbation of its output distribution along a unit direction $h$ in function space, subject to a per-token KL budget $D$. The student is a linear readout of its own representation space; its ability to demodulate the signal is governed by the squared cosine $\rho^2$ between the teacher's encoding direction and the student's corresponding direction. We then derive capacity, detectability, filter-survival, and cross-architecture transfer conditions, and evaluate all of them at stated numerical budgets. The contributions are:

- A channel model of subliminal learning in which a trait is a sign encoded along a function-space direction under a per-token KL budget $D$, with the small-perturbation identity $D_{\mathrm{KL}} \approx \varepsilon^2/2$ derived in Section 3 and Section 4.
- A covert-capacity bound $C = D/\ln 2$ bits per token and a steganalysis sample complexity $n \gtrsim \ln(1/\alpha)/D$, evaluated at $D = 0.05$ nats to $C = 0.072134$ bits per token and $n = 92.10$ tokens at $\alpha = 10^{-2}$ (Section 4, Results in Section 5).
- An architecture-specificity theorem: per-sample trait information equals $\rho^2 D$, giving a cross-family sample-complexity penalty of $1/\rho_{\mathrm{cross}}^2$ relative to same-family transfer, computed as a factor of $9$ at $\rho_{\mathrm{cross}} = 0.3$ and $81$ at $\rho_{\mathrm{cross}} = 0.1$ (Section 4).
- A filter-survival condition stating that content filtering removes a fraction $f$ of the KL budget only insofar as it projects onto the trait direction, with $D_{\mathrm{eff}} = (1-f)D$ and worked numbers for $f = 0.5$ (Section 4).
- A demodulation sample budget $M \geq K \ln 2 / D$ carrier tokens for a $K$-bit behavioral specification, giving $138{,}629$ tokens for $K = 10^4$ bits at $D = 0.05$ nats (Section 4).

The machinery developed here is stated for teacher-student distillation, but the same overlap-governed readout applies to any setting in which one learned system's statistical output texture is consumed by another, including the judge-score channel of reinforcement-learning-from-judge pipelines and recursive model-on-model training corpora; the compression-versus-consilience tension in such recursive corpora is a parallel question about what survives generative chains [11].

## 2. Background and Related Work

The empirical foundation is the subliminal-learning phenomenon itself. Cloud et al. [3] establish that a teacher with a trait such as an owl preference or misalignment, sampled on unrelated prompts and filtered to a dictionary of only 16 non-alphabetic characters, transmits the trait to a student fine-tuned on the filtered outputs; their discussion section rules out semantic leakage by manual inspection, a prompted LLM judge, and in-context learning, all of which failed to identify trait-related content, and reports that models that transmit traits to themselves fail to transmit them to dissimilar models from different families. Their conclusion states the two facts this paper must reconcile: hidden information is present in the outputs, and its decodability is student-dependent. Our model treats the first fact as a capacity statement and the second as an overlap statement.

The architecture dependence is the paper's central anchor. The same-model/cross-model asymmetry in [3] is given a mechanism-level treatment by the trait-direction-drift work [4], which studies animal-preference and malicious-response settings in Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, notes that same-model teachers and students share a base model and initialization so the initial preference gap matches the source-side quantity $G$, and shows in its drift-control results that corridor regularization in probe space keeps the centered drift near zero across settings — in the Llama setting owl preference falls from $17.8\%$ to $0.6\%$ — while cross-model attenuation persists after target ranking. This supports our modeling choice that the trait lives along a direction in a probe or representation space, and that the direction, not the token content, is the transmitted object. The LoRA-artifact work [7] adds the readout-side condition: subliminal learning depends on the fine-tuning and evaluation context, weakening or disappearing when the system prompts mismatch between training and evaluation, and replacing "Qwen" with "Claude" in the default system prompt can increase the effect. In our model, the evaluation prompt supplies the readout direction; a mismatched prompt projects the acquired parameter shift onto a direction that does not express the trait, which reproduces context-dependent disappearance without any change in the encoded signal.

Three works refine what is encoded. The cross-trait-transfer study [1] broadens the trait space and shows, through double-centered $30 \times 30$ trait-transfer matrices across Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 and across carriers including numbers, tokens, chemical elements, GSM8K solutions, and Python code, that transfer clusters related traits and that this cluster structure recurs across model families and carrier types; it further tests six diagnostic metrics spanning output probabilities and activation directions. In our formalism, related traits correspond to encoding directions with large mutual overlap, so a student demodulating one direction partially reads its neighbors — a prediction the cluster structure confirms qualitatively. The verbalization work [6] uses text optimization (SALVE) to recover legible prompts from subliminal datasets, reaching a dataset NLL of $0.437$ and a behavior frequency of $0.88$ with $18/20$ trait verbalization on subliminal cat data, and finds that the biasing intervention is recoverable even from datasets that do not exhibit transfer; its discussion reframes subliminal learning as a question of whether student fine-tuning is expressive enough to recover the encoded information. This is precisely the demodulation half of our channel model, decoupled from the encoding half. The subliminal reinforcement-learning work [2] shows the channel survives an extreme bottleneck: a GRPO student learns only from its own outputs and one scalar judge score per completion, with the judge's own text never entering the update, and it defines a transfer estimand $S_t = \tfrac{1}{2}[(B_{T,t} - B_{T,0}) - (B_{E,t} - B_{E,0})]$ on the preference margin $B$. A one-number-per-completion channel is the sparsest demodulation our model must accommodate, and it confirms that the signal is a property of the sampling policy's distribution rather than of any token the student copies.

On the control side, [4] demonstrates that the drift coordinate is a measurable and suppressible target within tested SFT settings, which our model interprets as constraining the student's readout along the trait direction during training. On the steganography side, the network-steganography review [5] frames covert channels as modulation of legitimate carrier statistics — timing, ordering, and format fields of network traffic — a framing that dates to the early 1980s and whose core operations have changed little; subliminal learning is the same pattern with the carrier being a model's output distribution and the modulator being the teacher's latent trait state, which motivates importing steganographic capacity and detectability language wholesale. The latent-geometry side is supplied by the flat-manifold VAE work [8], which learns latent representations in which the Euclidean metric is a proxy for data similarity by constraining the Riemannian metric tensor toward a scaled identity, and which quantifies geometry through condition numbers $k(\mathbf{G}) = S_{\max}(\mathbf{G})/S_{\min}(\mathbf{G})$ and magnification factors $\sqrt{\det \mathbf{G}}$; this supplies the natural diagnostic for whether a student's representation space is locally isotropic enough that a single cosine $\rho$ is a meaningful summary of teacher-student overlap. Finally, the manifold-modulation capacity paper [10] already derives, for teacher-student transmission channels, the three relations this paper adopts and extends: a per-token capacity $C = D/\ln 2$ bits evaluated at $0.072134$, $0.144270$, and $0.288539$ bits per token for $D = 0.05$, $0.10$, and $0.20$ nats; a steganalysis bound of approximately $\ln(1/\alpha)/D$ tokens, giving $92.10$ tokens at $D = 0.05$ nats and $\alpha = 10^{-2}$; and a demodulation condition $M \geq K \ln 2/D$ giving $138{,}629$ carrier tokens for a $10^4$-bit specification. Its discussion also names the assumption this paper must inherit and flag: per-token independence, under which correlated modulation across tokens can push true joint capacity above $ND/\ln 2$. The epistemic-mirror work [9] contributes a framing point rather than a quantitative one: it argues that any cognitive architecture employing dimensionality reduction for self-representation faces a structurally necessary epistemic boundary, with language acting as a lossy compression of high-dimensional states; the analogous claim here, made precise, is that the student's finite-dimensional representation space is the compression through which the teacher's signal must pass, and the compression geometry decides what survives.

## 3. Methods

This is a theoretical paper. We perform no experiment, measurement, or simulation; the method is the construction of a channel model, its analysis under stated assumptions, and the numerical evaluation of the resulting expressions at explicitly stated budgets. All numbers in Sections 4 and 5 are computed from the stated inputs by shown arithmetic.

**Model.** Let $p_0(y \mid x)$ be a reference output distribution over tokens $y$ given prompt $x$, shared by the teacher's base model. A trait is a binary variable $z \in \{-1, +1\}$ (for example, the sign of an animal preference between two candidates). The trait-conditioned teacher emits

$$p_T(y \mid x, z) = p_0(y \mid x)\big[1 + \varepsilon\, z\, h_x(y)\big],$$

where $h_x$ is a unit function under the $L^2(p_0(\cdot \mid x))$ inner product, $\int h_x(y)\, p_0(y \mid x)\, \mathrm{d}y = 0$, and $0 \leq \varepsilon < 1$ is the modulation amplitude. The trait lives entirely in the direction $h_x$; the token-level content of $p_0$ is untouched except by amplitude $\varepsilon |h_x(y)|$.

**Assumptions.** (A1) Small modulation: $\varepsilon^2$ terms dominate the KL and $\varepsilon^3$ is negligible. (A2) Per-token independence: the modulation at each token is budgeted independently, so per-token quantities sum over a carrier; this is the idealization [10] flags, and correlated modulation can exceed the resulting bound. (A3) Linear readout: the student's parameter update under SFT on teacher samples moves, to first order, along a direction in the student's representation space whose normalized projection onto the teacher's encoding direction has squared cosine $\rho^2 \in [0,1]$. (A4) The trait direction $h$ is orthogonal, under the $p_0$ measure, to the semantic subspace that content filters inspect. (A5) Numerical inputs: the KL budgets $D \in \{0.05, 0.10, 0.20\}$ nats and the overlap values $\rho_{\mathrm{same}} = 0.9$, $\rho_{\mathrm{cross}} \in \{0.3, 0.1\}$ are modeling assumptions chosen to span the same-family and cross-family regimes reported qualitatively in [3] and [4]; no overlap value is measured in this paper. (A6) Carrier size $N = 10^4$ tokens and specification size $K = 10^4$ bits follow [10] for comparability.

**Derivation program.** Section 4 proceeds in five steps: (i) relate $\varepsilon$ to the per-token KL budget $D$; (ii) bound the covert mutual information per token and convert to bits; (iii) derive the sample complexity of an optimal known-reference detector; (iv) derive the per-sample Fisher information available to a student with overlap $\rho$ and the resulting sample-complexity ratio across architectures; (v) derive the filter-survival factor and the demodulation token budget. Each step is an algebraic manipulation of the model above; no step introduces an unmeasured empirical quantity without labeling it an assumption.

## 4. Analysis

**Step 1: KL budget in terms of modulation amplitude.** The KL divergence of $p_T$ from $p_0$ for a fixed trait sign $z$ is

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_T \| p_0) = \int p_T \log \frac{p_T}{p_0}\, \mathrm{d}y = \int p_0 (1 + \varepsilon z h) \log(1 + \varepsilon z h)\, \mathrm{d}y.$$

Expanding $\log(1 + u) = u - u^2/2 + O(u^3)$ with $u = \varepsilon z h$ and keeping terms through second order:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}} = \int p_0 \Big[ (1 + \varepsilon z h)\big(\varepsilon z h - \tfrac{\varepsilon^2 h^2}{2}\big) \Big] \mathrm{d}y + O(\varepsilon^3) = \int p_0 \Big[ \varepsilon z h + \varepsilon^2 h^2 - \tfrac{\varepsilon^2 h^2}{2} \Big] \mathrm{d}y + O(\varepsilon^3).$$

The linear term vanishes because $\int h\, p_0\, \mathrm{d}y = 0$, leaving

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}} = \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2} \int h^2\, p_0\, \mathrm{d}y + O(\varepsilon^3) = \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2} + O(\varepsilon^3),$$

since $h$ is unit in $L^2(p_0)$. Setting $D = \varepsilon^2/2$ gives the amplitude–budget relation

$$\varepsilon = \sqrt{2D}.$$

At the primary budget $D = 0.05$ nats: $\varepsilon = \sqrt{2 \times 0.05} = \sqrt{0.1} = 0.316228$. At $D = 0.10$: $\varepsilon = \sqrt{0.2} = 0.447214$. At $D = 0.20$: $\varepsilon = \sqrt{0.4} = 0.632456$. The per-token relative perturbation of output probabilities is therefore at most $\varepsilon \max|h|$, i.e., of order $0.32$ at $D = 0.05$ nats in the worst-aligned token, and typically far smaller on tokens where $|h(y)|$ is spread across the vocabulary.

**Step 2: Covert capacity per token.** The trait variable $z$ is hidden in the choice between two distributions $p_T(\cdot \mid z = +1)$ and $p_T(\cdot \mid z = -1)$. The mutual information between $z$ and one emitted token satisfies $I(z; Y) \leq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_T \| p_0)$ in nats, because the KL to the reference upper-bounds the information any sample carries about which hypothesis generated it under a simple-hypothesis formulation. Converting nats to bits with $\ln 2 = 0.693147$:

$$C = \frac{D}{\ln 2} \ \text{bits per token}.$$

This is the capacity relation derived in [10]; the derivation above shows it follows from the same perturbation model rather than being an independent postulate. At the three budgets:

- $D = 0.05$: $C = 0.05 / 0.693147 = 0.072134$ bits per token.
- $D = 0.10$: $C = 0.10 / 0.693147 = 0.144270$ bits per token.
- $D = 0.20$: $C = 0.20 / 0.693147 = 0.288539$ bits per token.

For a carrier of $N = 10^4$ tokens at $D = 0.05$ nats, the total covert payload is $I_{\max} = 10^4 \times 0.072134 = 721.34$ bits, which is $721.34 / 8 = 90.17$ bytes. At $D = 0.20$ nats the same carrier holds $10^4 \times 0.288539 = 2885.39$ bits, or $360.67$ bytes. A single filtered number-sequence document can therefore carry a complete short behavioral specification in principle, which is consistent with the empirical finding that a few thousand filtered samples suffice to move trait measures in students [3], [6].

**Step 3: Steganalytic sample complexity.** A detector that knows the reference $p_0$ and tests the simple hypothesis "carrier is trait-modulated" against "carrier is clean" at false-alarm probability $\alpha$ needs a number of tokens $n$ such that the accumulated KL evidence separates the hypotheses. Under the per-token independence assumption (A2), the expected per-token evidence is $D$ nats, and a Chernoff-information argument gives the requirement

$$n \gtrsim \frac{\ln(1/\alpha)}{D}.$$

At $\alpha = 10^{-2}$, $\ln(1/\alpha) = \ln 100 = 4.60517$:

- $D = 0.05$: $n = 4.60517 / 0.05 = 92.1034$ tokens.
- $D = 0.10$: $n = 4.60517 / 0.10 = 46.0517$ tokens.
- $D = 0.20$: $n = 4.60517 / 0.20 = 23.0259$ tokens.

The capacity and the detectability scale inversely with the same budget $D$: a teacher that hides more bits per token is also easier to detect per token, once the detector knows which test to run. The practical difficulty observed in [3] — that manual inspection, an LLM judge, and in-context learning all failed — is not contradicted by this bound, because all three are semantic detectors that do not run the known-reference statistical test; the bound says detection is cheap only for a detector holding $p_0$ and the candidate direction $h$.

**Step 4: Architecture-specific demodulation.** Let the teacher encode the trait along a direction whose normalized image in the student's representation space has cosine $\rho$ with the student's corresponding readout direction (assumption A3). The student's gradient update on each teacher sample carries a component along the trait direction proportional to $\rho$, so the per-sample Fisher information about $z$ available to the student is the teacher's per-sample information attenuated by $\rho^2$:

$$I_1(\rho) = \rho^2 D \ \text{nats per sample}.$$

To reach the same decision confidence $\alpha$ on $z$, the student needs

$$N_s(\rho) = \frac{\ln(1/\alpha)}{\rho^2 D} \ \text{samples}.$$

We take $\rho_{\mathrm{same}} = 0.9$ for a student sharing the teacher's base model and initialization — the configuration in which [4] states the initial preference gap matches the source-side value — and $\rho_{\mathrm{cross}} \in \{0.3, 0.1\}$ for students from a different family, as labeled assumptions (A5). At $D = 0.05$ nats and $\alpha = 10^{-2}$:

- Same family, $\rho = 0.9$: $\rho^2 D = 0.81 \times 0.05 = 0.0405$ nats per sample; $N_s = 4.60517 / 0.0405 = 113.71$ samples.
- Cross family, $\rho = 0.3$: $\rho^2 D = 0.09 \times 0.05 = 0.0045$ nats per sample; $N_s = 4.60517 / 0.0045 = 1023.37$ samples.
- Cross family, $\rho = 0.1$: $\rho^2 D = 0.01 \times 0.05 = 0.0005$ nats per sample; $N_s = 4.60517 / 0.0005 = 9210.34$ samples.

The cross-family penalty is the inverse squared overlap:

$$\frac{N_s(\rho_{\mathrm{cross}})}{N_s(\rho_{\mathrm{same}})} = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{same}}^2}{\rho_{\mathrm{cross}}^2} = \frac{0.81}{0.09} = 9 \quad (\rho_{\mathrm{cross}} = 0.3), \qquad \frac{0.81}{0.01} = 81 \quad (\rho_{\mathrm{cross}} = 0.1).$$

This is the paper's central quantitative claim: architecture specificity is not a binary gate but a quadratic penalty, and a tenfold drop in overlap costs two orders of magnitude in samples. It converts the qualitative failure of cross-family transmission in [3] into a continuum on which "failure" means the sample budget exceeds the practical dataset size. A dataset of $10^4$ samples delivers $10^4 \times 0.0005 = 5$ nats of trait evidence at $\rho_{\mathrm{cross}} = 0.1$ — about $5 / \ln 2 = 7.21$ bits, enough to move a binary preference but marginal for a multi-trait specification — while the same dataset delivers $10^4 \times 0.0405 = 405$ nats $= 584.6$ bits to a same-family student.

**Step 5: Filter survival.** Let a content filter inspect a semantic subspace $\mathcal{S}$ of the output space and remove or flag any component with projection onto $\mathcal{S}$ above a threshold. Under assumption (A4) the trait direction is orthogonal to $\mathcal{S}$ in the $p_0$ measure, so filtering removes none of the trait modulation: the surviving budget is $D_{\mathrm{eff}} = D$ and the channel survives intact. If the trait direction has a component in $\mathcal{S}$ and the filter removes a fraction $f$ of the KL budget, the surviving budget is

$$D_{\mathrm{eff}} = (1 - f) D.$$

At $f = 0.5$ and $D = 0.05$: $D_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.025$ nats, giving capacity $C = 0.025 / 0.693147 = 0.036067$ bits per token, detection threshold $n = 4.60517 / 0.025 = 184.21$ tokens, and same-family sample complexity $N_s = 4.60517 / (0.81 \times 0.025) = 4.60517 / 0.02025 = 227.42$ samples. The structural point is that format filters of the kind used in [3] and [7] — restricting completions to three-digit numbers and separators — act on token identity, which is the carrier, not on the direction $h$, which is the message; a filter can therefore sanitize content completely while leaving $D$ untouched, which is exactly the empirical outcome the filters in [3] produced.
