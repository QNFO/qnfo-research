# Ultrametric Quantum Error Correction on Bruhat–Tits Trees: Barrier Conjecture, Classical Voting Evidence, and the Quantization Question

## Abstract

Passive fault tolerance — protection of logical qubits by encoding geometry alone, without repeated syndrome measurement — would remove the dominant overhead in scalable quantum computing if it could be made to work for quantum information rather than only for classical bits. We study a conjectured scheme in which logical qubits are encoded on a Bruhat–Tits tree, the infinite homogeneous tree of a non-Archimedean local field whose metric satisfies the strong triangle inequality. The conjecture states that the energy barrier against a logical error grows as $E_{\text{barrier}}(d)=2^{d}$ in tree depth $d$; the supporting evidence is a classical simulation report of zero logical errors at a physical error rate $p=0.4$ for depth $d\geq3$ under majority-vote decoding. Two claims of the prior version of this analysis [8] require correction, and we correct them here. First, the barrier $E_{\text{barrier}}(d)=2^{d}$ is not derived from the ultrametric cut structure: the minimal-cut count $2^{d}$ holds for any rooted binary tree and uses no property of the strong triangle inequality, so we treat the barrier as a posited model, consistent with the values $B(3)=8$ and $B(4)=16$ reported in [9], [10], and [11] but not a consequence of ultrametricity. Second, the iterated majority-vote cascade at $p=0.4$ — which we compute explicitly as $0.1736704\to0.0055485\to2.905\times10^{-10}$ over three levels — is a generic property of recursive majority voting on any branching structure and cannot confirm a threshold mechanism specific to the Bruhat–Tits geometry. We further show that the zero-error report bounds the logical error probability only at $p_{L}\leq2.9957\times10^{-5}$ even for $N=10^{5}$ trials, derive a thermal-activation projection $p_{L}\leq e^{-2^{d}}$ under the stated barrier model, and state the quantization gap — whether the strong triangle inequality confines quantum errors under superposition, entanglement, and coherent noise — as an explicit falsifiable hypothesis with named tests. If the conjecture holds, syndrome-extraction hardware could be replaced by a static energy landscape; nothing in the present evidence yet supports that step.

## 1. Introduction

Scalable quantum computing is presently blocked by error-correction overhead. Active stabilizer codes such as the surface code require repeated syndrome extraction, low-latency real-time decoding, and large ancilla qubit counts, and the engineering cost of these cycles limits the size of computation that can be run on near-term hardware. A cloud-based quantum service provider faces this cost directly: every logical qubit demands a physical-to-logical overhead on the order of $10^{2}$–$10^{4}$ (an assumption stated and labeled as such in [10], which uses an internal estimate of $10^{3}:1$ for the surface code), continuous measurement infrastructure, and power for decoding. A design that suppressed logical errors passively — by the geometry of the encoding itself, with no measurement cycle — would change the architecture: syndrome-extraction hardware and decoders could be replaced by a static Hamiltonian whose energy landscape blocks error propagation, and the physical-to-logical overhead could fall accordingly.

This paper investigates one specific passive proposal: encode logical information on a Bruhat–Tits tree. A Bruhat–Tits tree is the infinite $(q+1)$-regular tree associated with a local field $k$ complete with respect to a non-Archimedean absolute value; its path metric is ultrametric, satisfying the strong triangle inequality $d_{\mathrm{BT}}(x,z)\leq\max\{d_{\mathrm{BT}}(x,y),\,d_{\mathrm{BT}}(y,z)\}$, so every triangle is isosceles with a unique long side. The tree is not an arbitrary graph: for a local field $k$ and the matrix algebra $\mathbb{M}_{2}(k)$, the set of maximal orders containing a given suborder is a subtree of the Bruhat–Tits tree called the branch of the order, and branches have been applied to the global selectivity problem and to computing local embeddings [3]. The conjecture under study asserts (i) that the energy barrier against a logical error on a tree of depth $d$ scales as $E_{\text{barrier}}(d)=2^{d}$, and (ii) that this scaling yields passive fault tolerance with thresholds exceeding those of surface codes, without active syndrome measurement.

Two corrections relative to the prior version of this analysis [8] frame the whole paper, and both concern what the existing evidence actually establishes.

**Correction 1: the barrier is a conjecture, not a derivation.** The prior version stated that the barrier $E_{\text{barrier}}(d)=2^{d}\,\epsilon$ was "derived from the ultrametric cut structure." That statement does not hold. The argument counts sites on a level of a rooted binary tree: a minimal separating set at depth $d$ has size $2^{d}$ because the tree branches in two at every node. This count is a property of any rooted binary tree — a classical repetition structure with no non-Archimedean content — and it invokes the strong triangle inequality nowhere. The follow-up literature is consistent with this reading: [9] states that its exponential-cut claim is a *model* of the strong triangle inequality ("we model this property as implying that any path connecting two distant vertices must cross a cut whose size grows exponentially with the hierarchical level"), not a theorem derived from it, and [10] reports that its distance result is conditional on an encoder that is not constructed. We therefore treat $E_{\text{barrier}}(d)=2^{d}$ throughout as a posited barrier model, compatible with the values $B(3)=8$, $B(4)=16$ reported in [9] and [11] and $E_{\text{barrier}}(3)=8$ in [10], but not derived from ultrametric geometry.

**Correction 2: the voting cascade is generic, not ultrametric.** The prior version concluded from its iterated-vote computation that the cascade "confirms a genuine recursive threshold mechanism of the tree geometry." That conclusion also does not hold. The computed cascade is a property of the majority-vote function $f$ iterated on a branching structure: $f(p)<p$ for every $p<1/2$ on a binary tree of any provenance, and the fixed-point behavior follows from the binomial arithmetic alone. Nothing in the computation uses the Bruhat–Tits tree, its $(q+1)$-regularity, or the strong triangle inequality; an ordinary binary repetition hierarchy would produce the same numbers. The cascade therefore confirms that recursive majority voting suppresses errors at $p=0.4$ — a classical, decoder-level fact — and is silent about whether ultrametric geometry contributes anything.

With these corrections in place, the supporting evidence must be stated plainly. The claim that classical simulations using majority-vote decoding on classical bits found zero logical errors at a $40\%$ physical error rate for $d\geq3$ is an unverified report from the proposal's notebook notes; no cited work documents the simulation, its trial count, or its decoder. We treat it as a working assumption and analyze what it would imply if true. The central open question is whether the strong triangle inequality confines quantum errors — superpositions of error paths, entangled error states, and coherent noise — or whether the classical advantage is a decoding artifact that evaporates under full quantum channels. The survey literature states that quantum channels behave differently from classical channels, creating difficulties in developing robust quantum codes [4], which is precisely the epistemic gap between the classical evidence and the quantum claim.

If the conjecture holds, the decision that changes is architectural: hardware designers could allocate resources to engineering a static energy landscape rather than to measurement cycles and decoders. If it fails — specifically, if coherent error paths interfere across tree branches in a way that reconverges paths the ultrametric separates classically — the proposal is dead, and the failure mode itself would be a sharpened statement of why passive protection requires active measurement.

Our contributions are:

1. A corrected status for the barrier conjecture $E_{\text{barrier}}(d)=2^{d}\,\epsilon$: presented as a posited model with an explicit demonstration that the cut-counting argument does not use ultrametricity (Section 3).
2. An explicit arithmetic analysis of the classical majority-vote cascade at $p=0.4$, giving $0.1736704\to0.0055485\to2.905\times10^{-10}$ over three levels, together with the corrected interpretation that this cascade is generic to recursive majority voting and not evidence for a Bruhat–Tits-specific mechanism (Section 4).
3. A statistical reinterpretation of the reported "zero logical errors" as a confidence bound $p_{L}\leq\ln20/N$, not a proof of a vanishing error rate (Section 4).
4. A thermal-activation projection $p_{L}\leq e^{-2^{d}}$ under the stated barrier model, with the quantization gap stated as explicit Hypothesis Q rather than a result (Section 4).
5. A specification of the quantum Monte Carlo and tensor-network simulations, and of the theorem that would need to be proved, that would resolve the open question, together with the engineering pathway and the smallest next experiment (Sections 5 and 7).

Throughout we separate what is posited, what is computed from stated inputs, what is reported by cited works, and what is conjecture. The corrected framing also matters beyond this proposal: any program that claims a geometric origin for an error barrier must show that the barrier calculation uses the geometry in question, and the same discipline applies to classical coding hierarchies and to adiabatic gap arguments alike.

## 2. Background and Related Work

**Continuous-time QEC.** Reference [1] investigates continuous-time quantum error correction (CTQEC), in which both noise and error-correcting operations are processes continuous in time, based on continuous weak measurements and feedback, analyzed from the subsystem principle — the statement that protected information is identified with a subsystem $\mathcal{H}^{A}$ of the system Hilbert space such that a CPTP recovery map $\mathcal{R}$ restores the reduced state after the noise map $\mathcal{E}$. Its outlook notes that constructing optimal CTQEC protocols remains open and that the equilibrium code-space fidelity could be useful for assessing performance under non-Markovian decoherence, where stochastic evolutions are generally too complicated for analytical treatment. CTQEC is the nearest active foil to our proposal: it removes the discrete measurement circuit but retains the feedback loop, whereas a genuinely passive scheme would rely on the energy landscape of the encoding geometry alone.

**Entanglement-assisted codes.** Reference [2] gives a self-contained introduction to entanglement-assisted quantum error-correcting codes (EAQECCs), in which the encoder may append not only ancillas but also ebits — pre-shared entanglement between sender and receiver — before the encoding unitary. Its conclusions state that this entanglement can increase either the communication rate or the number of correctable errors, and that the framework extends the stabilizer formalism while allowing quantum codes to be built from classical linear codes without the dual-containing constraint. The relevance here is structural: if ultrametric confinement fails for independent quantum errors, pre-shared entanglement across distant tree branches is one candidate mechanism for restoring correction capacity, and the EAQECC formalism of [2] is the language in which such a modification would be expressed.

**Bruhat–Tits trees in arithmetic.** Reference [3] develops, for a local field $k$ and the matrix algebra $\mathfrak{A}=\mathbb{M}_{2}(k)$, a theory for computing the set $S(\Omega)$ of maximal orders containing a given order $\Omega$; this set is a subtree of the Bruhat–Tits tree called the branch of the order, and the study of branches plays a role in the selectivity problem and in determining quotient graphs for arithmetically important subgroups. Its graph conventions define a graph through a vertex set and a symmetric neighborhood relation, with the valency of a vertex the number of its neighbors. This is the source of the geometric object we encode on: the tree is the incidence geometry of arithmetic over a non-Archimedean field. We stress, per Correction 1, that nothing in the arithmetic theory of the tree supplies an error barrier; the tree supplies a geometry, and the barrier is an additional physical posit about dynamics on that geometry.

**From classical to quantum codes.** Reference [4] surveys error-correcting codes from the classical to the quantum setting, citing Preskill's claim that the 21st century would be the golden age of quantum error correction, and states that quantum channels behave differently from classical channels, creating difficulties in developing robust quantum codes, while classical error control methods are well developed. Its classical section introduces the binary symmetric channel with parameter $p$, which flips each transmitted bit with probability $p$ independent of all other events, as the model sufficient to illustrate the basic ideas of error correction; its conclusions note that both quantum and classical schemes add ancillary bits and measure a syndrome, with similar conditions for error detectability and correctability. This is exactly the epistemic gap we interrogate: the $p=0.4$ evidence is a classical-channel result on a binary symmetric channel of the kind [4] uses to illustrate classical correction, and the survey's warning is the generic form of the quantization question we pose as Hypothesis Q.

**Pauli structure of quantum noise.** Reference [5] introduces the main ideas of QEC — encoding, syndrome extraction, error operators, and code construction — and shows that general noise and relaxation of two-state systems can always be understood as a combination of Pauli operators acting on the system, with each code correcting a subset of these errors. Its background records that Shor proved 9 qubits protect a single qubit against general errors and Steane achieved the same with 7, and its three-bit-code section analyzes the simplest case under independent noise that applies $\sigma_{x}$ with probability $p$. This Pauli decomposition is the technical hinge of our analysis: any local quantum channel admits an operator-sum expansion in Pauli terms, so the quantum question reduces to whether ultrametric confinement holds for each Pauli component and whether the coherent superposition of error paths — which has no classical analogue — preserves or destroys the confinement.

**Adiabatic error suppression.** Reference [6] treats error suppression and correction in adiabatic quantum computation, modeling a many-body system undergoing adiabatic evolution coupled to an environment through single-qubit Pauli error operators $E_{j}\otimes B_{j}$, and deriving a dynamical master equation for the encoded dynamics. Its discussion states that the model unifies previously proposed techniques — error suppression, energy gap protection, and dynamical decoupling — under one dynamical framework, allows calculation of rates of leakage from the codespace, and extends to error correction by local cooling. The adiabatic setting is the closest existing analogue to passive protection: there, too, an energy gap rather than a measurement cycle is the protective resource. Our posited barrier $E_{\text{barrier}}(d)=2^{d}\,\epsilon$ is structurally an adiabatic-style gap statement transplanted to an ultrametric geometry, and the tension between gap protection and thermal excitation analyzed in [6] is exactly the failure mode we bound in Section 4 — with the caveat, per Correction 1, that no Hamiltonian with this gap has been constructed on the tree.

**Non-standard Hilbert spaces and thresholds.** Reference [7] proposes quaternion-based strategies for QEC by extending quantum mechanics into quaternionic Hilbert spaces, defining quaternionic analogues of Pauli operators and gates that ensure inner-product preservation, and building a quaternionic $[[5,1,3]]$ code. Its text reports a threshold of approximately $p_{\text{th}}\approx0.015$ for the five-qubit quaternionic code, situated between prior reported values, and cites a logical error suppression factor of $\lambda=2.14\pm0.02$ from increasing code distance on a superconducting processor, with an upper bound of $0.189$ for surface codes under correlated noise. These numbers calibrate the competitive landscape: any ultrametric threshold claim must be compared against thresholds in this range, and the suppression factor $\lambda\approx2.14$ per distance increment is the empirical benchmark that a passive scheme claiming thresholds "far exceeding surface codes" would need to beat.

**The prior version of this analysis.** Reference [8] is the published version 1.0.1 of this paper, whose abstract and conclusion stated that the barrier conjecture was derived from the ultrametric cut structure and whose iterated-vote discussion tied the suppression to the Bruhat–Tits structure. Both statements are corrected in the present revision, on the grounds set out in Section 1; the quantitative computations of [8] that we reproduce here are unchanged by the correction.
