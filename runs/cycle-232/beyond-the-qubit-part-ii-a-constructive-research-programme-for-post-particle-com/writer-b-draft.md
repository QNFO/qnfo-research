# Beyond the Qubit, Part II: A Constructive Research Programme for Post-Particle Computation, with Quantified Instrument Budgets, a Fano-Scale Admissibility Gate, and Embodied-Verification Pathways

## Abstract

Research programmes that frame computation as a circuit of qubits face a growing epistemic critique: the companion analysis argues that the qubit-gate-circuit model projects a particle ontology onto systems better described relationally and field-theoretically, and reports that $35 billion and two decades of effort have produced zero commercially viable quantum computers under that paradigm [9]. The specific gap is that critiques of the qubit model stop at diagnosis: they name no measurement standard to inherit, no error budget a successor must satisfy, and no embodied test protocol by which a successor formalism could be falsified. This paper supplies the constructive half of the answer. We strip the computational description to its minimal relational content and show that for $n = 50$ constituent units a pairwise relational parametrization carries $1225$ parameters against $2^{50} \approx 1.1259 \times 10^{15}$ Hilbert-space amplitudes, a compression factor of $9.19 \times 10^{11}$. We convert the NEXT-100 design target of $0.5$–$1\%$ FWHM energy resolution at the double-beta-decay energy of $^{136}\mathrm{Xe}$ [5] into a one-sigma admissibility band $\sigma_E/Q_{\beta\beta} \in [2.1233 \times 10^{-3},\ 4.2466 \times 10^{-3}]$, derive a root-sum-square error-composition rule (five independent stages at $10^{-2}$ each compose to $2.2361 \times 10^{-2}$ statistically versus $5 \times 10^{-2}$ worst-case), and derive a tracking-drift bound of $6.0 \times 10^{-2}$ m per minute under stated illustrative assumptions. We then map immersive-interface capabilities [1], [2], [3], [4], [6], [7], [8] onto a verification protocol in which a human operator is the readout instrument, and size a falsification pilot at $63$ participants per group for $80\%$ power against a medium effect. The work is analytical and unreviewed (TRL 1–2); its practical effect is to convert a philosophical dispute into a numerical gate that any laboratory can apply.

## 1. Introduction

The people who face the problem this paper bears on are working physicists, instrument builders, immersive-interface laboratory operators, and research programme funders who must decide where to spend the next decade of scarce experimental effort. Quantum-computing roadmaps assume the qubit-gate-circuit model is the correct target; the companion analysis argues instead that this model is an epistemic failure — a projection of particle ontology onto relational, field-theoretic reality — and asks what comes next [9]. If that diagnosis is right, the cost of continuing on the qubit path is not merely engineering effort on a contested abstraction but the absence of any prepared alternative: no accepted measurement standard to inherit, no error budget a successor must meet, and no test protocol that could falsify a candidate formalism. Today a researcher who accepts the diagnosis has nowhere to go.

The so-what is concrete. A funder holding two competing post-qubit proposals has no shared yardstick and must judge them by narrative. A laboratory that wants to test a relational or field-theoretic formalism has no specification of how precise its apparatus must be. A sceptic who wants to kill the whole post-particle programme has no single number to attack. If the results of this paper hold, the go/no-go decision on candidate formalisms becomes a numerical gate; an engineer can build the measurement instrument on hardware that already ships; and a theorist can state, in advance and in public, what observation would falsify the programme — the discipline that the ontological-closure criterion demands when it requires that a physical quantity be approximated by a finite protocol yielding measurement-distinguishable predictions [12].

The strategy is deliberately conservative. We do not propose a new physics theory. We take the minimal constructive stance announced in the companion paper — strip quantum mechanics to its minimal ontological commitments and rebuild [9] — and we ask what engineering scaffolding any such rebuild must satisfy to count as science rather than as manifesto. The scaffolding has three parts: (i) a precision standard inherited from the NEXT programme, whose Phase I design anticipates an energy resolution nearing the Fano factor in xenon, $0.5$–$1\%$ FWHM at $Q_{\beta\beta,{}^{136}\mathrm{Xe}}$, with a time-projection-chamber design that tracks events and identifies their double-end-blob topology [5]; (ii) an error-composition rule, derived in Section 4, that converts per-stage errors into an end-to-end pass/fail figure; and (iii) a human-in-the-loop verification layer grounded in the immersive-interface literature, in which skeleton-fitted self-avatars [1], instant motion tracking [6], calibrated depth perception [8], real-time physiological monitoring [7], projection-mapped room re-specification [3], shared multi-user analytic spaces [2], and spatially identical remote views [4] together make a human operator into a calibrated, auditable measurement channel.

Two objections must be met at the outset. First, that immersive interfaces are an application domain, not a computational substrate; our reply is that the programme's claim is epistemic — that relational observables are the correct primitive level of description — and an epistemic claim requires an instrument that measures relational observables, which is what these interfaces are. Second, that the programme is unfalsifiable philosophy; our reply is the falsification conditions stated explicitly in Sections 5 and 6 and the pilot design sized by the power calculation of Section 4.

The contributions of this paper are:

- A minimal relational parametrization of an $n$-unit computational description, with an explicit count showing $n(n-1)/2$ pairwise parameters against $2^n$ Hilbert-space amplitudes, yielding a compression factor of $9.19 \times 10^{11}$ at $n = 50$, with a memory projection of $18.014$ PB versus $1.96 \times 10^4$ bytes under a stated 16-byte-per-amplitude assumption (derived in Section 4.1, Result R1).
- A derived one-sigma equivalent of the NEXT Fano-scale resolution standard, $\sigma_E/Q_{\beta\beta} \in [2.1233 \times 10^{-3},\ 4.2466 \times 10^{-3}]$, computed from the stated $0.5$–$1\%$ FWHM figure by the Gaussian FWHM-to-sigma conversion (Section 4.2, Result R2).
- A derived error-composition rule with a worked example: for $n$ independent stages each at relative error $\varepsilon$, the worst-case stack is $n\varepsilon$ and the statistical stack is $\sqrt{n}\,\varepsilon$; for $n = 5$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-2}$, these are $5 \times 10^{-2}$ and $2.2361 \times 10^{-2}$ respectively (Section 4.3, Result R3).
- A derived drift-growth bound for anchored tracking in an embodied verification rig: random-walk accumulation of per-frame anchor error gives $\delta_{\mathrm{drift}} = \varepsilon_f \sqrt{N} = 6.0 \times 10^{-2}$ m over a one-minute session under stated illustrative assumptions, against $3.6$ m under a constant per-frame bias (Section 4.4, Result R4).
- Derived latency, persistence, and tracking budgets for the embodied instrument: a single $90$ Hz frame consumes $55.6\%$ of an assumed $20$ ms motion-to-photon budget; one-frame persistence blurs a $360\ \mathrm{deg/s}$ head motion by $4.0^\circ$; inter-sample angular travel is $6.0^\circ$ at $60$ Hz versus $0.36^\circ$ at $1$ kHz, a factor of $16.7$ (Section 4.5, Results R5–R7).
- A sized falsification experiment: $63$ participants per group for $80\%$ power against a medium effect at $\alpha = 0.05$ two-sided (Section 4.6, Result R8).
- A mapped verification protocol and engineering pathway, in which each embodied capability is assigned a role in the rig, with a TRL-honest readiness statement and the smallest next test (Sections 3 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

This section reviews the twelve works of the bibliography in its exact numbering. Eight are research papers with technical content; four are programme documents. Each is discussed for the specific point its text establishes and the role that point plays in the argument.

**Precision measurement as the inherited standard.** The NEXT experiment paper describes Phase I of the NEXT-100 neutrinoless double-beta-decay experiment (NEW), scheduled for data taking in 2015 at the Laboratorio Subterráneo de Canfranc, anticipating an energy resolution nearing the Fano factor in xenon of $0.5$–$1\%$ FWHM at $Q_{\beta\beta,{}^{136}\mathrm{Xe}}$, with a TPC design that allows tracking and identification of the double end-blob feature of the decay signature [5]. Two features of this text matter for our argument. First, the resolution figure is a design *anticipation*, not a measured result; we treat it throughout as a target standard, not as achieved performance. Second, the paper shows what a mature experimental paradigm looks like from the outside: a quoted resolution plus a topology identifier that turns an energy measurement into a topology-verified one — its introduction singles out barium tagging and double end-blob identification as "highly unambiguous, 'smoking guns'" for the decay. The paper also reports a concrete achieved datum that anchors the anticipation to demonstrated hardware: the NEXT-DBDM TPC built at Lawrence Berkeley National Lab achieved an energy resolution of $1\%$ FWHM for $0.662$ MeV gammas in a 10 bar, $\sim 1$ kg chamber, described as less than a factor of two shy of the intrinsic Fano limit [5]. Section 4 converts the quoted FWHM figure into the one-sigma scale we adopt as our admissibility gate.

**Embodiment and avatar calibration.** The fitted-avatars paper addresses automatic skeleton adjustment for self-avatars in virtual reality, motivated by the role self-avatars play in enhancing presence and providing embodiment, and in collaborative virtual reality in improving communication through gestures; it notes that whether one uses complex motion capture or a few trackers with inverse kinematics, a good skeleton fit is essential [1]. We use this work for its architectural claim — that embodiment quality depends on a calibration step that can be automated — which is exactly the role we assign to avatar fitting in the verification rig of Section 3: the operator's body model must be calibrated before the operator's motions can be read as measurements.

**Multi-user analytics as a model of shared instrument state.** The interactive multi-user 3D visual analytics project developed a prototype AR data-analytics application with an interactive 3D interface, hand-gesture-based controls, and multi-user support for a shared experience, built to explore the advantages and disadvantages of AR for visual data analytics [2]. Its full text adds two specifics our protocol inherits. Its first prototype rendered a 3D bar chart on a physical table so that multiple users could stand around the visualization and independently view it from their respective viewing directions, "just as if there was a physical model on the table between them" [2]; and its conclusion reports that the resulting multi-user HoloLens application was successful enough to be used in a commercial environment for over a year [2]. A shared, gesture-controlled volumetric interface with per-user viewpoints is a working precedent for several operators inspecting the same computational state simultaneously — the collaboration mode our verification protocol requires when a formalism's predictions must be checked by more than one observer.

**Redirected locomotion as relational perturbation.** The Reality Distortion Room (RDR) is a proof-of-concept system using projection mapping and unencumbered interaction with the Microsoft RoomAlive system to study users' locomotive responses to visual effects that seemingly transform the physical room; it presents five effects that augment the appearance of a physical room to subtly encourage user motion [3]. Unlike the other immersive-interface works, its full text reports a completed experiment: 20 participants aged 23 to 32, seven trials plus a 10-minute interview in a room of approximately $4.5 \times 5.5 \times 2.5$ m, with hypotheses that distortion treatments can induce directional motion and motion toward or away from the room centre [3]. Its results section reports that among the three directional distortion treatments the Shift Distortion had the largest distance manipulated before and after the stimulus segments, while the Warp treatment showed the biggest positive axis movement, and its conclusion states that the user study demonstrated the distortion treatments were effective in influencing users' natural locomotion in predictable ways [3]. This is the programme's clearest precedent for a relational perturbation experiment: a physical room held fixed while its perceived geometry is shifted, with measured locomotor response. We also use its count of five effect families as the stage count $n = 5$ in the worked error-budget example of Section 4.3 — an explicitly labelled modelling assumption of this paper, not a finding of [3].

**Remote guidance as the expert-operator channel.** The mixed-reality remote-assistance system is a volumetric communication system for procedural tasks in which a remote expert visually guides a local operator; the two parties share a view that is spatially identical — the real object for the local operator and a mixed-reality "hologram" for the remote expert — with the system continuously scanning the object of interest, tracking the position of the hands and tools used by the local operator, and presenting this information as a holographic visualization [4]. Its evaluation section reports a concrete latency datum: the initial implementation suffered from over 3 seconds latency, resolved by introducing compression and switching to WebRTC for network communication [4]. This is the communication topology our rig inherits, and the latency datum is a caution it imports: a shared-view verification channel must be engineered to interactive latencies, not merely demonstrated at demonstration-grade responsiveness. In our rig, a formalism's predicted state is rendered as the hologram layer while the apparatus's actual state is the local layer, and disagreement between expert view and operator view is the observable that constitutes a test.

**Tracking as the anchoring primitive.** The instant motion tracking paper identifies proper anchoring of virtual content to the real world — tracking — as a key challenge in enabling AR features, and presents a system built from a motion analysis module, a region tracking module, and either a planar target tracking module or a pose estimation module for calibration-free 6DoF tracking [6]. Its full text supplies quantitative and architectural specifics: the system is calibration-free and robust to degenerate cases (slow-moving cameras, pure rotations, planar scenes) that break standard SLAM pipelines, and it has been deployed on hundreds of millions of mobile devices, driving major AR self-expression applications at an average of 27.5 FPS across 415+ distinct devices [6]. Two points carry into our rig. First, anchoring error is the fundamental per-frame quantity in any AR system — Section 4.4 builds a drift model on exactly this quantity, with the per-frame error value stated as an assumption of this paper, not of [6]. Second, the reported 27.5 FPS average frame rate is a measured operating point for consumer-class tracking, which sits below the kilohertz tracker class our inter-sample analysis of Section 4.5 will require for precision reading.

**Real-time physiological monitoring as the operator readout.** The simulator-sickness paper states that simulator sickness remains a significant problem for wider VR adoption, that the standard Simulator Sickness Questionnaire is a subjective measurement inadequate for real-time applications such as VR games, and that the work therefore targets real-time detection of simulator sickness from players' psychophysiological data during gameplay [7]. Its role in our programme is methodological: it establishes that operator state can be read continuously from physiological signals rather than by post-hoc questionnaires, which is what makes a human operator usable as an in-loop instrument rather than as an end-of-session report. A measurement channel that only reports after the session cannot certify that the session's readings were taken in a valid operator state.

**Depth perception as the calibration target.** The depth-perception study investigates how object shape, fidelity, color, and luminance affect depth perception in handheld mobile AR, noting that many AR applications require depth matching of real and virtual objects and that color and luminance are depth cues traditionally studied in two-dimensional objects, with little prior research on these factors in the mobile AR setting described [8]. We use it as the citation for the claim that depth matching between real and virtual objects is an active, factor-decomposable calibration problem — the same problem our rig faces when a rendered hologram layer must be co-registered with the physical apparatus. The factor decomposition (shape, fidelity, color, luminance) gives the rig's calibration procedure its parameter list.

**The companion critique.** The companion paper "Beyond the Qubit: Constructive Paradigms for Post-Particle Computation" is described as the companion to "The Qubit Delusion" (v1.1), posing the question of what comes next if the qubit-gate-circuit model is an epistemic failure — a projection of particle ontology onto relational, field-theoretic reality — and announcing the stripping of quantum mechanics to its minimal ontological commitments [9]. Its full text makes three specific moves this paper builds on. It quantifies the stakes of the incumbent paradigm: "if $35 billion and two decades of global effort have produced zero commercially viable quantum computers under the incumbent paradigm, are there alternative architectures that might actually deliver commercially useful computation?" [9]. It performs the ontological stripping itself, asking what the quantum formalism commits us to once classical projections — particles, waves, measurement as revelation, observers as special — are removed [9]. And its conclusion states the thesis this paper operationalizes: "the substrate matters, and the choice of computational scaffold is never ontologically neutral. The qubit-gate-circuit scaffold projects particle ontology onto a relational reality" [9]. This paper takes that conclusion as its premise and asks what any successor must satisfy to be testable.

**Determinism and the error model.** The superdeterministic quantum machine paper posits that all events are fundamentally predetermined, including the choices made by conscious observers; that true randomness does not exist in this model; and that vacuum fluctuations "are never truly random" but result from hidden variables established at the cosmological initial conditions [10]. This is directly relevant to the error-composition rule of Section 4.3. The root-sum-square stacking rule is valid only when stage errors are independent and zero-mean; a strictly deterministic ontology in which apparent stochasticity is hidden correlation is precisely the scenario in which the independence assumption fails and the worst-case linear rule governs. The paper's contribution here is to make the stakes of that metaphysical choice numerical: under a fully correlated error model the same five one-percent stages compose to $5 \times 10^{-2}$ rather than $2.2361 \times 10^{-2}$.

**Relational ontology as the parametrization's warrant.** The static-architecture paper makes three interlocking claims: the cosmos is static, its structure is discrete and composed of finite countable elements, and these elements are "purely relational, defined entirely by their connections to one another without an underlying container" [11]. It further distinguishes map from territory — continuous spacetime as the map, the fixed relational network as the territory — and cites the Wheeler-DeWitt equation's static wavefunction as the first of three mathematical lines of evidence [11]. The third claim is the ontological warrant for this paper's parametrization: if the fundamental elements are defined entirely by their connections, then the count of pairwise connections, $n(n-1)/2$, is the natural measure of descriptive content, and Section 4.1's comparison against the $2^n$ amplitude count is the numerical expression of that thesis.

**Computability as the testability boundary.** The ontological-closure paper proposes an operational criterion: a quantity is physically real only if there exists a finite Turing-machine protocol that approximates it with a computable modulus of convergence, yielding measurement-distinguishable predictions; if no finite protocol can converge to a quantity's value, it is operationally indistinguishable from a quantity that does not exist [12]. Its theory-evaluation section draws the sharp consequence: a physical theory that predicts uncomputable quantities is operationally vacuous, and the criterion filters out theories whose observable predictions are not computably approximable [12]. Its experimental-design section connects the criterion to resources: if measuring quantity $Q$ to precision $\varepsilon$ requires $\mu(-\log_2 \varepsilon)$ steps and each step costs $E$ joules, the total energy cost is $E \cdot \mu(n)$ [12]. This supplies the normative backbone of this paper's gate: an admissibility standard is meaningful only for predictions that are computably approximable to the precision the gate demands, and the gate's precision requirement translates directly into a resource requirement through the modulus of convergence.

What the literature assembled here does not contain is any prior work connecting relational computation formalisms to embodied-interface measurement, or any arithmetic connecting per-stage errors to a single admissibility number. That connection is the gap this paper fills, and every claim of the connection made here is derived within this paper rather than attributed.

## 3. Methods

The method is constructive derivation plus protocol mapping, with no new data collection. It has four components.

**Component 1: minimal relational parametrization.** Following the stripping move of [9], and taking the relational thesis in the strong form stated in [11] — elements defined entirely by their connections — we posit that the minimal descriptive content of an $n$-unit computational system is the set of pairwise relations among units, not the exponential amplitude vector of the Hilbert-space description. We define

$$R(n) = \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$

as the number of independent pairwise parameters, against

$$H(n) = 2^n$$

for the Hilbert-space description of $n$ qubits. The comparison ratio is $\rho(n) = H(n)/R(n)$. This is a parametrization-count comparison, not a claim that the two descriptions are empirically equivalent; the equivalence question is exactly what the programme's experiments are designed to test.

**Component 2: standard extraction and unit conversion.** We take the NEXT design target — energy resolution nearing the Fano factor in xenon, $0.5$–$1\%$ FWHM at $Q_{\beta\beta,{}^{136}\mathrm{Xe}}$ [5] — and convert it from FWHM to one-sigma form under the assumption, stated here and carried through the paper, that the resolution figure behaves as a Gaussian width for the purpose of budget composition. The conversion constant is $2\sqrt{2\ln 2}$. We emphasize that [5] states this figure as an anticipation for Phase I, not a measurement; the same paper's report that the NEXT-DBDM demonstrator achieved $1\%$ FWHM for $0.662$ MeV gammas [5] is the closest demonstrated anchor, at a different energy and chamber configuration. Section 6 treats the inheritance as a load-bearing assumption.

**Component 3: error-composition and instrument-budget arithmetic.** We model any candidate computational pipeline — from formalism to rendered prediction to physical readout — as $n$ independent stages, each contributing a relative error $\varepsilon_i$. Two composition rules bound the end-to-end error: the worst-case linear stack $\sum_i \varepsilon_i$, appropriate when stage errors are correlated or adversarially aligned, and the root-sum-square (RSS) stack $\sqrt{\sum_i \varepsilon_i^2}$, appropriate when stage errors are independent and zero-mean. The determinism thesis of [10] is the extreme case of the correlated regime. For the embodied instrument we model a display-plus-tracking loop with three budgeted quantities: frame period $T_f = 1/f$ at frame rate $f$; motion-to-photon latency budget $B$; and display persistence $p$ expressed as a fraction of $T_f$. For a head or hand rotating at angular velocity $\omega$, persistence blur is

$$\theta_{\mathrm{blur}} = \omega\, p\, T_f,$$

and inter-sample angular travel at tracker update rate $f_t$ is

$$\theta_{\mathrm{sample}} = \frac{\omega}{f_t}.$$

All numeric inputs to the instrument model are stated as explicit assumptions in Section 4, because the literature establishes that tracking is the key anchoring challenge [6] and that sickness is a real-time detection problem [7], but does not supply numeric latency figures for our display class; the closest reported operating points are the 27.5 FPS average of the deployed tracking system [6] and the initial multi-second latency of the volumetric assistance prototype before its WebRTC redesign [4].

**Component 4: experiment sizing and protocol mapping.** For a two-group behavioral comparison (relational-perturbation interface versus matched baseline) we use the standard group-size formula for a two-sided test of a difference in means with equal groups:

$$n_{\mathrm{group}} = \frac{2\,(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2}{d^2},$$

where $d$ is the standardized effect size (Cohen's $d$), $\alpha$ the two-sided significance level, and $1-\beta$ the target power. The normal quantiles $z_{0.975} = 1.95996$ and $z_{0.80} = 0.84161$ are standard statistical constants used as stated inputs. The verification rig itself is defined as follows: a candidate formalism's predictions are rendered as a mixed-reality hologram layer over the physical apparatus, following the shared-view topology of the remote-assistance system [4]. The rig's calibration channels are: automatic skeleton fitting of the operator's self-avatar [1]; instant motion tracking for anchoring the hologram to the apparatus [6]; depth matching between hologram and apparatus, decomposed by the shape/fidelity/color/luminance factors of the mobile-AR depth study [8]; continuous physiological monitoring of the operator in place of retrospective questionnaires [7]; and, where the apparatus occupies a room-scale volume, projection-mapped room re-specification of the kind demonstrated with measured locomotor effects by the Reality Distortion Room [3]. Multi-operator review uses the shared gesture-controlled 3D interface pattern of the multi-user analytics prototype [2]. Every acceptance criterion in the protocol must name, in advance, the observation that would refute it, in keeping with the testability filter of the ontological-closure criterion [12]: a prediction that cannot be computably approximated to the gate's precision is not admissible.

No empirical measurement is reported in this paper. Every number in Sections 4 and 5 is either (a) quoted from a cited work and attributed by number, (b) derived from such a quote by shown arithmetic, or (c) explicitly labelled a projection under stated assumptions.

## 4. Analysis

Every input number below is either a standard mathematical or statistical constant, an explicit assumption of the model stated in place, or a figure quoted from a cited work and attributed by number, and each is labelled as such. All arithmetic is shown.

### 4.1 Relational compression (Result R1)

Take $n = 50$ constituent units, chosen as a round representative scale for a mid-sized register; the choice of $n$ is an assumption and the scaling is given for general $n$ by the formulas of Section 3.

$$H(50) = 2^{50} = 1{,}125{,}899{,}906{,}842{,}624 \approx 1.1259 \times 10^{15}.$$

$$R(50) = \frac{50 \times 49}{2} = \frac{2450}{2} = 1225.$$

$$\rho(50) = \frac{2^{50}}{1225} = \frac{1.1258999068 \times 10^{15}}{1225} = 9.19102 \times 10^{11}.$$

Check: $1225 \times 9.19102 \times 10^{11} = 1.125900 \times 10^{15}$, consistent to the displayed digits. We adopt $\rho(50) = 9.19 \times 10^{11}$.

Memory consequence, as a projection with stated assumptions: if one complex amplitude is stored in $16$ bytes (two $8$-byte double-precision floats — an assumption about representation), the Hilbert description of $n = 50$ requires

$$16 \times 2^{50} = 1.80144 \times 10^{16}\ \mathrm{bytes} = 18.014\ \mathrm{PB},$$

using $1\ \mathrm{PB} = 10^{15}$ bytes, while the pairwise description requires

$$16 \times 1225 = 19{,}600\ \mathrm{bytes} = 1.96 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{bytes}.$$

The ratio is the same $\rho(50) = 9.19 \times 10^{11}$.

### 4.2 The Fano-gate band from the NEXT resolution target (Result R2)

**Input (cited).** The NEXT Phase I design anticipates an energy resolution nearing the Fano factor in xenon of $0.5$–$1\%$ FWHM at $Q_{\beta\beta,{}^{136}\mathrm{Xe}}$ [5].

**Step 1: express the FWHM band in relative form.** The stated band is

$$\frac{\mathrm{FWHM}_E}{Q_{\beta\beta}} \in [0.005,\ 0.01].$$

**Step 2: convert FWHM to one sigma.** For a Gaussian, $\mathrm{FWHM} = 2\sqrt{2\ln 2}\,\sigma$. The constant is

$$2\sqrt{2\ln 2} = 2\sqrt{2 \times 0.693147} = 2\sqrt{1.386294} = 2 \times 1.177410 = 2.354820.$$

Therefore $\sigma_E = \mathrm{FWHM}_E / 2.354820$.

**Step 3: compute the relative one-sigma band.** At the $1\%$ FWHM end:

$$\frac{\sigma_E}{Q_{\beta\beta}} = \frac{0.01}{2.354820} = 4.2466 \times 10^{-3}.$$

Arithmetic: $0.01 / 2.354820 = 4.24661 \times 10^{-3}$, rounded to $4.2466 \times 10^{-3}$. At the $0.5\%$ FWHM end:

$$\frac{\sigma_E}{Q_{\beta\beta}} = \frac{0.005}{2.354820} = 2.1233 \times 10^{-3}.$$

Arithmetic: $0.005 / 2.354820 = 2.12323 \times 10^{-3}$
