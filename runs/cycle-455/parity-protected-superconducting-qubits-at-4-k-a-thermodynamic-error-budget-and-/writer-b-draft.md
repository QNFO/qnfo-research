# Parity-Protected Superconducting Qubits at 4 K: A Thermodynamic Error-Budget and Cooling-Crossover Analysis

## Abstract

Scalable superconducting quantum processors are conventionally operated at millikelvin temperatures, where dilution-refrigerator cooling power is capped near tens of microwatts while every added qubit, cable, and amplifier increases the heat load on a stage that cannot be expanded cheaply. The specific gap addressed here is quantitative: no published analysis relates the cooling-power scaling of cryogenic plants, the heat dissipated per qubit, and the thermally activated error rates of superconducting qubits into a single crossover criterion that says when error-protecting a qubit beats cooling it. We derive such a criterion from the BCS gap-edge Boltzmann factor $p_{\mathrm{th}}(T) = e^{-\Delta/k_{B}T}$, the cooling-power law $\kappa(T) = \kappa_{0}(T/T_{0})^{\alpha}$, and a per-qubit heat-load assumption. For niobium ($T_{c} = 9.2$ K, $\Delta/k_{B} = 16.192$ K) we compute $p_{\mathrm{th}}(4\ \mathrm{K}) = 1.7458 \times 10^{-2}$ against $4.73 \times 10^{-71}$ at 100 mK, a penalty of $3.69 \times 10^{68}$; against a target error $p_{\mathrm{target}} = 10^{-4}$ this demands a parity-selective suppression factor $S \approx 175$, and aluminum junctions ($T_{c} = 1.2$ K) are excluded outright at 4 K ($p_{\mathrm{th}} = 0.5897$). With the conservative cooling-power ratio $2 \times 10^{4}$ between the 4 K and millikelvin stages, the permissible qubit budget rises from $5 \times 10^{3}$ to $10^{8}$ under a 10 nW per-qubit load assumption. The result reframes refrigeration roadmaps: the binding design parameter becomes parity-selective suppression, not base temperature.

## 1. Introduction

The operators building superconducting quantum processors face a scaling wall that is thermodynamic before it is electronic. A dilution refrigerator's mixing chamber, operating near 10 millikelvin, delivers a cooling power capped in the microwatt regime — typically about 50 microwatts for standard commercial systems — while the 4 kelvin pulse-tube stage of the same cryostat offers roughly 20,000 times more, often exceeding 1 watt [8]. Every physical qubit added to the processor carries wiring that bridges the 300 K room-temperature flange to the 10 mK mixing chamber, and because electrical conductivity of the required coaxial lines correlates with thermal conductivity through the Wiedemann–Franz law, this wiring imports a parasitic heat load that scales linearly with qubit count whether or not the qubits are active [8]. The consequence is a hard ceiling: beyond a few hundred to a few thousand qubits, the heat generated by control and measurement systems exceeds the available cooling capacity, and additional qubits degrade the system through thermal crosstalk rather than improving it [9]. Today this ceiling blocks the path from hundred-qubit demonstrators toward the million-qubit regime that fault-tolerant algorithms require, and the standard response — build larger and colder dilution refrigerators — attacks a stage whose cooling power is fixed by the immutable thermodynamics of helium-3/helium-4 mixing rather than by engineering maturity [8].

What would change if the analysis in this paper holds: the hardware roadmap decision of where to spend engineering effort would shift from pushing base temperature down and refrigerator capacity up, toward building error protection into the qubit itself so that it can operate at the 4 K stage, where cooling budget, wiring tolerance, and controls infrastructure are all orders of magnitude more generous. Large-scale 4 K cryogenic operation is mature industrial practice — Fermilab operates and tests superconducting 1.3 GHz cryomodules on standalone Tevatron-style refrigerators at the New Muon Lab facility [2], and the PIP-II project's cryogenic distribution and controls system specifies industrial-grade instrumentation and control hardware, including a Siemens PCS7 system with 26 remote-IO cabinets and 48 relay racks for temperature, valve, level, and heater readouts [4] — so a qubit platform compatible with that stage inherits an existing engineering ecosystem rather than a research-grade one.

The candidate qubit platform for 4 K operation is the parity-protected superconducting qubit. Two families exist in the literature. The first embeds topological Josephson junctions in $0$–$\pi$ qubits: a pair of $0$–$\pi$ qubits built on two-dimensional topological-insulator Josephson junctions forms a parity-spin qubit whose even and odd parity ground states are protected against both charge noise and flux noise, with the topological junction itself suppressing fabrication variation in the Josephson energy [1]. The second couples a conventional transmon to Majorana bound states: the top-transmon uses the superconducting charge qubit as a fermion-parity meter whose coupling to the topological qubit can be switched exponentially by increasing the ratio $E_{J}/E_{C}$, and which is insensitive to subgap quasiparticle excitations — the defining parity-protection property [3]. More recently, a gate-tunable superconductor–altermagnet–superconductor Josephson junction has been proposed to give full gate-voltage control of a parity-protected qubit, removing the magnetic-flux biasing that introduces flux noise, control overhead, and limited scalability in current implementations [5]. All three designs share the property that the protected degree of freedom is fermion parity rather than an energy eigenstate separated from the thermal bath by the superconducting gap alone, which is precisely the property a 4 K architecture must exploit.

The gap this paper fills is the quantitative crossover criterion. Prior work has established the thermodynamic bottleneck qualitatively [8], stated the cooling-power scaling law with exponent $\alpha$ between 2 and 3 [9], and performed a first reconciled error-budget calculation for niobium-based parity qubits at 4 K [6]. What is missing is a single derivation that combines the gap-edge thermal excitation probability, the required parity-selective suppression, the cooling-power ratio between stages, and a per-qubit heat load into an explicit crossover temperature and qubit-budget comparison, so that an architect can decide numerically — not rhetorically — whether protection or cooling is the better buy at a given temperature.

The contributions of this paper are:

- A derivation of the gap-edge thermal excitation probabilities for niobium and aluminum at 4 K and at 100 mK, with fully shown arithmetic, giving $p_{\mathrm{th}}^{\mathrm{Nb}}(4\ \mathrm{K}) = 1.7458 \times 10^{-2}$, $p_{\mathrm{th}}^{\mathrm{Nb}}(100\ \mathrm{mK}) = 4.73 \times 10^{-71}$, and $p_{\mathrm{th}}^{\mathrm{Al}}(4\ \mathrm{K}) = 0.5897$ (Section 4, Steps 1–6).
- A derived requirement on parity-selective suppression: against a target physical error $p_{\mathrm{target}} = 10^{-4}$, an unprotected niobium qubit at 4 K requires $S = 174.6$, and the unsuppressed crossover temperature is $T^{*} = 1.7586$ K (Section 4, Steps 7–8).
- A derived qubit-budget comparison: with the conservative cooling-power ratio $2 \times 10^{4}$ from the literature and an assumed 10 nW per-qubit heat load, the permissible qubit count rises from $5 \times 10^{3}$ at the millikelvin stage to $10^{8}$ at the 4 K stage, and the thermal-photon population of a 5 GHz readout line rises from $0.0998$ to $16.18$ photons between the two stages (Section 4, Steps 9–11).
- An identification of the three error channels that remain unquantified — $\mathrm{Nb_{2}O_{5}}$ two-level-system loss, quasiparticle poisoning of unprotected ancillary elements, and thermal noise in 4 K parity readout — carried over from the prior analysis and restated as open conditions on the crossover claim (Sections 4 and 6).

The crossover criterion derived here is stated in terms that adjacent fields already use: the same gap-edge Boltzmann factor governs quasiparticle poisoning in kinetic-inductance detectors, and the same cooling-power scaling governs any cryogenic detector array, so the numerical criterion may transfer wherever superconducting devices meet limited cryogenic budgets.

## 2. Background and Related Work

The thermodynamic bottleneck argument has two independent lines of support in the literature, a qubit-design line, and a prior quantitative analysis that this paper extends and partially revises.

On the thermodynamic side, the scaling-asymmetry analysis [8] quantifies the mismatch that motivates this paper: the mixing chamber of a dilution refrigerator, operating at approximately 10 millikelvin, is physically constrained to the microwatt regime, typically capping at 50 microwatts for standard commercial systems, while the 4 K pulse-tube stage offers a cooling budget roughly 20,000 times larger, often exceeding 1 watt [8]. The same work identifies the wiring bottleneck as the tangible mechanism: every qubit requires a signal path bridging the 300 K flange to the 10 mK mixing chamber, the cable material's electrical and thermal conductivities are linked by the Wiedemann–Franz law, and the resulting parasitic heat load scales linearly with qubit count regardless of activity [8]. It further grounds the entropy side of the budget in the Landauer limit, under which logically irreversible error-correction cycles must dump entropy into the thermal bath, and it reports a validation of its numerical model against 2024 peer-reviewed coherence data from niobium trilayer and tantalum qubits, calibrating a microscopic TLS coupling constant and an intrinsic $1/f$ noise floor [8]. The cooling-capacity scaling law is stated independently in the Thermodynamic Imperative analysis [9], which writes $\kappa(T) = \kappa_{0}(T/T_{0})^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha$ typically between 2 and 3, and derives a scalability condition $\lim_{N \to \infty} Q_{\mathrm{Total}}(N,T)/\kappa(T) < 1$ whose violation means that even with perfect qubit coherence and optimal error-correcting codes, heat from control electronics and measurement would exceed available cooling capacity beyond a few hundred qubits [9]. The same work notes a materials route — moiré superlattices in twisted bilayer graphene or TMDs that could host topological superconducting states with critical temperatures exceeding 4 K — while flagging that twist-angle control at the required $\pm 0.05^{\circ}$ precision across wafer scales remains an unsolved fabrication bottleneck [9]. This paper uses [8]'s empirical 20,000-fold ratio as its conservative baseline and treats [9]'s $\alpha \in [2,3]$ law as an upper-range sensitivity case; the choice is stated explicitly in Section 4.

On the qubit-design side, the parity-protected superconducting qubit based on topological-insulator Josephson junctions [1] proposes two $0$–$\pi$ qubits built on 2D topological-insulator Josephson junctions, arguing that the topological junction provides protection against fabrication variations and thus ensures the identical Josephson junctions the $0$–$\pi$ qubit requires; its model section treats the two lowest states of a single $0$–$\pi$ qubit as a spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ degree of freedom and combines two such qubits shunted by a capacitor into a parity-spin qubit designed to improve charge noise and flux noise simultaneously, with a second-order sweet-spot expansion of the Hamiltonian in the noise parameters $\lambda \in \{n_{g}, \phi_{e}\}$ [1]. The top-transmon proposal [3] approaches parity protection from the Majorana side: it couples a transmon charge qubit to four Majorana bound states on superconducting islands, and its conclusion states the property this paper relies on — the superconducting qubit functions as a fermion-parity meter that is insensitive to subgap excitations, with the coupling to the topological qubit switchable exponentially small by increasing $E_{J}/E_{C}$, giving an exponential on–off switch on the transmon's adjustable $E_{J}/E_{C}$ ratio [3]. The gate-voltage-control proposal [5] removes the flux-bias overhead of current parity-protected implementations by using a superconductor–altermagnet–superconductor Josephson junction, deriving the Andreev levels of the junction from a BdG Hamiltonian in a scattering-matrix treatment and computing the Fourier coefficients $E_{J,1}$, $E_{J,2}$ of the Josephson potential numerically with a KWANT tight-binding model at parameters $d = 50a$, $W = 7a$, $\Delta = 0.007t$, $L = 325a$, with an InSb-type channel ($m^{*} = 0.024 m_{e}$, $n = 2.7 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm{cm^{-2}}$, $\mu \approx 23$ meV) and an aluminum superconductor with $\Delta = 0.18$ meV and $\xi \approx 1000$ nm [5]. The aluminum gap value stated in [5] matters directly for this paper: Step 6 below shows that aluminum is thermodynamically excluded as a junction material at 4 K, so the altermagnetic control mechanism must be re-hosted on a higher-$T_{c}$ superconductor to serve a 4 K architecture.

On the prior-analysis side, the reconciled thermodynamic error-budget paper [6] is the direct predecessor. It computed $k_{B}T/h = 83.3465$ GHz at 4 K and $2.08366$ GHz at 100 mK, found $p_{\mathrm{th}}^{\mathrm{Nb}}(4\ \mathrm{K}) = 1.5006 \times 10^{-2}$ against $\approx 1.12 \times 10^{-73}$ at 100 mK with an operating-point penalty of order $10^{71.13}$, excluded aluminum at 4 K with $p_{\mathrm{th}}^{\mathrm{Al}}(4\ \mathrm{K}) = 0.5898$, and derived a required suppression factor $S = 150$ on one identified channel against a $10^{-4}$ target, meeting the target only below $T^{*} \approx 1.82$ K [6]. Its discussion states explicitly what its analysis does not show: three error channels remain unquantified — thermally activated TLS loss in $\mathrm{Nb_{2}O_{5}}$, quasiparticle poisoning of unprotected ancillary elements required for gate operations, and thermal noise in parity readout at 4 K, where the thermal photon population at the 83 GHz scale is non-negligible for any readout line not heavily attenuated and filtered [6]. This paper recomputes the niobium thermal factor from the standard BCS gap convention $\Delta = 1.76\, k_{B}T_{c}$, obtaining $1.7458 \times 10^{-2}$ rather than [6]'s $1.5006 \times 10^{-2}$ — a factor of 1.16 that does not change any conclusion — and extends the analysis with the cooling-crossover and qubit-budget calculations that [6] does not perform.

Finally, the survey-correction paper [7] contributes a caution about how platform claims propagate into decisions: it demonstrates that a factual error in survey literature — there, the premature announcement that non-Abelian anyons were experimentally confirmed in 2020 in fractional quantum Hall systems, where the confirmed $\nu = 1/3$ observations are Abelian with exchange acting by a scalar phase of unit modulus — propagates into real funding and research decisions, and it complements its corrections with derived figures such as an anyon-sector error-correction threshold $p_{c} \geq 0.037$, a factor of 37 above an assumed operating error rate of $10^{-3}$ [7]. The lesson applied here is that the 4 K architecture claim must carry its numbers and its unmet conditions openly, as [7] does and as [6] does, rather than as a qualitative posture.

The cryogenic-engineering works [2] and [4] establish what the 4 K stage's industrial ecosystem looks like. The New Muon Lab facility at Fermilab is a superconducting 1.3 GHz cryomodule test facility in which a single Type III+ cryomodule is cooled and tested using a single Tevatron-style standalone refrigerator [2] — evidence that multi-watt-class cryogenic plants with the thermodynamic headroom [8] quantifies are operated routinely. The PIP-II cryogenic controls design specifies a Siemens PCS7 controls system with 26 remote-IO Rittal cabinets and 48 relay racks for temperature readouts, valve positioners, level, and heater control across the cryogenic distribution system and cryomodules [4] — evidence that the instrumentation and control layer for large 4 K-class cryogenic infrastructure is standardized industrial technology, not a research frontier.

## 3. Methods

This paper is an analytical derivation with fully shown arithmetic. It performs no experiment, no device simulation, and no measurement; every number in Section 5 is computed in Section 4 from stated inputs, or is labeled a projection with stated assumptions.

**Model.** The physical error channel that temperature controls directly is thermally activated quasiparticle excitation across the superconducting gap. Under the BCS assumption, the probability scale for a quasiparticle to be thermally excited is the gap-edge Boltzmann factor

$$p_{\mathrm{th}}(T) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta}{k_{B}T}\right),$$

where $\Delta$ is the superconducting gap and $k_{B}$ is Boltzmann's constant. We take the BCS gap convention $\Delta = 1.76\, k_{B} T_{c}$, so that $\Delta/k_{B} = 1.76\, T_{c}$ in kelvin. This is the same channel structure the prior analysis used [6]; the only convention difference is the gap prefactor, which we state where it matters (Step 3 note).

The parity-protection mechanism enters as a single phenomenological suppression factor $S_{\mathrm{par}}$: the ratio of the unprotected thermally activated error probability to the error probability of the protected qubit on the same channel. The literature supports the existence of such suppression in two forms: the top-transmon's parity meter is insensitive to subgap excitations, with coupling switchable exponentially small in $E_{J}/E_{C}$ [3], and the topological-insulator parity qubit suppresses charge and flux noise simultaneously at second order at its sweet spots [1]. No source fixes a numerical value of $S_{\mathrm{par}}$ for a realized device, so we treat $S_{\mathrm{par}}$ as a design requirement to be met rather than a measured quantity, and we state an illustrative value only as an assumption.

The thermodynamic side uses the cooling-power ratio between stages. Two characterizations exist in the literature: the empirical ratio of roughly $2 \times 10^{4}$ between the 4 K pulse-tube stage (exceeding 1 W) and the 10 mK mixing chamber (capped near 50 µW) [8], and the power law $\kappa(T) = \kappa_{0}(T/T_{0})^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha \in [2,3]$ [9]. We take the empirical ratio as the conservative baseline; the divergence between the two characterizations and its consequence are stated in Section 4.

**Assumptions.** We make the following assumptions, each labeled where used: (A1) the BCS gap convention $\Delta = 1.76\, k_{B}T_{c}$; (A2) a target physical error probability per gate operation $p_{\mathrm{target}} = 10^{-4}$, the same target the prior analysis used [6]; (A3) a per-qubit heat load $q = 10\ \mathrm{nW}$ at both stages, a stand-in value no source provides, used only for the qubit-budget projection; (A4) a readout frequency $f = 5$ GHz for the thermal-photon calculation; (A5) that the parity-protected qubit's protected channel is the thermally activated quasiparticle channel, with the three channels the prior analysis left unquantified — $\mathrm{Nb_{2}O_{5}}$ TLS loss, ancilla quasiparticle poisoning, and 4 K readout thermal noise — carried as open conditions rather than folded into the budget [6].

**Derivation plan.** Section 4 proceeds in eleven steps: thermal frequency scales (Step 1), gap scales (Step 2), niobium thermal factors at both temperatures (Steps 3–4), the aluminum exclusion (Step 5–6), the required suppression factor and unsuppressed crossover temperature (Steps 7–8), the cooling-power ratio under both characterizations (Step 9), the qubit-budget projection (Step 10), and the readout thermal-photon population (Step 11). All logarithms are natural unless written $\log_{10}$.
