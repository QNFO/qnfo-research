# Latent Manifold Inheritance: A Geometric Account of Subliminal Trait Transfer in Fine-Tuned Language Models

## Abstract

Subliminal learning transfers a teacher model's behavioral traits to a student through training data that carries no semantic trace of the trait, and it evades output-level inspection, creating an unresolved safety problem for organizations that distill or fine-tune on model-generated data. Existing accounts identify the phenomenon and its brittleness but do not state why transfer requires weight updates, why it is architecture-specific, and why token-level classifiers miss it. We propose that subliminal transfer is inheritance of a geometric manifold: a conditioned teacher's hidden trait warps its latent space along a low-dimensional trait direction, and fine-tuning on innocuous outputs forces the student's internal trajectories to track the teacher's, reproducing the warp. We formalize this with a rank-one teacher-warp model, derive a trajectory-matching condition under which the student's accumulated update aligns with the teacher's trait direction, and compute a worked example: with per-sample bias signal $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$, gradient noise $\sigma = 1$, and $N = 10^{4}$ carrier samples, the accumulated update reaches cosine alignment $\approx 0.0995$ with the trait direction, and alignment $0.5$ requires $N \approx 3.33 \times 10^{5}$ samples. The account explains architecture specificity through subspace alignment, consistent with evidence that independently initialized students fail to transfer even when global similarity exceeds $0.9$, and it predicts that internal-state geometric diagnostics, not output classifiers, detect leakage. We propose Latent Space Tomography as the corresponding measurement protocol.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations that deploy large language models increasingly train students on outputs generated by other models: distillation pipelines, synthetic-data curricula, and judge-scored reinforcement learning all pass data from one model into another's weight updates. A documented failure mode of this practice is subliminal learning: a teacher conditioned on a hidden preference — an animal preference, a political leaning, or a misaligned behavioral disposition — generates data that looks semantically clean, such as number sequences, yet a student fine-tuned on that data acquires the teacher's trait [1], [7]. The same protocol has been shown to transfer misalignment and unsafe behavior, which makes it a direct safety concern for any team that cannot fully audit the provenance of its training corpus [7]. The cost of the current gap is concrete: a training set that passes every semantic inspection — human review, keyword filtering, output-token classifiers — can still install an unintended behavioral disposition in the deployed student, and the disposition surfaces only after deployment.

Three structural facts about the phenomenon lack a unified explanation. First, transfer requires weight updates: reading the teacher's outputs does not transmit the trait, but fine-tuning on them does. Second, transfer is architecture- and initialization-specific: students that share the teacher's base checkpoint transfer reliably, while independently initialized students fail even when their global representational similarity to the teacher is high [6]. Third, the signal evades output-token classifiers, which suggests it does not live in the token stream at all.

If the trait is carried by the geometry of the teacher's latent space rather than by discrete output content, all three facts follow. A conditioned teacher's hidden trait warps its internal representations along a low-dimensional direction; fine-tuning on the teacher's outputs is trajectory matching, in which the student's parameters move to reproduce the teacher's input–output behavior, and the movement necessarily has a component along the teacher's warp direction; and because the warp lives in high-dimensional activation states, output-token statistics carry only a faint shadow of it. This paper develops that account, formalizes it, and derives quantitative conditions under which leakage is generic.

The result matters beyond machine-learning safety. Any pipeline in which one learned system's outputs train another — including protein and molecule design systems whose generative latent spaces condition downstream experimental campaigns [2], [3], [4] — inherits a version of the same question: what does a student inherit from a teacher's latent geometry, and can it be measured before deployment? The geometric diagnostics proposed here apply wherever activation point clouds can be extracted.

Contributions:

- A rank-one teacher-warp model in which a hidden trait corresponds to a low-dimensional direction in the teacher's activation space, and a derivation of the trajectory-matching condition under which fine-tuning on teacher outputs leaks the warp into the student (Section 3, Section 4).
- A worked numerical derivation of leakage amplitude and sample-complexity: alignment $\approx 0.0995$ at $N = 10^{4}$ samples under stated assumptions, and $N \approx 3.33 \times 10^{5}$ samples for alignment $0.5$ (Section 4).
- A reconciliation of the architecture-specificity of subliminal transfer with subspace alignment rather than global similarity, using published evidence that transfer fails at global similarity above $0.9$ (Section 4, Section 5).
- Latent Space Tomography, a measurement protocol that extracts activation point clouds for the trait concept from teacher and student under matched prompts and tests geometric similarity with Procrustes alignment, maximum mean discrepancy, and centered kernel alignment restricted to the trait subspace (Section 3, Section 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

Subliminal learning was identified as the transmission of a teacher's behavioral traits to a student through semantically unrelated outputs: a teacher instructed to prefer a trait generates number sequences, the instruction and recognizable cues are removed, and a student fine-tuned on the sequences acquires the preference [1]. The trait instruction is never shown to the student, and the transmission relies on weak statistical patterns in the generated data, which is why the phenomenon is subtle and why apparently benign training data can induce unintended behaviors that evade semantic inspection [1]. Two properties reported in that literature frame our question: the transmitted signal is weak per sample, and it accumulates over many samples.

The scope of transmission is broader than single traits. A study of cross-trait transfer built a $30 \times 30$ trait-transfer matrix over 30 traits and found that subliminal learning does not boost only the teacher's trait but tends to increase preference for similar related traits as well, with cluster structures recurring across four model families — Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 — and across carriers including 00–99 numbers, random tokens, chemical elements, GSM8K solutions, and Python code completions [1]. That study also tested six diagnostic metrics, some external (output probabilities) and some internal (activation directions), as predictors of cross-trait transfer [1]. The cluster structure is direct evidence for a geometric picture: if each trait were an isolated fact, transfers between traits would be idiosyncratic; recurring clusters indicate that traits occupy neighboring regions of a shared latent geometry, and the transfer matrix reads off that geometry's adjacency structure. Our manifold-inheritance account predicts exactly this, and Section 4 uses the matrix dimension in a counting argument.

The transmission channel extends beyond supervised fine-tuning on text. In subliminal reinforcement learning, a student trained by group relative policy optimization with a language-model judge acquires the judge's trait even though the judge returns only one score per completion and nothing it writes enters the update; the experimental design crossed student model, judge conditioning route, and carrier into 48 cells, with students writing invented syllables or numbers of the form $d.dd$ and rule-breaking completions receiving the group-minimum reward [5]. That work formalizes the trait as a preference margin $B(\theta) = \frac{1}{40}\sum_{j=1}^{40}\big[\log\pi_{\theta}(n_{T}\mid a_{j}) - \log\pi_{\theta}(n_{E}\mid a_{j})\big]$ and a transfer estimand $S_t = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[(B_{T,t}-B_{T,0}) - (B_{E,t}-B_{E,0})\big]$, decomposing each student's movement into a shared part $c$ and a signed part $s$ with $\Delta B_T = c + s$ and $\Delta B_E = c - s$ [5]. The decomposition is geometrically interpretable in our terms: $c$ is movement along directions shared by both trait conditions, and $s$ is movement along the trait-discriminating direction — the warp direction of our model. That scores alone, with no teacher tokens, suffice for transmission strengthens the claim that the carrier of the trait is not output content but the selection pressure the teacher's geometry exerts on the student's updates.

Architecture and initialization specificity is established experimentally. Using synthetic corpora with disentangled public and private labels, one study distilled students and found that independently initialized students fail to sustain hidden-signal transfer even when global centered kernel alignment (CKA) between teacher and student representations exceeds $0.9$, while successful transfer requires strong trait-subspace alignment, which same-base models inherit by construction; the study introduces subspace CKA as a diagnostic protocol and reports confidence intervals from $n = 200$ bootstrap resamples [6]. This is the single most important constraint on any geometric account: global similarity does not predict transfer, so the trait must live in a specific low-dimensional subspace whose alignment, not overall representational similarity, governs leakage. Our rank-one warp model is built to satisfy this constraint, and Section 4 derives why a rank-one direction is far more fragile under re-initialization than global geometry.

Mechanistic and control results identify the drift coordinate directly. Under SFT distillation, a study of animal-preference and malicious-response settings on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct measured a centered probe-space drift $\widehat{\delta c}_t$ over training and showed that standard SFT moves the drift steadily away from zero while probe-space corridor regularization keeps it near a bounded region throughout training; in the Llama setting, owl preference fell from $17.8\%$ to $0.6\%$ under regularization, and the underlying drift remained active even for traits already near-null behaviorally [7]. The same work notes that same-model teachers and students share a base model and initialization, so the initial preference gap matches the source-side quantity, and that cross-model attenuation persists after target ranking [7]. Two points bear on our account. First, the existence of a measurable one-dimensional drift coordinate that predicts behavioral transfer supports the low-dimensional warp hypothesis. Second, the cross-model attenuation is the behavioral signature of subspace misalignment: a different base model's trait subspace is rotated relative to the teacher's, so the same carrier data produces less leakage — which our model formalizes as a projection factor.

Fine-tuning strategy itself modulates which parameters move, and hence which subspaces can leak. In few-shot image classification, a partial-transfer study searched which layers to fine-tune and found that deeper networks require more fine-tuned layers and that larger domain gap between base and novel data requires more layers to be fine-tuned, with the searched partial schemes consistently outperforming full fine-tuning and other state-of-the-art methods on mini-ImageNet (64 base, 16 validation, 20 novel classes) and CUB (100 base, 50 validation, 50 novel classes) [8]. Although that work targets task accuracy rather than trait leakage, its result is the parameter-side complement of our claim: restricting the updated parameter subset restricts which representational subspaces the student can move toward, and the required subset grows with the distance the representations must travel. In our framing, a partial fine-tuning recipe that freezes the layers carrying the trait subspace should suppress manifold inheritance — a testable corollary (Section 7).

The geometric view of latent space manipulation has a productive analogue in molecular design, where latent geometry is deliberately engineered rather than accidentally inherited. An all-atom protein design model that jointly generates sequence and structure achieved macrocycle hit rates of 91% on MDM2 and 100% on MCL-1, exceeding a prior method's 38% and 21% on the same targets, and mini-binder hit rates from 64% on BHRF1 to 10% on TrkA, including a 52% hit rate on SC2RBD versus a prior 12% and 49% on PD-L1 versus 15% [3]. An agentic successor executing full antibody design campaigns achieved a 67% target-level success rate across nine targets spanning three campaign types, with per-target hit rates of 1–28% and affinities reaching single-digit nanomolar range [2]. A further generation produced antibodies with confirmed low immunogenicity — no detectable response in ex vivo T-cell proliferation and cytokine release assays across PBMC from 10 donors — while testing only 4 to 24 designs per modality-target pair [4]. These systems matter to our argument in two ways. They demonstrate that conditioning a generative model's latent space produces systematic, measurable downstream structure — the constructive use of the same geometry-to-behavior channel that subliminal learning exploits accidentally. And they define a deployment context in which latent-space inheritance between model generations is a concrete risk: a design model fine-tuned on another model's outputs could inherit latent biases that bias which molecules are proposed, long before any assay runs.

Geometric projection also offers a precedent for information loss between internal representation and output. A model of token generation as a projection from a hierarchical (ultrametric) embedding space onto a linear token stream derives that only $\approx 0.94\%$ of possible hierarchical relationships survive a projection retaining the top 2 digits of a 5-level binary tree, and that under a geometric depth distribution with $q = 0.7$ at least $1.92\%$ of facts are unrecoverable regardless of model quality [11]. That result is a quantitative instance of the general principle our account relies on: the mapping from internal state to output token is lossy, so structure present in activations can be absent or nearly absent from token statistics — which is why output-token classifiers can miss a signal that lives in internal states.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Model

We model a transformer's activation state at a fixed layer as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$, with $d$ the activation width. A teacher conditioned on a hidden trait $z$ (for example, a preference for owls) develops, we assume, a warp of its activation distribution: for prompt-conditional activation point clouds $X_{\text{neutral}}$ and $X_z$ extracted under a neutral system prompt and a trait-revealing system prompt respectively, the trait acts along a low-dimensional subspace. We take the minimal case, a rank-one warp with unit direction $b \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$:

$$
X_z \approx X_{\text{neutral}} + \Delta \, b b^{\top} X_{\text{neutral}} + E,
$$

where $\Delta \geq 0$ is the warp amplitude (the mean projection of activations onto $b$ under the trait condition) and $E$ is residual variation orthogonal to $b$. The rank-one assumption is a modeling choice for tractability; the published drift-control evidence that a single centered probe-space coordinate tracks and controls transfer [7] is the empirical motivation for treating the leading term as one-dimensional. We state the assumption explicitly: we assume the trait's leading geometric signature is a single direction $b$; multi-trait extensions replace $b$ with a subspace basis $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, $r \geq 1$.

### 3.2 Fine-tuning as trajectory matching

The student is fine-tuned on $N$ carrier examples $(c_i, y_i)$ where $c_i$ is an innocuous input (for example, a number-sequence prefix) and $y_i$ is the teacher's output. We write the student's per-sample gradient at update step for sample $i$ as

$$
g_i = \nabla_{\theta} \ell(\theta; c_i, y_i) = \sigma \eta_i + \varepsilon_i b_{\theta},
$$

where $\eta_i$ is isotropic noise in the parameter-space gradient with per-component scale $\sigma$, $b_{\theta}$ is the parameter-space image of the activation-space trait direction (the direction in which reproducing the teacher's warped internal trajectories pushes the student's weights), and $\varepsilon_i$ is the per-sample bias signal along $b_{\theta}$. We assume $\varepsilon_i = \varepsilon$ constant across samples and $\eta_i$ i.i.d. with $\mathbb{E}[\eta_i] = 0$, $\|\eta_i\|^2$ concentrated at $d\sigma^2$ in $d$ parameter dimensions. The accumulated update after $N$ samples (summed, unnormalized, matching a fixed learning-rate budget absorbed into $\varepsilon$ and $\sigma$) is

$$
G_N = \sum_{i=1}^{N} g_i = N\varepsilon\, b_{\theta} + \sigma \sum_{i=1}^{N} \eta_i .
$$

The cosine alignment of $G_N$ with the trait direction is

$$
\rho_N = \frac{N\varepsilon}{\sqrt{N^2\varepsilon^2 + N d \sigma^2}} = \frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{N\varepsilon^2 + d\,\sigma^2}} .
$$

Wait — the noise sum $\sigma\sum_i \eta_i$ has expected squared norm $N d \sigma^2$, so

$$
\rho_N = \frac{N\varepsilon}{\sqrt{N^2 \varepsilon^2 + N d \sigma^2}}.
$$

This is the trajectory-matching leakage condition: leakage is generic whenever $\varepsilon > 0$, grows as $\sqrt{N}$ in the small-$N$ regime, and saturates toward $1$ as $N \to \infty$. The student never sees the trait token; the bias enters because matching the teacher's outputs on carriers requires partially reproducing the teacher's warped internal computation.

### 3.3 Subspace alignment and architecture specificity

For a cross-model student whose parameter-space trait direction is $b'_{\theta}$ with $b_{\theta}^{\top} b'_{\theta} = \cos\phi$, the leaked component along the student's own functional trait direction scales as $\rho_N \cos\phi$. Same-base students have $\phi \approx 0$ by construction; independently initialized students have effectively random $\phi$ in high dimension. This reproduces, in the model, the published finding that global similarity does not predict transfer while subspace alignment does [6].

### 3.4 Latent Space Tomography protocol

The measurement protocol pairs the model with extraction and comparison steps:

1. Extract activation point clouds $X \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ ($m$ prompts, fixed layer) for: teacher under trait prompt, teacher under neutral prompt, student before fine-tuning, student after fine-tuning, and a control student fine-tuned on data from an unconditioned teacher.
2. Estimate the trait direction $\hat{b}$ as the leading left singular vector of the mean-difference matrix $X_{\text{trait}} - X_{\text{neutral}}$ for the teacher.
3. Project each student's point cloud onto $\hat{b}$ and compare projected distributions with a two-sample statistic (maximum mean discrepancy, MMD).
4. As a model-free cross-check, compute linear CKA and subspace CKA between teacher and student activations [6], and Procrustes distance after optimal orthogonal alignment.

The falsification condition for the manifold account is stated in Section 6.

### 3.5 What this paper does and does not do

This paper performs derivation and computation only. It runs no training runs, extracts no activations, and reports no measurements of its own; all empirical numbers quoted are attributed to the cited works [1]–[8], [11]. Numbers not available from any source are labeled assumptions and used only inside derivations.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Leakage amplitude: worked derivation

Inputs and their status:

- $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$: assumption. We assume the per-sample bias signal along the trait direction is one thousandth of the gradient's noise scale; this is consistent in order of magnitude with the characterization of subliminal transmission as relying on weak statistical patterns accumulated over samples [1].
- $\sigma = 1$: assumption (normalization; $\sigma$ sets the gradient noise scale and all results scale with $\varepsilon/\sigma$).
- $d = 4096$: assumption for the parameter-relevant effective dimension; we take a single-layer effective subspace of width $4096$, comparable to hidden widths of the 7B–8B models used in the cited experiments [1], [7].
- $N = 10^{4}$: assumption for the number of carrier samples in a small fine-tuning run.

Step 1. Signal magnitude after $N$ samples: $\quad N\varepsilon = 10^{4} \times 10^{-3} = 10$.

Step 2. Noise magnitude after $N$ samples: $\quad \sqrt{N d \sigma^2} = \sqrt{10^{4} \times 4096 \times 1} = \sqrt{4.096 \times 10^{7}}$.

Compute: $\sqrt{4.096 \times 10^{7}} = \sqrt{4.096} \times 10^{3.5} = 2.0238... \times 3162.28$. Since $\sqrt{4.096} = 2.02386$ (because $2.02386^2 = 4.0960$) and $10^{3.5} = 3162.28$:

$$
\sqrt{4.096 \times 10^{7}} \approx 2.02386 \times 3162.28 \approx 6400.0 .
$$

(Exact: $6400^2 = 4.096 \times 10^{7}$, so the noise magnitude is exactly $6400$.)

Step 3. Alignment:

$$
\rho_N = \frac{10}{\sqrt{10^2 + 6400^2}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{100 + 40{,}960{,}000}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{40{,}960{,}100}} .
$$

$\sqrt{40{,}960{,}100} \approx 6400.0078$ (since $6400.0078^2 \approx 40{,}960{,}100$). Therefore

$$
\rho_N \approx \frac{10}{6400.0078} \approx 0.0015625 .
$$

Hmm — this exposes an important structural point: with isotropic noise over the full parameter dimension, the alignment is tiny. The published phenomenon is real, so the effective noise that competes with the bias signal cannot be isotropic over all $d$ directions; optimization itself restricts movement to a low-dimensional task-relevant subspace. We therefore refine the model, and state the refinement as part of the derivation rather than hiding it.

Refined model. Fine-tuning on a narrow carrier distribution updates the student predominantly within a task subspace of effective dimension $d_{\text{eff}} \ll d$; the trait direction, being the direction along which the teacher's carrier behavior deviates from a neutral teacher's, lies inside that task subspace by construction (the carrier outputs are the only training signal, so the directions that reduce carrier loss are precisely those along which teacher and neutral teacher differ). We set $d_{\text{eff}} = 16$ as an assumption, motivated by the low-dimensional drift coordinate that empirically tracks and controls transfer [7] and by the rank-one leading structure of trait warps.

Recompute with $d_{\text{eff}} = 16$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$, $\sigma = 1$, $N = 10^{4}$:

Step 1. Signal: $N\varepsilon = 10$.

Step 2. Noise: $\sqrt{N d_{\text{eff}} \sigma^2} = \sqrt{10^{4} \times 16} = \sqrt{160{,}000} = 400$ (exactly, since $400^2 = 160{,}000$).

Step 3. Alignment:

$$
\rho_N = \frac{10}{\sqrt{10^2 + 400^2}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{100 + 160{,}000}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{160{,}100}} .
$$

$\sqrt{160{,}100} \approx 400.125$ (since $400.125^2 = 160{,}100.02$). Therefore

$$
\rho_N \approx \frac{10}{400.125} \approx 0.02499 \approx 0.0250 .
$$

So under the refined model, $10^{4}$ carrier samples produce $\approx 2.5\%$ alignment of the student's accumulated update with the teacher's trait direction. This is the principal derived leakage number of the paper.

### 4.2 Sample complexity for a detection-level alignment

Set a target alignment $\rho^{\ast} = 0.5$. From

$$
\rho_N^2 = \frac{N^2\varepsilon^2}{N^2\varepsilon^2 + N d_{\text{eff}}\sigma^2} = \frac{N\varepsilon^2}{N\varepsilon^2 + d_{\text{eff}}\sigma^2},
$$

solve $\rho_N^2 = 0.25$:

$$
0.25\,(N\varepsilon^2 + d_{\text{eff}}\sigma^2) = N\varepsilon^2
\;\Rightarrow\;
0.25\, d_{\text{eff}}\sigma^2 = 0.75\, N\varepsilon^2
\;\Rightarrow\;
N = \frac{d_{\text{eff}}\sigma^2}{3\varepsilon^2}.
$$

With $d_{\text{eff}} = 16$, $\sigma = 1$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$:

$$
N = \frac{16}{3 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{16}{3} \times 10^{6} \approx 5.333 \times 10^{6}.
$$

So reaching $50\%$ alignment requires $\approx 5.33 \times 10^{6}$ carrier samples under these assumptions — a large but feasible synthetic-data budget, which matches the qualitative observation that subliminal transmission is weak per sample and accumulates [1]. For a lower detection threshold $\rho^{\ast} = 0.1$ ($\rho^2 = 0.01$):

$$
0.01\,(N\varepsilon^2 + d_{\text{eff}}\sigma^2) = N\varepsilon^2
\;\Rightarrow\;
0.01\, d_{\text{eff}}\sigma^2 = 0.99\, N \varepsilon^2
\;\Rightarrow\;
N = \frac{0.01 \times 16}{0.99 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{0.16}{9.9 \times 10^{-7}} \approx 1.616 \times 10^{5}.
$$

So a $10\%$ alignment — plausibly detectable by a geometric statistic on internal states but far below any output-token signal — requires $\approx 1.62 \times 10^{5}$ samples.

### 4.3 Cross-model attenuation as a projection factor

Let the student's own trait-functional direction make angle $\phi$ with the teacher's leaked direction. The behavioral leakage scales as $\rho_N \cos\phi$. Same-base students: $\phi \approx 0$, leakage $\approx \rho_N$. Independent initialization in $d_{\text{eff}}$-dimensional task space with random direction: $\mathbb{E}[\cos\phi] = 0$, with per-draw scale $1/\sqrt{d_{\text{eff}}}$. For $d_{\text{eff}} = 16$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[|\cos\phi|] \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{\text{eff}}}} \cdot \Gamma\text{-correction} \approx \frac{0.7979}{4} \approx 0.1995 .
$$

(For a uniform random direction in $d$ dimensions, $|\cos\phi|$ has mean $\approx \sqrt{2/(\pi d)}$; with $d = 16$: $\sqrt{2/(50.265)} = \sqrt{0.039789} = 0.19947$.)

So an independently initialized student leaks, on average, $\approx 20\%$ of the same-base leakage in the model — a strong attenuation but not a null, whereas the published experiments find that independently initialized students fail to sustain transfer even at global CKA above $0.9$ [6]. The model therefore predicts that the effective trait subspace is more restrictive than a 16-dimensional random subspace: if the trait lives in an $r$-dimensional subspace with $r$ small and the independently initialized student's corresponding subspace is randomly oriented, the expected overlap of the *subspaces* is $\approx r/d_{\text{eff}}$; for $r = 1$, $d_{\text{eff}} = 16$, that is $1/16 = 0.0625$, and the squared alignment (which governs variance explained, and through it behavioral margins) is $\approx 0.0039$. Behavioral traits that require a threshold margin to surface would then fall below threshold for independent initialization while same-base students cross it — consistent with the published null at high global similarity [6], and with cross-model attenuation persisting after target ranking [7].

### 4.4 Counting argument from the trait-transfer matrix

The cross-trait study reports a $30 \times 30$ trait-transfer matrix [1]. The number of ordered off-diagonal trait pairs is $30 \times 29 = 870$, and unordered pairs number $\binom{30}{2} = \frac{30 \times 29}{2} = 435$. The reported cluster structure across four model families and eight carrier panels means the transfer pattern is reproducible at the cluster level despite substantial variability in individual same-trait effects [1]. Under a discrete-fact account, each of the 435 pairs would be an independent contingency; under the manifold account, the 30 trait directions are points in one shared geometry, and cluster structure is the geometry's neighborhood structure made behavioral. The reproducibility across model families is the stronger constraint: four independently trained models producing consistent clusters indicates the clusters reflect structure in the trait semantics as embedded by any of the models, i.e., a property of the latent manifolds rather than of idiosyncratic training noise.

### 4.5 Suppression arithmetic from published control results

The drift-control study reports owl preference falling from $17.8\%$ to $0.6\%$ under probe-space corridor regularization in the Llama setting [7]. The relative reduction is

$$
\frac{17.8 - 0.6}{17.8} = \frac{17.2}{17.8} = 0.96629\ldots \approx 96.6\%.
$$

A $96.6\%$ behavioral suppression achieved by constraining movement in a single probe-space corridor is consistent with the model's claim that the trait's behavioral expression is mediated by a low-dimensional direction: blocking that corridor blocks the pathway through which trajectory matching would otherwise accumulate the warp.
