# Simulation Inconsistency Detection via Neurobiological and Cognitive Interfaces

## Abstract

The hypothesis that experienced reality is rendered by a digital substrate predicts measurable timing artifacts at the interface between that substrate and the human cognitive system, but the existing literature lacks a mechanistic account of *where* in neural processing such artifacts would appear and *how many* observations their detection requires. This paper supplies that account. We model the brain as an active constructor of percepts — post-hoc narrator, predictive-coding hierarchy, and agency attributor — and argue that a rendering engine must accommodate this constructive interface, leaving four classes of detectable signatures: narrative patch artifacts, superdeterministic cross-system correlations, ultrametric discretization of temporal binding, and load-dependent latency variability. We derive the quantization noise a 60 Hz render clock contributes to cross-modal timing, $\sigma_q = 4.811$ ms (2.406 ms at 120 Hz), reproduce the sample size $N = 1536$ needed to detect a 5% deviation from the assumed baseline glitch-detection accuracy of $p_0 = 0.5077$ under an explicit statistical convention, and compute two new requirements: approximately $4.7 \times 10^3$ trials to detect the variance inflation that a 60 Hz clock imposes on a 20 ms biological timing estimate, and approximately 1537 participants to detect a 0.05 cross-participant latency correlation consistent with a global frame reset. These figures show that single-laboratory studies are individually underpowered and that detection is feasible only through pooled, load-manipulated, multi-site designs. The framework converts a philosophical hypothesis into a bounded measurement program with explicit falsification conditions.

## 1. Introduction

Engineers who build teleoperation systems, virtual-reality platforms, and simulator-based training environments face a recurring measurement problem: when a human operator reports that audio and video feel out of sync, or when psychophysical testing reveals an abnormally wide window over which the brain binds events into one percept, the team must decide whether the asynchrony is an ordinary property of human neural timing or a defect of the delivery pipeline. The distinction matters commercially and scientifically. A binding window that widens under load may reflect attentional biology, or it may reflect a scheduler that renders each sensory modality on an independent clock; the two explanations call for different fixes, different tolerances, and different instrumentation. Today this decision is made without a quantitative budget: there is no standard answer to the question "how much timing jitter would a discrete render clock contribute, and how many measurements would it take to see it?" Answering that question would let simulator designers, neuro-engineers, and cognitive-load researchers separate substrate-level artifacts from biological noise with stated confidence, and would tell them in advance whether the test is affordable at all.

This paper addresses that gap by treating the human cognitive system not as a passive receiver of a rendered world but as an active constructor of it — a framing with independent support in neuroscience — and by asking where, in that constructive machinery, a rendering engine's computational compromises would become visible. The brain narrates its actions after the fact, generates percepts by minimizing prediction error, compresses the interval between a voluntary action and its outcome, and stretches remembered duration under arousal. A simulator must feed this machinery data coherent enough that the machinery's own smoothing does not expose discontinuities; the paper's central claim is that the machinery's smoothing operations are precisely the places where imperfect data delivery leaves characteristic, quantifiable traces.

The work continues a research line established in three earlier papers from the same program. The quantitative predecessor [11] assumed that a digital substrate renders visual and auditory streams on independent clocks, took a baseline temporal-glitch detection accuracy of $p_0 = 0.5077$ for existing multimodal models, and computed the statistics of detecting the resulting asynchronies: a 60 Hz render clock contributes $\sigma_q = 4.811$ ms of latency noise, and detecting a 5% deviation from baseline requires $N = 1536$ independent trials. A companion catalog [10] showed that culturally recurrent "sacred" integers carry a non-Archimedean, ultrametric structure: scoring integers by a divisibility-depth function $\Omega(n)$ separates a sacred set (mean $\Omega = 2.4771$) from matched random integers (mean $1.8681$) at $p \approx 0.03$, evidence that human cognition itself organizes quantity in a hierarchical, tree-like metric. The present paper [9] extends both: it supplies the neurobiological mechanisms that the quantitative framework treated as assumptions, and it turns the observer's cognitive architecture from a source of metrological bias into a detection instrument.

The contributions are:

- A derivation of the quantization noise $\sigma_q$ that discrete render clocks of 60 Hz and 120 Hz contribute to cross-modal timing estimates, with the arithmetic shown (Section 4).
- A recomputation of the trial requirement $N = 1536$ for detecting a 5% deviation from the assumed baseline $p_0 = 0.5077$, under an explicitly stated statistical convention (Section 4).
- A new result: approximately $4.7 \times 10^3$ trials are required to detect the variance inflation a 60 Hz clock imposes on a 20 ms biological timing estimate, showing that the individual-level jitter test is at the edge of feasibility (Section 4).
- A new result: approximately 1537 participants are required to detect a cross-participant latency correlation of 0.05, the signature predicted by a global frame-reset architecture (Section 4).
- A taxonomy of four signature classes — narrative patch artifacts, superdeterministic correlations, ultrametric temporal discretization, and load-dependent latency — each mapped to a neural mechanism and to a measurement paradigm (Sections 2 and 3).
- Explicit falsification conditions and a limitations register for the whole framework (Section 6).

Beyond simulators and teleoperation, the statistical machinery developed here — budgeting sample sizes for the detection of small variance inflations and weak cross-system correlations — bears directly on any field that must distinguish a weak systemic artifact from biological or instrumental noise, including cognitive-load instrumentation and multi-sensor fusion.

## 2. Background and Related Work

The paper sits at the junction of cognitive neuroscience, formal anomaly detection, and human-machine interface engineering. Eight strands of prior work constrain what the framework can claim.

The quantitative foundation is [11], which models a digital rendering substrate operating on independent per-modality clocks and derives the detection statistics for the resulting cross-modal asynchronies. That work assumes a baseline temporal-glitch detection accuracy of $p_0 = 0.5077$ for existing multimodal models, computes that a 60 Hz render clock contributes $\sigma_q = 4.811$ ms of latency noise (2.406 ms at 120 Hz), and reports the trial requirements for each signature: $N = 1536$ trials for a 5% deviation from baseline, $n \approx 566$–$570$ trials for the Kolmogorov–Smirnov shape signature at distance $D \approx 0.057$, $n = 397$ trials for a 10 ms systematic offset, and $n \approx 433$ trials for a 10% load-induced widening of the temporal binding window. The present paper takes those numbers as its starting budget and supplies the mechanistic account of *why* the signatures take those forms.

The mechanism account begins with the constructive brain. [4] reviews cognitive computation with autonomously active neural networks and documents the central null model that any artifact claim must beat: the neural response to identical stimuli shows trial-to-trial variation often as large as the response itself, arising from ongoing internal dynamics that experimenters cannot control and customarily average out as noise. [4] further argues that most neural networks used in research and applications are stimulus–response systems reset to a default state between tasks, which is "clearly not the way the brain works," and develops reservoir computing and the notion of neural transients as an intermediate regime. For the present framework this is decisive: apparent neural "noise" has a well-characterized internal generator, so a claim of externally imposed jitter or of superdeterministic correlation must exceed what autonomous eigendynamics produces on its own.

The constructive account extends to high-level narration and its emotional modulation. [2] analyzes cognition and emotion from a functional standpoint and concludes that emotional control operates through neuromodulators as a *diffusive* modulation of neural activity rather than direct cognitive control, serving homeostasis and life-long fitness across two time windows (day-to-day survival via primary drives; life-long Darwinian fitness via emotional control). This matters for the retrospective time-distortion signature proposed in [9]: arousal-driven memory densification is a diffuse, homeostatic modulator, which makes it a poor discriminator between biological and substrate-level explanations unless its statistics are compared against the diffusive-control model [2] provides.

On the measurement side, [3] demonstrates a working pipeline for classifying cognitive load from functional near-infrared spectroscopy and eye tracking in a driving simulator using deep learning, and identifies a concrete gap: most driving-simulator studies employ only two workload levels, and low-light conditions remain underexplored. Since every load-dependent signature in the present framework (Sections 3 and 4) requires a graded, validated load variable, the two-level limitation [3] documents is a direct methodological constraint on the experimental program, and the fNIRS-plus-eye-tracking pipeline is the instrumentation it can borrow.

Automated anomaly flagging has an existing template in [5], which proposes Cefdet, a cognitive effectiveness network for action detection built on a fuzzy-driven cognitive effectiveness evaluation module (FCM) with four components — feature quantization, fuzzification, fuzzy inference, and defuzzification — that locates frames with "cognitive abnormalities" by combining confidence, inter-frame correlation, and position scores, validated on JHMDB, UCF101–24, and AVA2.2. The FCM design is a concrete precedent for the automated glitch-screening pipeline the present framework needs: a system that flags data frames whose integration the downstream consumer finds incoherent, before human review.

Formal deterministic inconsistency detection at scale is demonstrated in [1], which presents a hybrid neuro-symbolic framework combining large language models with symbolic logic to achieve *deterministic* detection of statutory inconsistency, using the U.S. Internal Revenue Code as a case study precisely because its complexity makes conflicts likely. The relevance is architectural: [1] shows that inconsistency detection over a large, complex, formally renderable corpus can be made deterministic rather than heuristic, which is the property a screening layer over rendered event streams would need.

Multi-observer diversity as a detection resource is quantified in [8], a cognitive diversity framework for radar target recognition in which the sensor chooses its perspectives adaptively. [8] reports that using two perspectives at $\Delta\theta = 5°$ reaches 85% performance, that the median number of perspectives taken by the cognitive algorithm is 3 irrespective of the angular step, and that the cognitive framework outperforms both single-perspective and fixed-number multi-perspective schemes. This is the methodological argument for the distributed, multi-participant designs in Section 7: a diverse ensemble of observers, each an imperfect sensor, outperforms any single observer for target (glitch) recognition.
