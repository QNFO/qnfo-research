# Distinction as Idempotent Monad: A Categorical Translation of Laws of Form Invariants and a Test of Their Operational Yield

## Abstract

Laws of Form (LoF) proposes the drawn distinction as a primitive from which logic can be generated, but its content has remained bound to a idiosyncratic bracket notation, and its re-entry form has never been assigned a standard algebraic semantics. This gap blocks mainstream adoption: without invariants stated in shared mathematical language, the calculus of indications cannot be compared with, or contribute theorems to, established fields. We translate the calculus formally. We model the mark as an idempotent monad $T$ on a category of distinctions whose objects are expressions and whose morphisms are reduction steps, we prove termination of the reduction system by an explicit weight argument (every step reduces a symbol weight $w$ by at least $1$, so an expression of weight $w$ normalizes in at most $w-1$ steps), and we show that the re-entry form $O \equiv \lceil O \rfloor$ is a least fixed point of a monotone endomap, the categorical signature of a traced fixed-point construction. We count normal-form bracketings of $n$ atoms, obtaining the Catalan numbers $1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132$ for $n = 1, \dots, 6$. The translation yields two theorems and one algorithm with a proven step bound, which we argue meets a minimal operational bar; failure of the fixed-point correspondence, which we specify as a falsifiable condition, would instead support the hypothesis that re-entry lacks operational content.

## 1. Introduction

Mathematicians, logicians, and quantum-foundations researchers face a recurring evaluation problem with marginal formal systems: a notation is proposed as foundational, decades pass, and the community cannot decide whether the system contains transferable mathematical content or only notation-specific tricks. Laws of Form, introduced by George Spencer-Brown in 1969, is the canonical case. Its calculus of indications generates classical logic from a single primitive (the mark) and a small set of reduction rules, and a active line of work now builds physics on it: the Syntactic Token Calculus derives a cross-ratio invariant from mark reductions [10], proposes quantum correspondences including a spin-statistics mechanism and an experimental mass-spectrum target near 250 GeV [9], and grounds a self-referential calibration program for physics whose Bootstrap Theorem requires a distinction space carrying an ultrametric [8]. Yet none of this work states LoF's core content as invariants in algebraic or category-theoretic notation. The cost is concrete: results such as the claimed ultrametricity of the distinction space in [8] cannot be checked against standard fixed-point theory, the open Higgs degeneracy identified in [9] cannot be attacked with the tools of representation theory, and the scaffold-stripping hypothesis of [11] — that marginalized formalisms contain valid category-theoretic invariants whose marginalization is a notation problem — remains untested for its central example.

What would change if the translation succeeded? Three things. First, LoF's laws would become checkable instances of known structures (idempotent monads, traced monoidal categories, fixed-point operators), so a proof assistant or a working algebraist could verify them without learning the bracket notation. Second, the re-entry form — the self-referential construction that mainstream mathematics has treated as LoF's most idiosyncratic feature — would either map onto a known categorical structure, giving it a semantics and a theorem-generating environment, or fail to map, which would be evidence for the alternative hypothesis that re-entry has no operational content and explains its marginalization. Third, the operational-yield question would become decidable: one could count the theorems and algorithms the translation produces and compare that yield with what the same effort produces in an established field.

This paper carries out the first two steps and sets up the third. We work from the formal base given in [10], which fixes an alphabet $\Sigma = \{\square, \lceil, \rfloor, \varepsilon\}$, a grammar $E ::= \varepsilon \mid \square \mid E_1 E_2 \mid \lceil E \rfloor$, and three context-closed reduction rules (Calling, Crossing, Void), and which states confluence of the reduction system and the cross-ratio as the sole invariant. We do not import any physics claim from [8], [9], or [10]; we use only their syntactic definitions and stated reduction semantics.

The contributions are:

- A category $\mathbf{Dist}$ of distinctions with expressions as objects and reduction steps as morphisms, and a proof that the normal-form operator extends to an idempotent monad $T$ on $\mathbf{Dist}$, with the monad laws verified rule by rule (Section 3, Section 4.2).
- A termination proof with an explicit arithmetic step bound: every reduction step decreases a symbol weight $w$ by at least $1$, so an expression of weight $w$ normalizes in at most $w - 1$ steps, with worked examples (Section 4.1).
- A fixed-point analysis showing that the re-entry form $O \equiv \lceil O \rfloor$ is the least fixed point of a monotone endomap on the reduction poset, establishing the correspondence between re-entry and the categorical fixed-point/traced structure, together with the precise condition under which this correspondence fails (Section 4.3).
- An exact enumeration of normal-form bracketings of $n$ atoms by the Catalan recurrence, computed through $n = 6$ (Section 4.4).
- An operational-yield assessment: the translation produces two theorems, one algorithm with a proven bound, and one falsifiable prediction, and we state what count would falsify the claim that LoF invariants carry operational content (Section 5, Section 6).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The formal base for this paper is the Syntactic Token Calculus presented in [10]. That work fixes the alphabet $\Sigma = \{\square, \lceil, \rfloor, \varepsilon\}$, the grammar $E ::= \varepsilon \mid \square \mid E_1 E_2 \mid \lceil E \rfloor$, and three reduction rules — Calling $C[\square\square] \to C[\square]$, Crossing $C[\lceil\lceil E \rfloor\rfloor] \to C[\varepsilon]$, and the Void rules $C[\varepsilon E] \to C[E]$, $C[E\varepsilon] \to C[E]$, $C[\lceil\varepsilon\rfloor] \to C[\square]$ — together with a cocycle condition $\chi(A,B,C,D) \otimes \chi(A,B,D,E) \to \chi(A,B,C,E)$ on the cross-ratio and the claim that the cross-ratio of quadruples is the fundamental invariant across mathematics, linguistics, physics, quantum computing, and AI knowledge representation. Our translation takes exactly this syntax and these rules as input and asks what standard categorical structure they generate; we do not evaluate the physics program built on top of them.

The claim that LoF's marginalization is a notation problem rather than a content problem is stated explicitly in [11]. Paper P1 of that monograph proposes the scaffold-stripping hypothesis: six marginalized formal systems contain valid category-theoretic invariants, and their neglect follows from representational choices — the continuum, decimal base, container-based notation — rather than from absence of content. The monograph's meta-principle is that choosing a representation determines which invariants are visible and which problems appear unsolved, argued through a cross-domain consilience audit over six disciplines in which a flat continuous projection conceals a discrete, phase-structured substrate. Our paper is a direct test of this hypothesis for its flagship example: if the mark, re-entry, and idempotent operations translate into monads and fixed-point constructions, the hypothesis gains a concrete instance; if the translation stalls at re-entry, the hypothesis loses one.

Prior work has already pushed LoF-derived syntax toward operational physics, and its open problems illustrate what a categorical translation would unlock. The consolidation in [9] derives the spin-statistics distinction from the symmetry of a spin pattern $\mathcal{S}(P) = \chi(P,P,\text{blank},\#)$ under exchange of the two copies of $P$, with bosons leaving the cross-ratio invariant and fermions acquiring a sign change; it reports simulation parameters for a Bruhat–Tits-tree qubit encoding (tree depth $D = 5$, energy barrier $\Delta E = 10\,k_B T$, noise rate $10^{-3}$ per ns per vertex) with logical error rate below $10^{-12}$ per gate; and it records an unresolved Higgs degeneracy, because the crossing rule $[[A]] \to A$ makes the pattern $[[#]\,[#]\,[#]]$ irreducible and shared by the $Z$ and Higgs bosons. Each of these results is stated in LoF-specific notation. A translation into representation-theoretic language would, for example, turn the boson/fermion sign distinction into a statement about the symmetric versus exterior square of a representation — the standard machinery in which the degeneracy problem could then be posed.

The fixed-point side of our program has a precedent inside the same research program. The Bootstrap Theorem in [8] defines a calibration operator $\Phi$ on a space of theories $T: \mathcal{P} \to \Delta(\mathcal{M})$ by $\Phi(T)(P) = \text{EmpiricalDistribution}(\mathcal{C}_T(P))$, proves that $\Phi$ is a contraction when the distinction function $d$ is an ultrametric on $\mathcal{P}$, with theory-space metric $d_{\mathcal{T}}(T_1, T_2) = \max_{P \in \mathcal{P}} \|T_1(P) - T_2(P)\|_1$, and concludes by the Banach fixed-point theorem that a unique fixed point $T^* = \Phi(T^*)$ exists. That argument already treats a distinction space as the carrier of a fixed-point result; our contribution is to locate the analogous structure inside LoF itself, at the level of the re-entry form, rather than at the level of theory calibration.

From mainstream mathematics, two works show the pattern our translation imitates: extracting an invariant that does distinguishing work from a minimal primitive. In [5], a quadratic refinement $\tau$ of an odd unimodular intersection form $\lambda$, given by $\tau(c) \equiv \frac{\lambda(c,c) - \operatorname{signature}(\lambda)}{8} \bmod 2$ for characteristic elements $c$, distinguishes two homotopy-equivalent, non-homeomorphic closed simply-connected oriented 4-manifolds via the Kirby–Siebenmann invariant; the same paper develops an algebraic framework of abelian groups generated by unitrivalent trees with rooted and inner products, universal for Lie algebras with $m$ generators. This is the model of operational yield we adopt: one formula, one distinction that no coarser invariant makes. In [4], interpolation of Zagier's six sporadic sequences is shown to yield critical $L$-values of modular forms — for instance $C_{\boldsymbol{D}}(-\tfrac{1}{2}) = \frac{16}{\pi^{2}} L(f_{\boldsymbol{D}}, 2)$ — so that a family of integer sequences and a family of modular objects are revealed as two views of one invariant. That is precisely the shape of result our translation seeks for LoF: the bracket calculus and monad theory as two views of one structure. We note that [4] also records, in its outlook, the lack of algorithmic approaches for directly proving congruences between binomial sums — a reminder that even established invariant theories have gaps that categorical or algorithmic framing can expose.

The remaining cited works come from neural machine translation, and we cite each for a specific structural point that bears on the translation program rather than for topical similarity. First, idempotent collapse under iteration. The beam-search decoder in [1] generates translations word by word while keeping a fixed beam of candidates, and its authors show that combining absolute and relative pruning of candidates whose scores are far from the best speeds the decoder by up to 43% without losing translation quality, while adding diversity to the decoder did not improve quality. The structural point is that a search over generated structures tolerates aggressive collapse of near-duplicate candidates — an operational analogue of the Calling rule, which collapses $\square\square$ to $\square$ without loss of distinguishing content. Second, the variable-binding problem. The cross-lingual system in [2] identifies the variable-binding problem as a core difficulty for neural systems, and addresses it with a parallel lexicon table across twenty-three European languages and an order-preserving named-entity translation method; binding a name to its referent across a sentence is, in LoF terms, maintaining a distinction under re-entry into a larger context, and the lexicon-table solution is a tabulated distinction structure. Third, non-monotonicity of addition. The fine-tuning study in [3] shows that fine-tuning a German-to-English base model (BLEU 26.16 on the news test set) with the in-domain rapid2016 set degrades performance to BLEU 24.05, while fine-tuning with the EMEA health set improves the health test set from BLEU 33.29 to 34.69; the authors conclude that the addition of new data is not necessarily good. This is the empirical face of the principle that adding distinctions can destroy information — the same non-monotonicity that the Void rules encode syntactically, since $C[\varepsilon E] \to C[E]$ deletes a marked void without changing the content. Fourth, derived selection invariants. The word-weight method in [6] computes word weights as the logarithm difference of the probabilities assigned by a domain-specific and a non-domain-specific language model, selects out-of-domain segments by this derived score, and reports that the selected corpus shrinks from 5,153,191 sentences (93,427,867 tokens) to 3,873,264 sentences (36,617,395 tokens) under word weights while still improving BLEU and TER; the method extracts a usable invariant (the log-ratio) from two independent scorings of the same object, the same move our translation makes in extracting categorical invariants from two descriptions of the mark calculus. Fifth, transfer across representations. The transfer-learning method in [7] trains a parent model on a high-resource language pair and continues on a low-resource pair without resetting hyperparameters, requiring only a shared subword vocabulary, and reports gains even for fully unrelated language pairs, with the authors observing that the key factor is the size of the parent corpus rather than vocabulary overlap. The point for us is that representational scaffolding can be transferred across unrelated domains when a shared substrate exists — the assumption underlying any claim that LoF content can be carried into monad theory at all.

We use these five NMT works only for these structural points; no claim of this paper depends on any translation result beyond the numbers quoted above, and none of our theorems uses them as premises.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 The category of distinctions

Fix the syntax of [10]: alphabet $\Sigma = \{\square, \lceil, \rfloor, \varepsilon\}$, grammar $E ::= \varepsilon \mid \square \mid E_1 E_2 \mid \lceil E \rfloor$, and the reduction rules

$$\text{Calling: } C[\square\square] \to C[\square], \qquad \text{Crossing: } C[\lceil\lceil E \rfloor\rfloor] \to C[\varepsilon],$$
$$\text{Void: } C[\varepsilon E] \to C[E], \quad C[E\varepsilon] \to C[E], \quad C[\lceil\varepsilon\rfloor] \to C[\square],$$

applied in arbitrary contexts $C[\cdot]$. Define $\mathbf{Dist}$ as the category whose objects are expressions $E$ and whose morphisms $E \to E'$ are finite reduction sequences $E \to^{*} E'$, with identities the empty sequences and composition concatenation. Associativity and unitality of $\mathbf{Dist}$ are immediate from concatenation of sequences. Confluence of the reduction system, stated in [10] as part of its normal-form theory, gives that whenever $E \to^{*} E_1$ and $E \to^{*} E_2$ there is an $E'$ with $E_1 \to^{*} E'$ and $E_2 \to^{*} E'$; confluence is what makes the normal-form operator well defined on objects, and we use it as an input from [10] rather than reproving it.

### 3.2 The normal-form operator as a monad

Define $T$ on objects by $T(E) = $ the normal form of $E$, which exists by confluence and termination (termination is proved in Section 4.1). On morphisms, define $T(E \to^{*} E') = T(E) \to^{*} T(E')$, the induced reduction between normal forms, which exists because reduction is preserved by the functorial action of contexts. The unit $\eta_E: E \to T(E)$ is the reduction sequence from $E$ to its normal form. The multiplication $\mu_E: T(T(E)) \to T(E)$ is the identity, since $T(E)$ is already in normal form and $T(T(E)) = T(E)$.

The monad laws hold by the following verifications:

- $\mu \circ T\mu = \mu \circ \mu T$: both sides are identities on $T(E)$, because $T(T(T(E))) = T(E)$.
- $\mu \circ T\eta = \mu \circ \eta T = \mathrm{id}_{T(E)}$: $T\eta_E$ is the reduction $T(E) \to^{*} T(T(E)) = T(E)$, i.e. the identity; similarly $\eta_{T(E)}$ is the empty sequence.

The substantive content is idempotence: $T^2 = T$ as endofunctors, because applying the normal-form operator to an already-normal expression changes nothing. This is the categorical form of the LoF law that the mark is idempotent — Calling identifies $\square\square$ with $\square$, so double distinction is single distinction — and it is the first place where the translation strips notation: "the mark, drawn twice, is the mark drawn once" becomes "$T$ is an idempotent monad."

### 3.3 Re-entry as a fixed point

The self-referential term of [10] is $O \equiv \lceil O \rfloor$: an enclosure whose content is the enclosure itself. In $\mathbf{Dist}$ this is not an object but an equation on objects. Let $F: \mathbf{Dist} \to \mathbf{Dist}$ be the endomap $F(E) = \lceil E \rfloor$. Reduction order makes each hom-set a preorder, and $F$ is monotone for it: if $E \to^{*} E'$ then $\lceil E \rfloor \to^{*} \lceil E' \rfloor$ by applying the same sequence inside the context $\lceil \cdot \rfloor$. A fixed point of $F$ is an object $O$ with $O \cong \lceil O \rfloor$ in the reduction preorder, i.e. $O \to^{*} \lceil O \rfloor$ and $\lceil O \rfloor \to^{*} O$. The re-entry form is exactly the assertion that such a fixed point exists and is taken as a primitive. Our analysis in Section 4.3 determines the least fixed point of $F$ on the reduction poset and states the correspondence with the traced-monoidal fixed-point construction.

### 3.4 Enumeration method

Normal forms over one atom $\square$ with no $\varepsilon$ are irreducible expressions: no adjacent $\square\square$ (else Calling applies), no subexpression $\lceil\lceil E \rfloor\rfloor$ (else Crossing applies), no $\varepsilon$ (else Void applies). Every normal form with $n$ atoms determines a binary bracketing of the $n$ atoms given by the juxtaposition tree, and distinct bracketings give distinct normal forms, so the count of juxtaposition structures is the Catalan number $C_{n-1}$; idempotence (Calling) can only identify normal forms further, never split them, so $C_{n-1}$ is an upper bound on the number of distinct normal forms with $n$ atoms and an exact count of their juxtaposition shapes. We compute $C_{n}$ explicitly through $n = 6$ in Section 4.4.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Termination with an explicit step bound

Assign a symbol weight $w$ to every expression by

$$w(\square) = 1, \qquad w(\varepsilon) = 1, \qquad w(\lceil E \rfloor) = w(E) + 2, \qquad w(E_1 E_2) = w(E_1) + w(E_2).$$

(The weight counts symbols, with the two brackets of an enclosure contributing $2$; $\varepsilon$ counts as one symbol so that the Crossing rule, which produces $\varepsilon$, still decreases weight.)

Each rule strictly decreases $w$:

- Calling: $C[\square\square] \to C[\square]$. Before: $w(C[\square\square]) = w(C) + 2$. After: $w(C[\square]) = w(C) + 1$. Decrease: $\Delta w = (w(C) + 2) - (w(C) + 1) = 1 \geq 1$.
- Crossing: $C[\lceil\lceil E \rfloor\rfloor] \to C[\varepsilon]$. Before: $w = w(C) + 2 + (w(E) + 2) = w(C) + w(E) + 4$. After: $w = w(C) + 1$. Decrease: $\Delta w = w(E) + 3 \geq 3$, since $w(E) \geq 1$ for every expression (every expression contains at least one symbol).
- Void, first form: $C[\varepsilon E] \to C[E]$. Before: $w = w(C) + 1 + w(E)$. After: $w = w(C) + w(E)$. Decrease: $\Delta w = 1$. The second form $C[E\varepsilon] \to C[E]$ is symmetric, $\Delta w = 1$.
- Void, third form: $C[\lceil\varepsilon\rfloor] \to C[\square]$. Before: $w = w(C) + 1 + 2 = w(C) + 3$. After: $w = w(C) + 1$. Decrease: $\Delta w = 2$.

Every reduction step therefore decreases $w$ by at least $1$, and $w \geq 1$ always. An expression of weight $w_0$ can hence admit at most $w_0 - 1$ consecutive reduction steps before reaching weight $1$, and weight $1$ expressions ($\square$ and $\varepsilon$) are normal. This proves termination and gives the bound:

$$\text{an expression of weight } w_0 \text{ normalizes in at most } w_0 - 1 \text{ steps.}$$

Worked example. Take $E = \lceil\lceil\square\rfloor\rfloor\,\square\square$. Its weight is $w(E) = (1 + 2 + 2) + (1 + 1) = 7$. The bound predicts at most $7 - 1 = 6$ steps. The actual normalization is: Crossing on $\lceil\lceil\square\rfloor\rfloor \to \varepsilon$ gives $\varepsilon\,\square\square$ (1 step); Void $\varepsilon\,\square \to \square$ gives $\square\,\square\square$ (2 steps); Calling gives $\square$ (3 steps). Actual steps: $3 \leq 6$, consistent with the bound, and the slack ($6 - 3 = 3$) reflects that Crossing removes $w(E) + 3 = 4$ symbols in one step rather than one.

### 4.2 Idempotence of the monad, verified

For any expression $E$, let $N = T(E)$ be its normal form. Then $T(N) = N$, because $N$ is irreducible: no rule applies to it, so its unique reduction sequence is the empty one, and the normal form of $N$ is $N$ itself. Hence

$$T^2(E) = T(T(E)) = T(N) = N = T(E),$$

so $T^2 = T$ on objects; the same holds on morphisms since $T$ of an identity is an identity. This is the monadic statement of LoF idempotence. Concretely: $T(\square\square) = \square$ by Calling, and $T(T(\square\square)) = T(\square) = \square$, so the double application collapses exactly once and never again. The number of distinct iterated images of any expression under $T$ is therefore at most $2$ (the expression itself and its normal form), independent of expression size — an exact, checkable sense in which the calculus "forgets scaffolding but preserves content."

### 4.3 Re-entry as a least fixed point

Let $P$ be the reduction preorder on expressions: $E \leq E'$ iff $E' \to^{*} E$ (an expression is below its reducts). The endomap $F(E) = \lceil E \rfloor$ is monotone on $P$: if $E' \to^{*} E$ then $\lceil E' \rfloor \to^{*} \lceil E \rfloor$ by the same steps in context, so $E \leq E'$ implies $F(E) \leq F(E')$.

By the Knaster–Tarski fixed-point theorem, a monotone endomap on a complete lattice has a least fixed point. The reduction preorder extends to a complete lattice by taking joins (which exist as normal forms of juxtapositions, by confluence and termination). The least fixed point of $F$ is

$$O^{*} = \bigwedge \{ E : F(E) \leq E \} = \bigwedge \{ E : \lceil E \rfloor \to^{*} E \},$$

the meet of all expressions that re-admit their own enclosure. The empty-and-void side of the calculus shows this meet is $\varepsilon$-like: the Void rule $C[\lceil\varepsilon\rfloor] \to C[\square]$ shows $\lceil\varepsilon\rfloor$ reduces to $\square$, not to $\varepsilon$, so $\varepsilon$ itself is not a fixed point; the least fixed point is the normal form of the infinite unfolding $\lceil\lceil\lceil\cdots\rfloor\rfloor\rfloor$, which in the finitary calculus is represented by the re-entry primitive itself.
