# Functorial Invariance and Context-Relative Indecomposability Across Presentations of the Prime Numbers

## Abstract

The prime numbers admit many simultaneous presentations: as the atomic subset of the natural numbers, as the support of the von Mangoldt function, as the zero data of the Riemann zeta function, as the output of sieve procedures, and as the output of concrete algorithms. Working number theorists move between these presentations freely, but the literature rarely states which theorems survive a change of presentation and which are artifacts of the encoding. We formalize the question with a category $\mathbf{RepPrimes}$ whose objects are presentations of the primes and whose morphisms are structure-preserving translations, and we extend it with a two-level schema in which "atomicity" is defined relative to a chosen factorization system. We prove that the Euler product, Möbius inversion, and the leading asymptotic $\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$ are invariants of this category, while the error term $E(x) = \pi(x) - \operatorname{Li}(x)$ is not: the Legendre-sieve presentation carries a main term inflated by the factor $2e^{-\gamma} = 1.1229189642$ relative to $x/\log x$, a $3.54\%$ excess over $\pi(10^6) = 78498$ whose corrected decomposition attributes the dominant cancellation to the excess of $\operatorname{Li}$ over $x/\log x$, not to the fluctuation of $\pi$. A weighted Germain-prime lower bound of $2.72069 \times 10^6$ at $x = 10^6$ converts, under stated assumptions, to a projected unweighted scale of $1.9693 \times 10^5$. The relativity schema explains why primes, uniformizers, and Galois conjugacy classes are each atomic only relative to a level, and a decidable syntactic test gives a sound transfer criterion.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The problem

Every mathematician who works with the primes maintains, usually without comment, a private equivalence relation on ways of talking about them. The statement $\sum_{n \le x} \Lambda(n) \sim x$ and the statement $\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$ are treated as the same theorem, because the first implies the second by partial summation and the second implies the first by a standard Abelian argument. The statement that $\zeta(s)$ has no zeros on $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$ is treated as equivalent to both. Yet the proofs live in different worlds: one in elementary arithmetic, one in complex analysis, one in the theory of Dirichlet series. The tacit claim that these are "the same theorem" is a claim about a category that has not been written down and used as a working tool.

The cost of leaving this claim tacit is concrete for the people who transport prime-theoretic statements between contexts: analytic number theorists porting sieve bounds into zeta-zero language, cryptographers relying on primality assumptions whose precise formulation depends on the ambient structure, and formalization specialists encoding prime objects in proof assistants, where a functorial characterization of the primes has been constructed explicitly as a decidable prime type mapped through a realizability topos to an étale sheaf whose support is the set of primes [9]. A hypothesis of the form "for all primes $p$" transfers between presentations; a hypothesis of the form "for the least prime $p$ with property $P$" generally does not, because the least element of a set is not preserved by an order-preserving map that fails to reflect order. A bound of the form $O(x/\log x)$ in a sieve presentation corresponds to a bound of a different shape in the analytic presentation, and conflating the two shapes produces error terms wrong by a factor that grows with $x$. If the invariance results of this paper hold, the transfer question acquires a mechanical answer: exactly the functorial image of a statement transfers, and a syntactic test decides which hypotheses are safe.

A second, deeper version of the same problem concerns not statements but objects. Which entities are the "atoms" of arithmetic — the indecomposable pieces out of which everything else factors? The candidate answers differ by level: primes in $\mathbb{N}$, uniformizers in $\mathbb{Z}_p$, conjugacy classes in the absolute Galois group, points of an adele space, primitive notions in a type-theoretic foundation. Each candidate is indecomposable relative to the factorization notion of its own level, and the same object can be atomic at one level and decomposable at another. If atomicity is context-relative in a controlled way — if moving to a richer level systematically shifts the indecomposables while preserving the analytic invariants — then the regress of "what is truly prime?" terminates not at a final object but at invariant-preserving equivalences, and the question "which presentation is the right one?" is replaced by the question "which invariants must a level preserve?"
