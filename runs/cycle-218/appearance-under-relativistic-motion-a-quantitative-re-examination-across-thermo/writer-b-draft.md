# Appearance Under Relativistic Motion: A Quantitative Re-Examination Across Thermodynamic, Radiative, and Structural Channels

## Abstract

A recurring question in relativistic physics is whether a physical system "appears" the same, or is in any operational sense invariant, to an observer moving at relativistic speed relative to it. The gap is definitional: "appears invariant" is ambiguous until one states which quantity is measured and under which protocol. We re-frame the question operationally — invariance of *what* observable, extracted *how* — and analyze three appearance channels using only special-relativistic kinematics with every arithmetic step shown: thermodynamic appearance (apparent temperature of a moving gas and of a blackbody radiation field), radiative appearance (Doppler factor, bolometric beaming, and radiation pressure on a receding mirror), and structural appearance (a modeled atmospheric re-entry event). For a representative speed $\beta_1 = 0.8$ we compute the Doppler factors $D_{+} = 3$ and $D_{-} = 1/3$, the two candidate temperature-transformation outcomes $T_0/\gamma_1 = 0.6\,T_0$ and $\gamma_1 T_0 = 1.666667\,T_0$, and the solid-angle-averaged apparent temperature of an isotropic blackbody field, $\bar{T} = 0.8240\,T_0$, a net cooling of $17.6\%$. We further compute an idealized receding-mirror radiation pressure $F_1/A = 0.1778\,u_0$ against the static value $2u_0$, a Jüttner tail suppression factor of $4.54\times10^{-5}$, and, for a modeled re-entry event at $\beta = 0.9$, a directional apparent-temperature ratio of exactly $19$ and a bolometric flux ratio of $130321$. The principal result: appearance is generically not invariant; only properly defined rest-frame quantities are, and invariance where it exists is engineered, as in momentum-entangled Bell states. This converts a philosophical puzzle into a set of checkable protocol-dependent numbers.

## 1. Introduction

Practitioners who interpret signals from relativistically moving systems face a concrete measurement problem: the number they extract from their instruments is not the rest-frame quantity they often want. Astrophysicists fitting temperatures, fluxes, and apparent velocities to relativistic jets must correct for Doppler factors and beaming that depend on viewing angle and internal velocity structure; a jet's apparent superluminal motion and its Doppler-boosted brightness are functions of collimation, not frame conventions alone [7]. Designers of light-driven propulsion, where relative speeds between light source and sail are expected to reach the relativistic regime, must compute polychromatic light–matter interaction for a moving object, a calculation that is challenging precisely because the object's optical response is velocity-dependent [3]. In thermodynamics, the century-old controversy over whether a moving body reads cooler ($T' = T/\gamma$), hotter ($T' = \gamma T$), or unchanged is unresolved at the level of what a moving observer's fitted temperature actually is; numerical simulations of relativistic gases come down on the cooler branch [1]. And in quantum communication, Lorentz boosts can degrade spin entanglement unless the shared state is prepared in a specific way, which matters for any protocol spanning observers in relative motion [6].

What is at stake is decision-relevant. If an observer assumes apparent quantities are invariant when they are not, a beaming factor of $361$ (computed below for a specific configuration) is misattributed to intrinsic luminosity; a radiation-pressure estimate can be off by an order of magnitude; a fitted temperature can shift by tens of percent. Conversely, if invariance holds for a properly prepared observable — as it does for Bell-state spin entanglement with entangled momenta [6] — protocols can be built on it deliberately. The capability this paper supplies is a protocol-explicit accounting: for each appearance channel, which observable is reconstructed, what it equals at a stated speed, and by what factor it departs from the rest-frame value.

This paper makes a narrow, verifiable contribution. We state explicit measurement protocols, derive the apparent observables with every arithmetic step shown, and quantify the departure from invariance at fixed representative speeds. We claim no new physics; we claim a clarified and quantified answer to the appearance-invariance question, assembled from standard kinematics and consistent with the cited literature. The contributions are:

- Derivation of the solid-angle-averaged apparent temperature of an isotropic blackbody field, $\bar{T} = T_0\,\operatorname{atanh}\beta/(\gamma\beta) = 0.8240\,T_0$ at $\beta = 0.8$, a $17.6\%$ cooling, with the integral shown in full (Section 4.1, part D).
- Derivation of the idealized radiation pressure on a receding perfect mirror, $F/A = u_0\,2\beta(1-\beta)/(1+\beta) = 0.1778\,u_0$ at $\beta = 0.8$, against $2u_0$ at rest (Section 4.1, part E).
- Derivation of the exact directional apparent-temperature ratio $(1+\beta)/(1-\beta) = 19$ and bolometric flux ratio $19^4 = 130321$ for a modeled re-entry event at $\beta = 0.9$ (Section 4.2).
- A synthesis establishing that the single positive invariance result in the examined literature [6] requires a special state preparation, supporting the conclusion that appearance invariance is engineered, not generic (Sections 2 and 6).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The question of relativistic appearance has a long and contested history, and each of the nine works examined here contributes a specific result or method that this paper uses.

**[1] Numeric Experiments in Relativistic Thermodynamics: A Moving System Appears Cooler (arXiv:0804.3836v3).** This work simulates a two-dimensional relativistic ideal gas with a relativistic elastic binary collision algorithm, using $100{,}000$ identical particles in a rectangular box with reflecting walls, and shows that the simulated gas faithfully obeys Jüttner's speed distribution $f(v) = v\gamma_v^4\exp(-\beta\gamma_v)/Z$ with normalization $Z = e^{-\beta}(1+\beta)/\beta^2$ and mean energy $\langle E\rangle = (\beta^2+2\beta+2)/(\beta^2+\beta)$. Combining the simulation with the relativistic equipartition theorem, the authors conclude that the simulations strongly favor the Mosengeil–Planck–Einstein formula $T' = T/\gamma$, so that moving objects appear cooler. Their introduction also documents the opposing tradition: Ott, Arzelies, and others proposed $T' = \gamma T$, while Einstein (in letters to von Laue), Landsberg, and others considered temperature a relativistic invariant, and Balescu explored a "gauge" equivalence of all transformations. This is the closest existing answer to our motivating question on the thermodynamic channel: the fitted temperature of a moving gas is not invariant but reduced. Our Section 4.1 derives the corresponding kinematic factors analytically for a blackbody field and obtains the same sign of effect on the angular average, and our part F uses the Jüttner weight their simulation validates.

**[2] Feynman-Vernon model of a moving thermal environment (arXiv:1803.10300v1).** This paper reviews the Feynman-Vernon formulation of linear dissipative systems for a Brownian particle in an external potential and introduces a generalized oscillator model in which the $n$-th bath oscillator's Lagrangian is $L_n = \frac{m}{2}[(\dot{q}_n - v_{\mathrm{env}})^2 - \omega_n^2(q_n - x)^2]$, obtained by shifting the oscillator velocities by a common function of time $v_{\mathrm{env}}(t)$ representing the velocity of the thermal environment. The formulation is summarized by an influence phase $\Delta S/\hbar$, and the authors state the model is expected to suit problems involving particle motion in moving environments such as superfluids. The structural relevance to our question is direct: if the thermal environment must be assigned a velocity $v_{\mathrm{env}}$ in the dissipative dynamics, then the environment's state as seen by the particle is frame-dependent, and the machinery of open quantum systems must carry that velocity as an explicit parameter. We use this as independent evidence that the thermodynamic appearance of a moving environment is a live formulation problem, not a settled convention.

**[3] Computing the interaction of light pulses with objects moving at relativistic speeds (arXiv:2404.05117v1).** This work provides a methodology for the theoretical and numerical analysis of polychromatic light pulses interacting with relativistically moving objects, motivated by optical traps, optical tweezers, and futuristic light sails where relative speeds should reach the relativistic regime. It applies the polychromatic T-matrix formalism, decomposing the incident field into multipolar components $f_{jm\lambda}(k)$, and computes energy and momentum transfer between a pulse and a uniformly moving silicon sphere by switching to the co-moving frame, where the rest-frame T-matrix applies. Notably, the authors find the alternative — direct use of the Lorentz-boosted T-matrix in the laboratory frame — impractical, because the interaction may occur arbitrarily far from the frame origin and multipolar orders cannot then be truncated without losing information about the scatterer. This establishes that the optical appearance (the mechanical response to incident light) of a moving object is velocity-dependent and computationally nontrivial, motivating the radiative channel of our analysis.

**[4] Rayleigh-Sommerfeld scalar diffraction by apertures moving at relativistic speeds (arXiv:2010.02547v2).** The authors analytically obtain Rayleigh-Sommerfeld scalar diffraction results for single-slit, double-slit, grating, circular, and rectangular apertures moving at relativistic speeds with velocity perpendicular to the direction of incidence, and study an oscillatory-shutter single slit. Their conclusion states that the moving-aperture results capture the transverse Doppler effect, $\omega' - \omega = \omega(\gamma - 1)$ and $\lambda' - \lambda = \lambda(1/\gamma - 1)$, in the intensity distribution observed from moving frames, that the results are accurate for all speeds, and that while experimental realization of the transverse Doppler effect on scalar diffraction would be difficult, realization via the oscillatory shutter would be easy. This is direct evidence that the structural appearance of an object — its diffraction signature — is not invariant under transverse relativistic motion, and it supplies a concrete experimental handle that our framework's structural channel could be tested against.

**[5] Particle acceleration timescales in relativistic shear flows (arXiv:astro-ph/0506719v1).** This review treats the acceleration of energetic particles in relativistic astrophysical jets with significant velocity shear. Its microscopic picture is that particles gain energy by scattering off small-scale magnetic irregularities with different local velocities, with energy conserved in the local commoving fluid frame, so that a particle crossing the shear changes momentum relative to the local scattering frame and a net gain occurs for isotropic distributions. The conclusions state that gradual shear acceleration in small-scale relativistic jets may naturally account for a steady second population of synchrotron-emitting particles beyond shock-accelerated ones, with spectra whose flatness depends on the turbulence level. For our purposes this shows that even the effective particle spectrum produced by a flow is profile- and frame-dependent: the appearance of an acceleration environment differs across frames and across flow structures, extending the non-invariance of appearance from thermodynamics to astrophysical particle populations.

**[6] Relativistic-invariant quantum entanglement between the spins of moving bodies (arXiv:quant-ph/0211159v2).** Building on the observation of Gingrich and Adami (Phys. Rev. Lett. 89, 270402 (2002)) that spin-momentum mixing under Lorentz boosts can change spin entanglement — so that maximally entangled spin states will most likely decohere depending on the initial momentum wave function — this paper shows that if the momenta of a spin-1/2 pair are appropriately entangled, with the state written as $\Psi(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}) = g(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})|\psi^{-}\rangle$ on the singlet $|\psi^{-}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle - |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)$, the entanglement between the spins of the Bell states remains maximal when viewed from any Lorentz-transformed frame. The authors further suggest a relativistic-invariant protocol for quantum communication allowing non-relativistic quantum information theory to be applied invariantly in relativistic situations. This is the one clear positive invariance result in the corpus, and it is instructive for our thesis: invariance is achieved not for free but by a specific preparation condition. Appearance invariance, where it exists, is engineered.

**[7] The influence of collimation on the appearance of relativistic jets (arXiv:1010.5895v1).** The authors numerically compute the apparent velocity and Doppler factor of a non-homokinetic jet under different velocity profiles $\gamma(\theta)$, using an intensity parametrization $I(\alpha) = I_0\left[\sqrt{1-\beta(\alpha)^2}/(1-\beta(\alpha)\cos\alpha)\right]^3$ for a shell of uniform surface emissivity and flat spectrum. Their conclusions are quantitative: for a velocity profile characterized by an angular scale $\Delta\theta$, the apparent velocity is upper-limited by $1/\Delta\theta$, so large apparent velocities require highly collimated jets; small apparent velocity and high Doppler factor can co-occur with large opening angles; and TeV blazars, which require apparently large Doppler factors but show generally subluminal motion, could be characterized by large opening angles. This is both substantive support and the closest methodological template for our work: apparent observables computed as explicit functions of viewing geometry and internal structure, exactly the protocol-explicit stance we adopt.

**[8] Appearance Under Relativistic Motion: A Quantitative Re-Examination Across Thermodynamic, Radiative, and Structural Channels (QNFO).** This prior treatment, under revision here, reports the same operational framing and a set of computed results: Doppler factors $D_{+} = 3$, $D_{-} = 1/3$, $D_{\perp} = 0.6$ at $\beta_1 = 0.8$; candidate temperature transformations $0.6\,T_0$ versus $1.666667\,T_0$; an angularly averaged blackbody apparent temperature $0.8240\,T_0$; a receding-mirror pressure $0.1778\,u_0$ against $2u_0$; and a re-entry event at $\beta = 0.9$ with directional temperature ratio $19$ and flux ratio $130321$. Its own discussion flags that the $17.6\%$ cooling figure is protocol-relative and that its mirror calculation should not be compared numerically to full blackbody-drag results. We recompute and carry forward these results with the arithmetic fully displayed, restructure the treatment, and add the engineering-translation analysis it lacked.

**[9] Critical Examination of Spacetime, Mass, and Gravity through Competing Ontological Frameworks (QNFO).** This work surveys challenges to the relativistic paradigm and reports, in its treatment of one-way light-speed anisotropy, that Stefan Marinov's coupled-shutters experiment — a one-way variant of the Fizeau technique — yielded a claimed Earth "absolute velocity" of $360 \pm 40$ km/s, a result that, if accurate, would re-establish a preferred frame, and that the experiment, despite its simplicity and low cost, has not been independently replicated. The same work catalogues further strains on the relativistic null hypothesis, including tentative Lorentz-invariance-violation evidence in gamma-ray bursts. We cite it for a specific boundary condition on our own assumptions: our analysis takes the isotropy of light speed and special-relativistic kinematics as given, and the existence of contested anisotropy claims, unreplicated to date, marks exactly the assumption whose failure would overturn every number in this paper.

**Synthesis.** Across thermodynamics [1], open quantum systems [2], optical interaction [3], diffraction [4], astrophysical acceleration [5], quantum information [6], and jet astronomy [7], the consistent picture is: rest-frame quantities are invariant; received and reconstructed appearance quantities are not, except under special preparations [6]. The prior quantitative treatment [8] established the numerical skeleton; the foundational-critique literature [9] marks the assumption base. Our contribution is to quantify the generic departure at fixed speeds with fully explicit arithmetic and to translate the result into protocol and engineering terms.

## 3. Methods

**Protocol definition.** We define an *appearance observable* $X_{\mathrm{app}}$ as any quantity an observer reconstructs from received signals — photon frequencies, fitted temperatures, radiation pressures, diffraction patterns, apparent velocities — and an *invariant observable* $X_{\mathrm{inv}}$ as a quantity defined in the system's rest frame: proper temperature $T_0$, proper length $L_0$, proper pressure. The invariance question is whether $X_{\mathrm{app}} = X_{\mathrm{inv}}$ for a moving observer, under a stated extraction protocol.

**Kinematic inputs.** All derivations use only: (i) the Lorentz factor $\gamma(\beta) = (1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}$; (ii) the relativistic Doppler factor for a line-of-sight velocity component $\beta_{\parallel}$, $D = 1/[\gamma(1-\beta_{\parallel})]$; (iii) photon number and momentum conservation at a moving perfect mirror; (iv) the Jüttner exponential weight $\exp[-(\gamma_{\mathrm{p}}-1)mc^2/(k_B T)]$ for a particle of Lorentz factor $\gamma_{\mathrm{p}}$, which the simulation of [1] shows a relativistic gas obeys.

**Representative speeds.** We fix $\beta_1 = 0.8$ as the primary benchmark for the thermodynamic and radiative channels, and additionally compute the speed at which the forward Doppler factor reaches $D = 10$. For the re-entry scenario we adopt $\beta = 0.9$ as an assumed representative speed. As a cross-check at a more modest speed we also evaluate the equipartition cooling branch at $\beta = 0.6$.

**Scope.** We treat flat spacetime only, inertial frames only, and idealized objects (perfect mirror, isotropic blackbody field, unidirectional monochromatic beam, single-temperature re-entry sheath). We assume the isotropy of the one-way speed of light, an assumption whose empirical status is discussed in [9]. All quantitative results in Section 5 are computed in Section 4; no simulation or experimental data are reported.

## 4. Analysis

All arithmetic is shown explicitly. No number is imported from an external measurement.

### 4.1 Thermodynamic and radiative channels at $\beta_1 = 0.8$

**A. Lorentz factor.**
$$\gamma_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta_1^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-0.64}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.36}} = \frac{1}{0.6} = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.666667.$$

**B. Doppler factors (longitudinal).** For motion directly toward the observer ($\beta_{\parallel} = -\beta_1$, approaching):
$$D_{+} = \frac{1}{\gamma_1(1-\beta_1)} = \frac{1}{(5/3)(0.2)} = \frac{1}{1/3} = 3.$$
For receding motion ($\beta_{\parallel} = +\beta_1$):
$$D_{-} = \frac{1}{\gamma_1(1+\beta_1)} = \frac{1}{(5/3)(1.8)} = \frac{1}{1/3} = \frac{1}{3}.$$
For transverse observation ($\beta_{\parallel} = 0$): $D_{\perp} = 1/\gamma_1 = 0.6$ (transverse Doppler redshift). The forward/backward apparent-temperature ratio is $D_{+}/D_{-} = 3/(1/3) = 9$.

**C. Candidate temperature transformations.** Two candidate rules appear in the relativistic-thermodynamics literature; as [1] documents, the Mosengeil–Planck–Einstein branch is $T' = T/\gamma$ and the opposing branch (Ott, Arzelies and others) is $T' = \gamma T$. We compute both at $\beta_1 = 0.8$ for a rest temperature $T_0$:
$$T_{\mathrm{cool}} = \frac{T_0}{\gamma_1} = \frac{3}{5}T_0 = 0.6\,T_0, \qquad T_{\mathrm{hot}} = \gamma_1 T_0 = \frac{5}{3}T_0 = 1.666667\,T_0.$$
The ratio between the two candidates is $\gamma_1^2 = 25/9 \approx 2.777778$. The simulation study of [1] concludes the cooler branch is realized for a moving gas; our blackbody calculation below independently yields a net-cooling result on the angular average. As a cross-check at $\beta = 0.6$: $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.36} = 1/0.8 = 1.25$, and the cooler branch gives $T' = T_0/1.25 = 0.8\,T_0$; for an assumed laboratory rest temperature $T_0 = 300\ \mathrm{K}$, $T' = 1.25^{-1}\times 300\ \mathrm{K} = 240\ \mathrm{K}$, a $20\%$ reduction.

**D. Apparent temperature of an isotropic blackbody field.** An observer moving at $\beta_1$ through an isotropic blackbody field of rest temperature $T_0$ measures, in a direction making angle $\theta$ with the velocity (photons arriving from direction $\theta$), an apparent temperature
$$T_{\mathrm{app}}(\theta) = \frac{T_0}{\gamma_1(1-\beta_1\cos\theta)}.$$
Forward direction ($\theta = 0$): $T_{\mathrm{app}} = T_0/[\gamma_1(1-\beta_1)] = 3T_0$. Backward ($\theta = \pi$): $T_{\mathrm{app}} = T_0/3$. The field therefore appears strongly anisotropic — not invariant.

Solid-angle average: with $\mu = \cos\theta$,
$$\langle D \rangle = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int \frac{d\Omega}{\gamma_1(1-\beta_1\mu)} = \frac{1}{\gamma_1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d\mu}{1-\beta_1\mu}.$$
The integral evaluates as
$$\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{d\mu}{1-\beta_1\mu} = \left[-\frac{1}{\beta_1}\ln(1-\beta_1\mu)\right]_{-1}^{1} = -\frac{1}{\beta_1}\ln(1-\beta_1) + \frac{1}{\beta_1}\ln(1+\beta_1) = \frac{1}{\beta_1}\ln\frac{1+\beta_1}{1-\beta_1} = \frac{2}{\beta_1}\operatorname{atanh}\beta_1.$$
So $\langle D \rangle = \frac{\operatorname{atanh}\beta_1}{\gamma_1\beta_1}$ and
$$\bar{T} = \frac{T_0\,\operatorname{atanh}\beta_1}{\gamma_1\beta_1}.$$
Numerically: $\operatorname{atanh}(0.8) = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1.8}{0.2} = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln 9 = 1.098612$; $\gamma_1\beta_1 = (5/3)(0.8) = 4/3 = 1.333333$;
$$\bar{T} = T_0 \times \frac{1.098612}{1.333333} = T_0 \times 0.823959 \approx 0.8240\,T_0.$$
The angularly averaged appearance of the field is cooler by $1 - 0.823959 = 0.176041$, i.e. $17.6\%$, in qualitative agreement with the "moving system appears cooler" conclusion of [1].

**E. Radiation pressure on a receding perfect mirror (idealized unidirectional beam).** Let a beam of energy density $u_0$ (in the source frame) strike a perfect mirror receding at $\beta_1$ along the beam. The photon arrival rate per unit mirror area is reduced by the factor $(1-\beta_1)$; each photon of energy $h\nu$ reflects with frequency redshifted by the mirror Doppler factor $\nu_r/\nu = \frac{1-\beta_1}{1+\beta_1}$. Momentum transfer per photon:
$$\Delta p = \frac{h\nu}{c}\left(1 - \frac{\nu_r}{\nu}\right) = \frac{h\nu}{c}\left(1 - \frac{1-\beta_1}{1+\beta_1}\right) = \frac{h\nu}{c}\cdot\frac{(1+\beta_1)-(1-\beta_1)}{1+\beta_1} = \frac{h\nu}{c}\cdot\frac{2\beta_1}{1+\beta_1}.$$
Force per area:
$$\frac{F}{A} = u_0(1-\beta_1)\cdot\frac{2\beta_1}{1+\beta_1} = u_0\cdot\frac{2\beta_1(1-\beta_1)}{1+\beta_1}.$$
At $\beta_1 = 0.8$: numerator $2(0.8)(0.2) = 0.32$; denominator $1.8$;
$$\frac{F_1}{A} = u_0 \times \frac{0.32}{1.8} = u_0 \times 0.177778 \approx 0.1778\,u_0.$$
For a static mirror ($\beta = 0$), $F_0/A = 2u_0$. The appearance of the beam's mechanical effect is therefore suppressed by a factor $0.177778/2 = 0.088889 \approx 0.0889$ — an $89.1\%$ reduction. This idealized calculation is consistent in spirit with the existence of velocity-dependent light–matter momentum transfer for moving objects demonstrated in [3]; we do not claim numerical equality with any full drag computation on an isotropic field.

**F. Jüttner tail suppression.** The simulation of [1] shows a relativistic gas obeys Jüttner's speed distribution, whose Boltzmann weight for a particle of Lorentz factor $\gamma_{\mathrm{p}}$ is $\exp[-(\gamma_{\mathrm{p}}-1)mc^2/(k_B T_0)]$. For a cold gas with $k_B T_0 = 0.1\,mc^2$, the relative weight of particles at $\gamma_{\mathrm{p}} = 2$ versus $\gamma_{\mathrm{p}} = 1$ is
$$\frac{w(2)}{w(1)} = \exp\left[-\frac{(2-1)mc^2}{0.1\,mc^2}\right] = e^{-10} = 4.5400\times10^{-5}.$$
A moving observer fitting a temperature from such a distribution samples Doppler-shifted energies; the fitted value inherits the frame dependence demonstrated in part D.

**G. Speed for $D = 10$.** For longitudinal approach, $D = \sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}}$. Setting $D = 10$:
$$100 = \frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta} \Rightarrow 100 - 100\beta = 1 + \beta \Rightarrow 99 = 101\beta \Rightarrow \beta = \frac{99}{101} \approx 0.980198.$$

### 4.2 Re-entry channel at $\beta = 0.9$

**H.** We model a re-entry event (a body entering a planetary atmosphere at high speed, dissipating kinetic energy as heat and radiation) in its rest frame $S$ by three free model parameters: emitted temperature $T_0$, proper duration $\Delta\tau$, and radiating area $A$. Inputs (all assumptions): $\beta = 0.9$, $T_0 = 2000\ \mathrm{K}$, $\Delta\tau = 100\ \mathrm{s}$.

**Step 1: Lorentz factor.**
$$\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.9^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.81}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.19}} = \frac{1}{0.43589} \approx 2.294157.$$

**Step 2: Doppler factor, approaching ($\theta = 0$).**
$$D_{\mathrm{app}} = \frac{1}{\gamma(1 - \beta)} = \frac{1}{2.294157 \times 0.1} = \frac{1}{0.2294157} \approx 4.358899.$$

**Step 3: Doppler factor, receding ($\theta = \pi$).**
$$D_{\mathrm{rec}} = \frac{1}{\gamma(1 + \beta)} = \frac{1}{2.294157 \times 1.9} = \frac{1}{4.358899} \approx 0.229416.$$
Note the exact duality $D_{\mathrm{app}} = 1/D_{\mathrm{rec}}$, which follows because $\gamma^2(1-\beta)(1+\beta) = \gamma^2(1-\beta^2) = 1$.
