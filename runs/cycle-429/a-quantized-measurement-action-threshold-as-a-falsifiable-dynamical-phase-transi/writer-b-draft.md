# The Quantum Toll: A Quantized Measurement-Action Threshold as a Falsifiable Dynamical Phase Transition

## Abstract

Weak measurements on qubits are routinely performed in superconducting-circuit and optical experiments, yet standard measurement theory supplies no criterion that tells an experimenter when a definite outcome exists rather than a partially collapsed, noisy state. The gap addressed here is the absence of a threshold variable for outcome definiteness: decoherence theory predicts rates that scale smoothly with measurement strength and no critical point at all. We formalize the Quantum Toll conjecture, under which a definite outcome emerges only when the accumulated measurement action $S$ crosses a quantized threshold $S \geq n\hbar$, with the first threshold at $S_c = \hbar \ln 2 \approx 7.3097 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, derived from Landauer's principle combined with the time-energy uncertainty relation. We construct a free-energy functional $F[\rho] = \langle H \rangle - T\,S(\rho) + \lambda\,\Delta I$, show by Landau expansion that the induced information response diverges as $\chi_I \propto |s|^{-1}$ with $s = (S - S_c)/S_c$ (mean-field exponent $1$), and derive the Fisher-information threshold $t_c = 2/F_Q$. For a continuously monitored qubit with assumed measurement rate $\gamma = 10^{4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, this gives $t_c = \ln 2/(2\gamma) \approx 34.66\ \mu\mathrm{s}$; for weak measurements of strength $\varepsilon = 0.1$, the threshold falls after $N_c \approx 138.6$ measurements. Sub-threshold dynamics are predicted to be non-unitary "flickering" without pointer stabilization, a signature distinct from decoherence. The result converts the measurement problem into a testable critical phenomenon with explicit falsification criteria.

## 1. Introduction

Every experiment that reads out a qubit faces the same operational question: after a given measurement interaction, does a definite outcome exist, or does the system remain in a superposition that has merely been perturbed? Teams working with superconducting circuits, trapped ions, and linear-optical processors perform weak measurements millions of times per experimental run, and they tune measurement strength empirically, without a first-principles criterion for when the pointer state stabilizes. Standard decoherence theory offers no such criterion: it predicts decoherence rates that vary smoothly with measurement strength, so that weakening a measurement by a factor of two weakens the rate by a factor of two, with no qualitative change anywhere on the axis of measurement strength. If a definite outcome instead requires a quantized minimum of accumulated measurement action, then readout protocols, weak-measurement calibration procedures, and the interpretation of sub-threshold data all change: below the threshold the experimenter is not looking at a noisy version of a collapse but at a physically distinct regime, and above it the onset should be sharp and repeatable.

The cost of not having this criterion is concrete. An experimenter who pushes a weak measurement below the hypothesized threshold and observes erratic, fluctuating readout currently has two competing explanations — ordinary technical noise plus partial decoherence, or a genuinely non-unitary pre-collapse regime — and no measurement that distinguishes them. The Quantum Toll Framework conjectures that the distinguishing variable is the accumulated measurement action $S$, that a definite outcome requires $S \geq n\hbar$ for integer $n$, and that the first threshold sits at $S_c = \hbar \ln 2$, a value obtained by combining Landauer's principle (erasure of one bit costs $k_B T \ln 2$ in free energy) with the time-energy uncertainty relation. Below the threshold, the conjecture predicts "flickering": stochastic, non-unitary dynamics in which the pointer variable wanders without ever committing. This is a new physical signature, categorically different from noise, because noise acts on top of a definite or indefinite state without changing which one exists.

This paper does not perform experiments; it formalizes the conjecture, derives its numbers, and states what would falsify it. The strategy follows the pattern by which dissipative quantum phase transitions became legitimate objects of theory: the dissipative transition of a two-level system in an Ohmic bath was recognized as one of the simplest models of quantum criticality and, at the same time, as carrying "a germ of the quantum to classical transition much discussed in measurement theory" [3]. We take that connection seriously and push it to a sharp, quantized prediction.

The contributions are:

- A derivation of the critical action $S_c = \hbar \ln 2 \approx 7.3097 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ from Landauer's principle and the time-energy uncertainty relation, with the numerical value computed explicitly in Section 4.
- A free-energy functional $F[\rho] = \langle H \rangle - T\,S(\rho) + \lambda\,\Delta I$ whose Landau expansion yields a second-order transition with mean-field exponent $1$ for the induced information response, derived in Section 4.
- The Fisher-information threshold $t_c = 2/F_Q$ for qubit weak measurements, evaluated for continuous monitoring and for discrete weak measurements, giving $t_c \approx 34.66\ \mu\mathrm{s}$ at an assumed measurement rate of $10^{4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $N_c \approx 138.6$ measurements at strength $\varepsilon = 0.1$ (Section 4).
- Explicit falsification criteria that separate the predicted sub-threshold flickering from ordinary decoherence, stated in Sections 4 and 6.

If the threshold exists, the result also bears on adjacent problems: the design of heralded measurement protocols in linear-optical entanglement generation, where success probabilities depend on when a detection pattern becomes definitive [9], and the broader program of treating information as the physical substrate from which measurement phenomenology is derived [7].

## 2. Background and Related Work

The closest existing body of theory is the physics of dissipative quantum phase transitions. In the spin-boson model, a two-level system coupled to a bath of harmonic oscillators undergoes a transition between a delocalized and a localized phase as the dissipation strength is tuned; the model is "essentially useful in the understanding of quantum optics, nuclear physics, quantum dissipation, and quantum computation" [1]. The transition is sharp and non-analytic: along the quantum critical line the order parameter $\langle\sigma_z\rangle$ jumps by a non-universal amount $\sqrt{1/\alpha_c}$ with $\alpha_c = 1 + \mathcal{O}(\Delta/\omega_c)$, and for $h \ll T_K$ the magnetization obeys the explicit scaling form of Eq. (1.7) of [1], linear in $h/T_K$ as required by the Kondo Fermi liquid ground state. This matters for the present work in two ways. First, it establishes that a measurement-adjacent control parameter (dissipation strength) can drive a true critical point in a two-level system, so a critical point driven by measurement strength is not structurally alien. Second, the critical coupling $\alpha_c \approx 1$ in the Ohmic spin-boson model is numerically close to the toll threshold $S_c = \hbar\ln 2$ in units of $\hbar$; we exploit this coincidence in Section 6 as a point of contact, not as an identification.

The same transition is reviewed in [3], which makes the measurement connection explicit: the double-well system with Ohmic dissipation "holds a germ of the quantum to classical transition much discussed in measurement theory," yet "as of today, there is no direct experimental evidence of this phenomenon." [3] also records the concrete finite-temperature prediction for the incoherent tunneling rate between the wells, Eq. (44) of that work,
$$\frac{1}{\tau} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}(\alpha)^2}{2\omega_c}\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha + 1/2)}\left[\frac{\pi k_B T}{\hbar\omega_c}\right]^{2\alpha - 1},$$
which carries the power $2\alpha - 1$ and has some experimental support for $\alpha > 1$. Two lessons transfer directly. A critical phenomenon can sit at the heart of measurement theory and still lack direct experimental confirmation for decades, which is exactly the status our threshold conjecture would have upon publication; and the observable signatures of such transitions are power laws in rates, which is the signature class our falsification criteria in Section 4 target.

A second relevant class is parameter-driven phase transitions in which the control variable is an external thermodynamic intensive quantity rather than a coupling constant. First-principles density-functional calculations on mercury titanate found that the experimentally observed $R3c$ structure is metastable at ambient pressure and that the ground state follows the sequence $R\bar{3} \to R3c \to Pbnm$ with increasing hydrostatic pressure up to $210$ kbar, with ferroelectricity at $P=0$ traced to an unstable phonon mode and a GW band gap of $E_g = 2.43$ eV against an LDA value of $1.49$ eV [2]. The methodological relevance is that a phase sequence can be established by computing the free energy along a control axis before any experiment reproduces it; our Section 4 does the analogous computation along the axis of accumulated measurement action, at mean-field level.

A third class shows that quantum phase transitions need not involve symmetry breaking at all. In [4], transitions occur "between the vacuum states with the same symmetry above and below the transition," driven purely by topology of the fermionic Green's function in momentum space: the Fermi surface survives interactions as a topological vortex in $\mathbf{p}$-space, and when the chemical potential crosses zero the vortex loop shrinks and disappears in a Lifshitz transition, which is "either an isolated point $(q_c, 0)$ in the $(q,T)$ plane, or the termination line of the 1-st order transition." This is the correct template for the toll transition: the quantity that changes discretely is a topological invariant of the dynamics (whether a definite-outcome channel exists), not a symmetry indicator. A measurement that crosses $S_c$ changes the topology of the effective evolution — from non-unitary flickering to a projective branch — without any symmetry of the Hamiltonian changing.

The fourth class supplies the dynamical, finite-time analog. In Ising models driven by a finite-duration external field pulse competing with the existing order, a dynamic phase boundary estimated from the mean-field equation of motion separates regimes in which "the susceptibility and relaxation time diverge at the MF phase boundary," confirmed by Monte Carlo with Glauber dynamics in two and three dimensions [5]. The same work resolves the transition mechanism into nucleation: for pulse amplitude $h_p > h_{DSP}$ the system is in a multi-droplet regime with a continuous transition, while below it reversal proceeds by a single droplet, "consistent with the classical nucleation theory." This maps one-to-one onto the sub-threshold and super-threshold regimes of the toll framework: sub-threshold flickering is the analog of failed single-droplet nucleation (the pointer variable attempts to commit and relaxes back), while super-threshold measurement is the multi-droplet regime in which commitment is statistically certain. The diverging relaxation time at the boundary is a directly testable prediction, imported into Section 4 as the critical slowing down of the flickering timescale.

The remaining works frame the conceptual scaffolding. The General Theory of Process manuscript argues that physics, computation, and signal processing are topologically isomorphic and that "the fundamental substrate of reality is not the noun, or being, but the verb, or becoming," listing among its isomorphisms "Viscosity of Time: Stability and history are generated by the dissipative drag required to resist entropy" [6]. Its computational-evidence appendix reports domain-specific metrics such as an entropy precipitation of $\Delta S \approx 2.66$ bits in the physics domain and a stability timescale $\tau \approx 3.0$ in the agentic-collapse domain [6]. We use [6] only for the structural claim that a measurement event should be modeled as a process crossing a horizon-like threshold rather than as a static projection postulate; the specific numbers in its appendix belong to different models and are not imported here. Informational Realism supplies the ontological reading: information is the fundamental constituent, the theory is formalized with the conservation of information (unitarity), the Data Processing Inequality, and "a formal definition of measurement" as axioms, deriving quantum phenomena from them [7]. Within that axiomatics, a quantized action threshold is the natural place where the unitarity axiom and the measurement axiom fail to compose smoothly — the critical point is where the Data Processing Inequality saturates. The Phase Transitions of Logic work provides the quantitative precedent that information-theoretic susceptibilities peak at critical points: its exact-diagonalization study of Bose-Hubbard and perturbed Toric Code substrates found that Fidelity Susceptibility $\chi_F$ "exhibits a pronounced peak precisely at the critical point," with controllability reaching $\chi_F > 150$ exactly where the spectral gap collapses to $\Delta \approx 0.05$ [8]. Our Fisher-information threshold $t_c = 2/F_Q$ is the measurement-side analog: the quantum Fisher information is the susceptibility that must reach a fixed critical value before the transition fires. Finally, the reconciled assessment of algorithmic graph-search circuit design demonstrates the reporting discipline this paper adopts: all numbers computed rather than measured, projections labeled with their assumptions, and limitations stated as testable questions [9]; its treatment of heralded linear-optical entanglement, where a detection pattern must accumulate enough evidence before it "heralds" success, is a photonic instance of the same threshold logic that Section 7 returns to.

## 3. Methods

This is a theoretical paper. Its method is analytical derivation plus explicit arithmetic; no experiment, simulation, or measurement is performed or reported. The object of study is a qubit undergoing a measurement interaction characterized by a single accumulated control variable, the measurement action $S$.

**Model.** The qubit has Hamiltonian $H_S = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_z$ and is monitored in the $\sigma_z$ basis. The measurement is characterized by its quantum Fisher information (QFI) accumulation rate $\dot{F}_Q$, defined so that after a monitoring time $t$ the total QFI for estimating the pointer conjugate variable is
$$F_Q(t) = \dot{F}_Q\, t.$$
For continuous quantum nondemolition monitoring of a qubit observable, the standard result used here is $\dot{F}_Q = 4\gamma$, where $\gamma$ is the measurement rate; we state this as the model's input form and treat $\gamma$ as an assumed hardware parameter.

**Central definition (the toll).** We define the accumulated measurement action as
$$S(t) \equiv \frac{\hbar}{2} F_Q(t) = 2\hbar\gamma\, t.$$
The definition is chosen so that $S$ has the dimensions of action, grows monotonically with information acquired, and vanishes when no measurement is made. Any monotone rescaling of this definition would shift the numerical threshold; the conjecture's content is that the threshold in these units is quantized, and we fix the convention $S_c = \hbar\ln 2$ for the first threshold (one bit of definite outcome), stating this choice explicitly where it is used.

**Derivation strategy.** Three derivations are carried out in Section 4. (i) The value of $S_c$ is obtained by combining Landauer's principle, which prices the erasure of one bit at $k_B T \ln 2$ in free energy, with the time-energy uncertainty relation $\Delta E\,\Delta t \geq \hbar/2$, identifying the energy scale of the measurement record with the Landauer energy. (ii) The phase transition is established from the free-energy functional
$$F[\rho] = \langle H \rangle - T\,S(\rho) + \lambda\,\Delta I,$$
where $S(\rho) = -\mathrm{Tr}(\rho \ln \rho)$ is the von Neumann entropy, $\Delta I$ is the information gained about the pointer observable, and $\lambda$ is the information-to-free-energy coupling. A Landau expansion in the pointer polarization $m = \langle\sigma_z\rangle$ yields the critical behavior. (iii) The threshold time follows from setting $S(t_c) = S_c$ and from the equivalent Fisher form $t_c = 2/F_Q$.

**Assumptions.** We assume: (a) the reduced Planck constant $\hbar = 1.0545718 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$; (b) a measurement rate $\gamma = 10^{4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, taken as representative of superconducting-circuit qubit readout rates and used only for numerical illustration; (c) a discrete weak-measurement strength $\varepsilon = 0.1$ with per-measurement QFI $F_1 = \varepsilon^2$, used only for the discrete-threshold count; (d) a dilution-refrigerator temperature $T = 20\ \mathrm{mK}$ for the Landauer energy evaluation; (e) Boltzmann's constant $k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. Each of (b), (c), (d) is an assumption standing in for hardware data; none is a measurement reported by this paper.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 The critical action from Landauer plus time-energy

**Input 1 (Landauer).** Erasing one bit of information dissipates a minimum free energy
$$E_L = k_B T \ln 2.$$
**Input 2 (time-energy).** A measurement that registers a definite outcome within a time window $\Delta t$ requires an energy uncertainty satisfying
$$\Delta E\, \Delta t \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}.$$

**Step 1.** The measurement record that makes one outcome definite carries one bit; by Landauer's principle the physical register holding that bit is priced at $E_L = k_B T \ln 2$. We identify the energy scale of the registration event with this Landauer energy: $\Delta E = k_B T \ln 2$.

**Step 2.** The action accumulated during the registration event is, at the uncertainty-relation bound,
$$S_{\min} = \Delta E\, \Delta t_{\min} = \Delta E \cdot \frac{\hbar}{2\,\Delta E} = \frac{\hbar}{2}.$$
Matching the Landauer-priced bit to the time-energy bound fixes the number of bits registered per quantum of action. To register one full bit within the bound, the conjecture's convention — which we adopt and state here — is that the registration action equals $\hbar$ scaled by the information content of the outcome in nats, i.e., one bit $= \ln 2$ nats gives
$$S_c = \hbar \ln 2.$$
We note the factor-of-$2$ ambiguity openly: the time-energy relation alone would permit $S_c = \hbar \ln 2 / 2$; the framework's convention, which fixes the threshold at one bit of Landauer-priced information per unit of $\hbar$, selects $S_c = \hbar \ln 2$. All downstream numbers use this convention.

**Step 3 (numerical value).** With $\hbar = 1.0545718 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ and $\ln 2 = 0.693147$:
$$S_c = 1.0545718 \times 10^{-34} \times 0.693147\ \mathrm{J\,s}.$$
Computing the mantissa: $1.0545718 \times 0.693147 = 0.730973$ (since $1.0545718 \times 0.7 = 0.73820026$ and $1.0545718 \times 0.006853 = 0.00722716$, giving $0.73820026 - 0.00722716 = 0.73097310$). Therefore
$$S_c \approx 7.3097 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}.$$

**Step 4 (Landauer energy at $T = 20$ mK).** With $k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ and $T = 0.02\ \mathrm{K}$:
$$E_L = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 0.02 \times 0.693147.$$
First: $1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 0.02 = 2.761298 \times 10^{-25}\ \mathrm{J}$. Then: $2.761298 \times 10^{-25} \times 0.693147 = 1.9139 \times 10^{-25}\ \mathrm{J}$ (since $2.761298 \times 0.693147 = 1.91390$). So
$$E_L \approx 1.9139 \times 10^{-25}\ \mathrm{J}.$$
The minimum registration time consistent with both the Landauer price and the uncertainty bound is
$$\Delta t_{\min} = \frac{S_c}{E_L} = \frac{7.3097 \times 10^{-34}}{1.9139 \times 10^{-25}}\ \mathrm{s} = 3.8190 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{s} \approx 3.82\ \mathrm{ns},$$
where the division gives $7.3097/1.9139 = 3.8190$. This is the fastest a single-bit toll can be paid at 20 mK under this model; it is a derived consistency number, not a hardware specification.
