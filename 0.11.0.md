## 0. What This Document Is

This is the capstone of the Word Cross-Ratio project — a retrospective that traces the full arc from its origin as a casual inquiry into word frequencies to its endpoint as a bridge between linguistic information theory and the mathematical cross-ratio tradition. It documents what was genuinely contributed, what remains limited by synthetic data, and what someone with access to real corpora and typological databases could do next.

---

## 1. The Arc: Three Phases in One Day

The project transformed three times in a single session, each phase discarding what didn't work and building on what did.

### Phase 1: Sapir-Whorf (0.1.md — 0.4.md)

**Original question:** "Does the frequency with which a Whorfian category is obligatorily marked predict the magnitude of cognitive effects?"

**What was done:**
- Bayesian MCMC simulation of 22 languages
- Zipf exponent estimation, crossed-effects hierarchical model
- Meta-regression: frequency load $\to$ effect size

**What was found:** A null result ($\beta_1 = -0.020$, HDI includes zero). The meta-regression detected no relationship between frequency load and effect size.

**What was wrong:**
1. The null result was **predetermined**: synthetic effect sizes were generated independently of frequency loads, guaranteeing no correlation would be found.
2. The question itself was **ill-posed**: it confused symptom (frequency) with cause (obligatoriness) and violated Zipf's own logic (high frequency = low information content, the opposite of what the hypothesis needed).
3. Effect sizes from different experimental paradigms (source memory vs. object categorization) were treated as commensurable.

**What survived:** The **measurement apparatus** — the Python pipeline for generating frequency distributions, fitting Bayesian models, and estimating category loads — was methodologically sound and worth repurposing.

### Phase 2: Information Architecture (0.5.0.md — 0.6.0.md)

**Reframed question:** "What mandatory information do different languages force into the communication channel, and what does this reveal about the design space of possible human languages?"

**Theoretical foundation:** Roman Jakobson ("languages differ in what they *must* convey"), Claude Shannon (information theory), Paul Grice (cooperative norms), Joseph Greenberg (implicational universals).

**What was done:**
- Shannon entropy computed for 22 languages
- Mandatory information loads classified into 4 types (epistemic, ontological, categorical, spatial)
- PCA design space mapping
- Compression-tax trade-off tested
- Gricean surplus computed

**What was found:**
1. **Entropy gradient:** Isolating (6.48 bits/word) $\to$ polysynthetic (6.80 bits/word) — clear, monotonic, predicted.
2. **Compression-tax trade-off:** $r(H, L_{\text{total}}) = -0.48$ — richer morphology substitutes for explicit category marking.
3. **Mutual exclusion:** Zero languages had both obligatory epistemic AND obligatory ontological marking — evidence for a universal upper bound on mandatory information.
4. **Design space:** PCA (70.6% variance, PC1+PC2) revealed two empty quadrants — genuine typological constraints.
5. **Gricean surplus:** German (4.8%), Arabic (4.6%), Russian (4.3%) most over-informative.

**What changed:** The project found its real contribution — not about cognition, but about **communication architecture**.

### Phase 3: Deepening, Extension, Bridge (0.7.0.py — 0.10.0.md)

**Path A — Mutual Exclusion Deepening:**
- Expanded from 4 to 8 mandatory domains (adding tense, aspect, number, definiteness)
- 10 of 28 pairwise combinations empty ($p < 0.0001$ by permutation test)
- Four mutually exclusive mandatory clusters identified: reference-tracking, source-tracking, categorical-judgment, spatial-coordinate
- Epistemic marking anti-correlated with every other domain type

**Path B — Scientific Register Comparison:**
- Generated scientific register profiles for 8 languages
- Epistemic load converges universally at $\sim$2.5% (8.8$\times$ increase from baseline)
- Entropy does NOT converge — morphological type continues to constrain information packaging
- Register convention is additive, not competitive with grammatical obligatoriness

**Path C — Cross-Project Synthesis:**
- Shannon entropy identified as an **invariant under recoding** — structurally analogous to the geometric cross-ratio being invariant under projective transformations
- Mutual exclusion principle identified as a **linguistic no-go theorem**
- Design space boundaries identified as **linguistic phase boundaries**
- Proposal: re-include Word Cross-Ratio in the Cross-Ratio Convergence synthesis

## 3. Honest Limitations

These are not caveats to be buried in a footnote. They are the boundaries of what this project can claim.

### 3.1 Synthetic Data

**Every numerical finding in this project depends on synthetic frequency data and LLM-informed domain assignments.** The entropy gradient, the mutual exclusion pattern, the compression-tax trade-off — all are as valid as the synthetic data they're computed from. The synthetic data was generated from typologically informed priors, so the findings reflect *what we expected to find* encoded in the generation parameters, not what real corpora would reveal.

**What this means:** The findings are **internally consistent demonstrations of what the methodology can detect**, not **externally validated discoveries about real languages**. The methodology is real; the specific numbers are illustrative.

### 3.2 The "Word" Problem

Shannon entropy per word-form is not a cross-linguistically valid measure. "Word" is a writing-system artifact that varies radically across languages:
- Mandarin: characters, not words
- Japanese: mixed kanji + kana
- Greenlandic: whole clauses as single orthographic words

The per-morpheme entropy analysis (L9 in LEARNINGS.md) partially addresses this, but the estimated morphemes-per-word values (1.1 for isolating, 4.0 for polysynthetic) are rough approximations, not measured quantities.

### 3.3 22-Language Sample

22 languages out of $\sim$7,000. The sample is biased toward:
- Major national languages (no minority languages)
- Eurasian languages (no Australian, Papuan, most African families)
- Written languages (no primarily oral languages)
- Well-documented languages (no underdescribed languages)

A finding of "zero languages with both epistemic and ontological obligation" in an $n = 22$ sample does not mean such languages don't exist. It means they're absent from our sample and from the LLM's parametric knowledge.

### 3.4 LLM as Domain Expert

All typological assignments — which domains are obligatory in which languages, what the percentage loads are — came from LLM parametric knowledge. For well-documented languages (English, German, Japanese), this knowledge is reasonably reliable. For underrepresented languages (Quechua, Tibetan, Navajo, Greenlandic), it is shallow at best and invented at worst.

### 3.5 No Real Validation

The project never tested its findings against:
- The World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS)
- Real parallel corpora
- Published psycholinguistic meta-analyses with verified effect sizes
- Native speaker judgments

**The project is a self-consistent simulation, not a verified study of real languages.**

## 4. The Methodology as Reusable Template

Despite the synthetic data limitation, the *methodology* is genuinely reusable. Here is what someone with real data would need to replicate or extend this work:

### 4.1 Required Data

| Data | Source | What it replaces |
|:-----|:-------|:-----------------|
| Word-frequency vectors (top 200, per language) | Real corpora (Wikipedia, CommonCrawl, BNC, etc.) | Synthetic frequency profiles from 0.3.py |
| Mandatory category assignments (per language, per domain) | WALS, grammar descriptions, native speaker consultation | LLM-informed domain assignments from 0.7.0.py |
| Effect sizes (if revisiting Sapir-Whorf) | Published meta-analyses with verified $d$ and SE | Synthetic effect sizes from 0.3.py |

### 4.2 Pipeline Architecture

The analysis pipeline (0.5.0.py, 0.7.0.py) is modular — each phase can be run independently:

```
0. Data Loading → per_lang_results (frequency vectors + typology)
1. Shannon Entropy → entropy_results (H, H_norm, N_eff, H/morpheme)
2. Mandatory Architecture → mandatory (4-type load classification)
3. Gricean Surplus → surplus (over-informativeness per language)
4. Compression-Tax Trade-Off → tradeoff (r(H, load), per-type correlations)
5. Greenbergian Correlations → greenberg (load correlation matrix, cluster tests)
6. Design Space Mapping → design_space (PCA, empty region analysis)
7. Mutual Exclusion → pairwise + permutation test (8-domain version)
8. Scientific Register → register comparison (optional, requires register data)
```

Each phase produces machine-readable JSON output. Phases are independent — you can swap in real frequency data at Phase 1 without changing anything downstream.

### 4.3 Reproducibility

- Seed = 42 (all random processes are seeded)
- MCMC: 4 chains, 2000 burn-in, 5000 samples, $\hat{R} < 1.05$, ESS $> 400$
- All code in self-contained Python files using only NumPy
- Versioned file naming (0.N.py, 0.N_results.json, 0.N.md) preserves provenance

## 5. Roadmap for Real-Data Validation

If someone with access to WALS, real corpora, or psycholinguistic databases wanted to take this further:

### Step 1: Validate the Mutual Exclusion Principle

**What to do:** Cross-reference our 8-domain obligatory assignments against WALS features (e.g., Feature 77A for evidentiality, 30A for gender, 37A for definite articles, 33A for plural marking, 66A for past tense, 65A for perfective/imperfective aspect). Test whether the zero-intersection pairs hold in the full WALS sample ($\sim$2,500 languages).

**What this would prove or disprove:** Whether mutual exclusion is a genuine linguistic universal or an artifact of our small, LLM-informed sample.

### Step 2: Compute Real Entropy Gradients

**What to do:** For 5–10 well-resourced languages (English, German, French, Russian, Mandarin, Japanese, Turkish, Finnish, Korean, Arabic), extract top-200 word-form frequencies from real corpora (Wikipedia dumps, CommonCrawl, or national corpora). Recompute Shannon entropy and compare to our synthetic estimates.

**What this would prove or disprove:** Whether the synthetic entropy gradient (isolating 6.48 $\to$ polysynthetic 6.80 bits) matches reality, and whether "word" is salvageable as a cross-linguistic unit.

### Step 3: Test the Compression-Tax Trade-Off with Real Data

**What to do:** Using the real entropy values from Step 2 and the WALS-validated mandatory assignments from Step 1, recompute the correlation between entropy and total mandatory load.

**What this would prove or disprove:** Whether $r = -0.48$ holds in real data or is an artifact of how we generated synthetic frequencies.

### Step 4: Real Scientific Register Comparison

**What to do:** For 3–5 languages, extract top-200 word frequencies from parallel scientific corpora (e.g., arXiv vs. Wikipedia for English; comparable sources for other languages). Recompute entropy and mandatory loads for the scientific register and compare to general register.

**What this would prove or disprove:** Whether epistemic load really converges at $\sim$2.5% across languages in scientific writing, and whether entropy convergence is genuinely absent.

## 6. Open Questions

These are questions the project raised but could not answer:

1. **Is mutual exclusion a processing constraint or a historical accident?** Do languages avoid combining mandatory clusters because the cognitive load is too high, or because the relevant grammaticalization pathways are mutually exclusive for historical reasons?

2. **What happens at the upper bound?** If the mandatory information budget is $\sim$5% of tokens, what breaks when a language exceeds it? Does comprehension degrade? Does grammaticalization fail? Are there documented cases of languages losing one mandatory category as another becomes obligatory?

3. **Is per-morpheme entropy genuinely cross-linguistically stable?** We estimated morphemes per word from typological averages. Real morpheme counts (from interlinear glossed corpora) would provide a genuine test of whether per-morpheme information density is a linguistic universal.

4. **Does scientific register convergence hold across fields?** We treated "science" as a monolith. Physics, biology, and sociology have very different epistemic norms. Does epistemic convergence vary by field?

5. **Can the mutual exclusion principle be derived from first principles?** Given Shannon's channel capacity theorem and known constraints on human working memory and processing speed, can we predict the maximum mandatory information budget from theory alone, without needing to measure it empirically?

## 8. Closing Assessment

The Word Cross-Ratio project began as a casual inquiry — "what are the most common words in different languages, and what does that tell us about how language shapes worldviews?" It was ill-defined, and its first serious attempt at an answer (the Sapir-Whorf meta-regression) produced a null result that was predetermined by synthetic data independence. A lesser project would have been abandoned at that point.

Instead, an assumption audit revealed that the measurement apparatus was sound but aimed at the wrong target. The reframing — from psychology (Sapir-Whorf) to communication theory (Jakobson/Shannon/Grice/Greenberg) — transformed a null result into a coherent set of findings. The deepening (Paths A+B+C) strengthened the strongest finding (mutual exclusion) and extended the framework to a new domain (scientific register) and a new context (cross-project synthesis).

**The project's genuine contribution is threefold:**

1. **A methodology:** Self-contained, reproducible, Bayesian, LLM-orchestrated — a template for computational linguistics research within the constraints of a chat thread.

2. **A framework:** Language as information architecture — treating languages as communication channels with different mandatory metadata schemas, analysed through Shannon entropy, Gricean surplus, and Greenbergian correlations.

3. **A bridge:** Shannon entropy as an invariant under recoding, connecting linguistic information theory to the mathematical cross-ratio tradition — extending the convergence principle to a new domain.

**The project's genuine limitation is singular:** all findings depend on synthetic data and need real-corpus validation. The methodology is real; the specific numbers are illustrative.

**The project is complete.** It has earned its place in the archive — not as a definitive study of human language, but as a demonstration that the right question, asked of the right data with the right tools, can produce coherent results even within the severe constraints of a single LLM conversation.
