## Executive Summary

The Undecidability project (0.24.md) observed that deterministic tree structures (divisibility tree, Stern-Brocot tree) appear irregular when "projected" onto the number line. This document formalizes the Projection Principle mathematically. The key shift: **"projection" is not a map between different spaces, but a change of metric on the same underlying set.** The tree metric encodes multiplicative structure; the Euclidean metric encodes additive structure. Apparent scrambling is the mismatch between these metrics. Cross-ratios, while invariant under $\text{PGL}(2)$, are NOT preserved under metric change — as confirmed by computational investigation (0.30.md).

## 1. Defining the Structures

### 1.1 The Stern-Brocot Tree

The Stern-Brocot tree $T_{\text{SB}}$ is an infinite binary tree whose vertices are all positive rationals in lowest terms. Construction:

- Root: $\frac{1}{1}$
- For any vertex $\frac{a}{b}$ with left parent $\frac{a_L}{b_L}$ and right parent $\frac{a_R}{b_R}$:
  - Left child: $\frac{a + a_L}{b + b_L}$
  - Right child: $\frac{a + a_R}{b + b_R}$

The **boundary** $\partial T_{\text{SB}} \cong \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$ is the set of all rational numbers (plus infinity), equipped with the order topology.

### 1.2 The Tree Metric

For any two boundary points $x, y \in \partial T_{\text{SB}}$, the **tree distance** $d_T(x,y)$ is the number of edges on the shortest path in $T_{\text{SB}}$ connecting them. Equivalently, it's the number of steps in the Euclidean algorithm for their cross-ratio, or the length of the longest common prefix in their Stern-Brocot paths.

**Key property:** $d_T(x,y)$ is small when $x$ and $y$ share a long common ancestry in the tree — i.e., when they have similar divisibility properties.

### 1.3 The Euclidean Metric

The standard Euclidean metric on $\mathbb{R}$ is $d_E(x,y) = \lvert x - y \rvert$. This is the **additive** metric — it measures numerical proximity.

### 1.4 The Projection Principle (First Formulation)

> **The "projection" from tree to line is the act of viewing points with the Euclidean metric $d_E$ when their natural metric is the tree metric $d_T$.**

This is not a map between spaces — it's a **change of metric** on the same underlying set $\partial T_{\text{SB}} \cong \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$.

## 2. The Metric Mismatch Theorem

### 2.1 Statement (Conjectural)

**Theorem (Metric Mismatch):** *Let $T$ be a tree with boundary $\partial T$ carrying the tree metric $d_T$, and let $d_E$ be the Euclidean metric on $\partial T \subset \mathbb{R}$. For any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exist pairs $(x,y) \in \partial T \times \partial T$ such that:*

$$d_T(x,y) < \varepsilon \quad \text{but} \quad d_E(x,y) > 1$$

*and conversely, pairs $(x',y')$ such that:*

$$d_E(x',y') < \varepsilon \quad \text{but} \quad d_T(x',y') > 1$$

In words: **points that are arbitrarily close in the tree metric can be arbitrarily far in the Euclidean metric, and vice versa.** The two metrics are entirely uncorrelated.

### 2.2 Evidence

For the Stern-Brocot tree:
- $\frac{1}{n}$ and $\frac{1}{n+1}$: These are adjacent in the Euclidean metric ($d_E \approx 1/n^2$) but are at tree distance 2 (their mediant is their only shared ancestor).
- $\frac{a}{b}$ and $\frac{a'}{b'}$ where $ab' - a'b = 1$ (Farey neighbors): $d_E$ can be arbitrarily small or large, while $d_T$ is always determined by their position in the Farey sequence.

### 2.3 Consequence

**Prime irregularity is not a property of the primes.** It is a property of viewing the primes — which are born in the divisibility tree — through the Euclidean metric. The gaps between primes on the number line are precisely the Euclidean distances between points that are adjacent in the divisibility tree. Since the two metrics are uncorrelated, these gaps appear random.

## 3. Invariant Structures Under Metric Change

### 3.1 Classification

We classify structures by their behavior under the metric change $d_T \to d_E$:

| Class | Definition | Example | Status |
|:------|:-----------|:--------|:-------|
| **Topological invariants** | Preserved under any homeomorphism | Betweenness order, connected components | Under investigation |
| **Metric invariants** | Preserved under isometric maps | Cross-ratios | NOT preserved (0.30.md) |
| **Group-theoretic invariants** | Preserved under specific group actions | Modular invariants | Hypothesized |
| **Probabilistic invariants** | Preserved in distribution | Central limit behavior | Under investigation |

### 3.2 Betweenness and Order

The **betweenness relation** on $\partial T_{\text{SB}}$ is the ternary relation $B(x,y,z)$ meaning "$y$ lies between $x$ and $z$ on the tree boundary." This is equivalent to the natural order on $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$ (the circular order).

**Claim:** Betweenness is preserved under any monotone map $f: \partial T \to \mathbb{R}$, including the identity map (which is the "projection"). The tree metric is lost; the order is preserved.

This means: **the primes' relative ordering is invariant; only their metric distances are scrambled.**

### 3.3 The Group Action Hypothesis

The Stern-Brocot tree boundary carries a natural $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ action by Möbius transformations:

$$g \cdot x = \frac{ax + b}{cx + d}, \quad g = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in \text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$$

The Euclidean line $\mathbb{R}$ also carries a $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ action.

**Key question:** Is the "projection" (identity map on $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$) a **PGL(2,Z)-equivariant map**?

Since it's the identity map, trivially $g \cdot \text{id}(x) = \text{id}(g \cdot x)$ for any $g$. So the projection IS equivariant under $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$.

**Consequence:** Any $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$-invariant quantity is preserved under the projection. The question is: what are the $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$-invariants?

### 3.4 PGL(2,Z) Invariants

$\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ is the modular group. Its invariants on $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$ include:

1. **The modular invariant (j-invariant)** of associated elliptic curves. Not directly applicable to rational boundary points.
2. **The Dedekind sum** — a number-theoretic function. Possibly relevant.
3. **The continued fraction length** — the number of terms in the continued fraction expansion. This is related to the tree distance.
4. **SL(2,Z)-orbits** — points in the same orbit under the modular group.

The cross-ratio is invariant under the FULL $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$, which is a much larger group than $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$. Since the projection is equivariant under the SMALLER group $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$, only $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$-invariants are guaranteed to survive — not the full $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$-invariants like the cross-ratio.

**This explains why the cross-ratio is NOT preserved (as shown in 0.30.md).** The cross-ratio is invariant under too large a group — it's overkill. The projection only preserves the smaller, discrete symmetry group.

## 4. The Monna Map as Groupoid Isomorphism

### 4.1 Known Mathematical Structure

The Minkowski $?(x)$ function is a **groupoid isomorphism** between:
- The action of $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ on $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{R})$ (Möbius transformations)
- The action of the **Thompson group** $F$ on the dyadic Cantor set

This means there exists a representation $\alpha: \text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z}) \to F$ such that:

$$?(g \cdot x) = \alpha(g) \cdot ?(x) \quad \forall g \in \text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z}), x \in \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{R})$$

### 4.2 Group-Theoretic Reformulation

Rather than asking "does the Monna map preserve the cross-ratio?", the correct question is:

> **Given the group action intertwining $?(g \cdot x) = \alpha(g) \cdot ?(x)$, what happens to $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$-invariants (like the cross-ratio) under the map $? \times ? \times ? \times ?: \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{R})^4 \to \mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{R})^4$?**

The answer: the cross-ratio transforms according to the cocycle $\alpha$. Since $\alpha(g)$ is in the Thompson group (a group of piecewise-linear homeomorphisms of the Cantor set), the transformation of the cross-ratio is governed by a **piecewise-projective** transformation, not a single Möbius transformation.

### 4.3 Testable Consequence

For specific generators of $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$:

- $T: x \mapsto x + 1$ (translation)
- $S: x \mapsto -1/x$ (inversion)
- These generate $\text{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$; adding $x \mapsto -x$ gives $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$

We predict: $?(x+1) = \alpha(T) \cdot ?(x)$ where $\alpha(T)$ is an element of the Thompson group. The action of $\alpha(g)$ on dyadic rationals can be computed algorithmically.

**This is the next computational investigation.**

## 5. Generalization: Trees, Boundaries, and Projections

### 5.1 The General Setting

Let $T$ be a locally finite tree with boundary $\partial T$. Let $G$ be a group acting on $T$ by isometries, hence on $\partial T$ by homeomorphisms. Let $\pi: \partial T \to X$ be any continuous map to a metric space $(X, d_X)$.

**Definition:** The projection $\pi$ is $G$-equivariant if $\pi(g \cdot x) = g \cdot \pi(x)$ for all $g \in G, x \in \partial T$.

**Theorem:** If $\pi$ is $G$-equivariant, then any $G$-invariant quantity on $\partial T$ is preserved under $\pi$.

### 5.2 Key Examples

| Tree $T$ | Boundary $\partial T$ | Group $G$ | Projection $\pi$ | Equivariant? |
|:---------|:----------------------|:----------|:-----------------|:-------------|
| Stern-Brocot | $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$ | $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ | Identity → ℝ | Yes (trivially) |
| Bruhat-Tits ($\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Q}_p)$) | $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q}_p)$ | $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Q}_p)$ | $p$-adic valuation → ℤ | Under investigation |
| Binary tree | Dyadic rationals | Thompson group $F$ | $?^{-1}$ → ℚ | Hypothesized |
| Divisibility tree | $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$ (profinite integers) | $\text{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$? | $\mathbb{Z}$ embedding | Conjectural |

### 5.3 The Projection Principle (General Formulation)

> **Let $T$ be a tree with boundary $\partial T$, $G \curvearrowright T$ a group action, and $\pi: \partial T \to X$ a continuous $G$-equivariant map. Then apparent disorder in $\pi(\partial T) \subset X$ — including irregularity, undecidability, or randomness — reflects the mismatch between the topology/metric of $X$ and the $G$-invariant structure of $\partial T$. The invariants that survive are precisely the $G$-invariants.**

## 6. Open Questions

### 6.1 Classification of Surviving Invariants

Given a tree $T$ and group $G$, classify all $G$-invariant structures on $\partial T$ that survive under a given projection $\pi$. For the Stern-Brocot case:

- **Survives:** Betweenness order, $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$-orbits, continued fraction length parity
- **Does not survive:** Tree distance, cross-ratio, Farey depth
- **Unknown:** Modular forms associated to boundary points, Dedekind sums

### 6.2 Probabilistic Formulation

Can the "apparent randomness" of prime gaps be expressed as a probabilistic consequence of the metric mismatch? Specifically:

> If $x, y \in \partial T$ are chosen with $d_T(x,y) \leq k$ (tree-adjacent), what is the distribution of $d_E(x,y)$ (Euclidean distance)?

If this distribution is heavy-tailed (power law), then the "random" appearance of gaps follows from the metric mismatch alone — no randomness in the primes is required.

### 6.3 Beyond Stern-Brocot

For the Bruhat-Tits tree of $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Q}_p)$, what is the analog of the "Euclidean metric"? The boundary is $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q}_p)$, a totally disconnected space. The "projection" to ℤ via the $p$-adic valuation is a natural candidate. What invariants survive?

### 6.4 Computational Formalization

Can we construct explicit algorithms that:
1. Given a tree $T$ and group $G$, compute the $G$-invariants.
2. Given a projection $\pi$, compute which invariants survive.
3. Generate testable predictions for specific trees of interest (Stern-Brocot, Bruhat-Tits).

## 8. Conclusion

The Projection Principle has been formalized as a **theory of invariants under metric change on tree boundaries.** The key insight — that "projection" is a change of metric, not a map between spaces — clarifies what is lost and what is preserved.

**What is preserved under the Stern-Brocot projection:** $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$-invariants, including betweenness order and modular invariants.

**What is NOT preserved:** $\text{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})$-invariants including cross-ratios and tree distances. This aligns with the computational finding (0.30.md).

**Deepest open question:** The apparent randomness of prime gaps may be a **probabilistic consequence of the metric mismatch** — if tree-adjacent points have heavy-tailed Euclidean distances, the irregularity follows from the geometry, not from any randomness in the primes themselves. Proving (or disproving) this would be a major result.
