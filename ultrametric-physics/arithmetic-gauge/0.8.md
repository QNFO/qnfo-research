# Prerequisites for Kapustin–Witten Duality

A companion note covering the background needed to approach the 2007 paper by Anton Kapustin and Edward Witten, *Electric-Magnetic Duality and the Geometric Langlands Program*.

---

## 1. Gauge Theory Basics

### Principal Bundles

Let G be a compact Lie group (the **gauge group**). A **principal G-bundle** is a smooth fiber bundle $\pi$: P $\to$ M whose fibers are isomorphic to G and on which G acts freely and transitively from the right. Locally, P looks like U $\times$ G for an open set U ⊂ M, but globally it may be non-trivial — the topology of P encodes topological charges (e.g., instanton number).

A **matter field** is a section of an associated vector bundle: given a representation $\rho$: G $\to$ GL(V), one forms E = P $\times$_$\rho$ V, and a section $\varphi$ ∈ $\Gamma$(E) is the physical field.

### Connection and Curvature

A **connection** on P is a g-valued 1-form A ∈ $\Omega ^1$(P, g) that is G-equivariant and reproduces the Lie algebra generator on vertical vectors. In physics, A is the **gauge potential** (generalizing the electromagnetic vector potential A_$\mu$ to non-abelian groups). In a local trivialization, A is a g-valued 1-form on M.

The **covariant derivative** is D = d + A. Acting on a matter field $\varphi$ in a representation $\rho$, it reads D$\varphi$ = d$\varphi$ + $\rho$(A) ∧ $\varphi$.

The **curvature** (field strength) is the g-valued 2-form

F = dA + A ∧ A = dA + ½[A, A]

where the bracket is the Lie bracket on g. F measures the failure of second covariant derivatives to commute: [D_$\mu$, D_$\nu$] = F_$\mu \nu$. It satisfies the **Bianchi identity**: DF = dF + [A, F] = 0, which is the non-abelian generalization of the homogeneous Maxwell equations.

### Yang–Mills Action

The dynamics of a pure gauge theory on a Riemannian manifold (M, g) are governed by the **Yang–Mills action**:

S_YM[A] = $\int$_M Tr(F ∧ ⋆F) = $\int$_M ½ Tr(F_$\mu \nu$ F^$\mu \nu \sqrt$g d^4x

where ⋆ is the Hodge star operator associated to the metric. Varying this action yields the Yang–Mills equation D ⋆ F = 0, the non-abelian analog of Maxwell's equations with source.

### Gauge Transformations

A gauge transformation is a map h: M $\to$ G. The connection transforms as

A $\to$ h A h$^{-} ^1$ + h dh$^{-} ^1$

and the curvature transforms covariantly: F $\to$ h F h$^{-} ^1$. Physical observables must be gauge-invariant; the simplest are Wilson loops W_R($\gamma$) = Tr_R P exp(∮_$\gamma$ A), the holonomy of A around a closed curve $\gamma$ in a representation R.

## 2. Supersymmetry

### Supercharges and the Superalgebra

Supersymmetry is a symmetry that exchanges bosons (integer-spin fields) with fermions (half-integer-spin fields). The generators are **supercharges** Q_$\alpha$, which are spinorial operators satisfying schematically

{Q, Q̄} ∼ $\gamma$^$\mu$ P_$\mu$

where P_$\mu$ is the translation generator (momentum). Applying a supercharge to a bosonic state produces a fermionic state and vice versa. In four spacetime dimensions, a supersymmetry with N supercharges (counting as Weyl spinors) is denoted N = 1, 2, or 4 supersymmetry.

### N = 4 Super Yang–Mills

N = 4 SYM is the maximally supersymmetric gauge theory in four dimensions, possessing 16 real supercharges (N = 4 Weyl spinors). It is a **conformal field theory**: the $\beta$-function vanishes exactly, so the theory is scale-invariant quantum mechanically. Its field content consists of:

- A gauge field A_$\mu$ (one spin-1 boson)
- Six real scalar fields $\varphi$ⁱ (i = 1, …, 6), transforming in the **6** of the R-symmetry group
- Four Weyl fermions $\psi$_$\alpha$^a (a = 1, …, 4), transforming in the **4** of the R-symmetry group

All fields transform in the adjoint representation of the gauge group G.

### R-Symmetry

The **R-symmetry** is the automorphism group of the supersymmetry algebra that rotates the supercharges among themselves. For N = 4 SYM in four dimensions, the R-symmetry is SU(4) ≅ Spin(6). The six scalars transform as a vector of SO(6) (the **6**), the four fermions as a spinor of SO(6) (the **4**), and the gauge field is an R-symmetry singlet.

### S-Duality

N = 4 SYM exhibits **S-duality**: the theory with gauge group G and complexified coupling $\tau$ = $\theta$/2$\pi$ + 4$\pi$i/g$^2$ is equivalent to the theory with Langlands dual gauge group ^LG and coupling −1/n_𝔤 $\tau$, where n_𝔤 is the ratio of lengths of long and short roots. In particular, the weak-coupling regime of one theory maps to the strong-coupling regime of the other. This duality is the physical origin of the geometric Langlands correspondence in Kapustin–Witten.

## 3. Langlands Dual Groups

### Root Data

Let G be a connected reductive algebraic group over $\mathbb{C}$. Fix a maximal torus T ⊂ G. The **character lattice** X*(T) = Hom(T, $\mathbb{C}$^$\times$) and the **cocharacter lattice** X_*(T) = Hom($\mathbb{C}$^$\times$, T) are dual free abelian groups of rank equal to the rank of G. The **roots** $\Phi$ ⊂ X*(T) are the non-zero weights of the adjoint representation, and the **coroots** $\Phi$^∨ ⊂ X_*(T) are their duals.

The quadruple (X*, $\Phi$, X_*, $\Phi$^∨), together with a perfect pairing ⟨,⟩: X* $\times$ X_* $\to \mathbb{Z}$, constitutes the **root datum** of G. A fundamental theorem states that a reductive group over an algebraically closed field is classified up to isomorphism by its root datum.

### The Langlands Dual

The **Langlands dual group** ^LG is the connected reductive group over $\mathbb{C}$ whose root datum is the dual of that of G:

^LG has root datum (X_*, $\Phi$^∨, X*, $\Phi$)

In other words, exchange the character and cocharacter lattices, and exchange roots with coroots. This operation is an involution: ^L(^LG) ≅ G.

### Key Examples

| G | ^LG | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| SU(n) | SU(n)/$\mathbb{Z}$_n = PSU(n) | The center $\mathbb{Z}$_n is "dualized away" |
| SO(2n+1) | Sp(2n) | Odd orthogonal dual to symplectic: B_n ↔ C_n |
| SO(2n) | SO(2n) | Self-dual (type D_n, with a subtle $\mathbb{Z}$_2 twist for Spin groups) |
| Sp(2n) | SO(2n+1) | Symplectic dual to odd orthogonal |
| G$_2$ | G$_2$ | Self-dual (all simply-laced groups are self-dual up to center) |

For G = SU(n), the Langlands dual ^LG = SU(n)/$\mathbb{Z}$_n has the same Lie algebra 𝔰𝔲(n) but a different center: the center of SU(n) is $\mathbb{Z}$_n, while ^LG is centerless. This distinction matters: S-duality in N = 4 SYM exchanges not just the coupling but also the global form of the gauge group.

## 4. Topological Field Theory

### Topological Twisting

A supersymmetric field theory on a curved manifold generally breaks supersymmetry because constant spinors do not exist on a generic curved space. **Topological twisting** solves this: one redefines the Lorentz group by mixing it with the R-symmetry group,

SO(4) $\to$ SO(4)′ = diag(SO(4) $\times$ U(1)_R)

such that some supercharges become scalars under the new Lorentz group. Scalar supercharges are well-defined on any curved manifold, so supersymmetry is preserved. The resulting theory is a **topological field theory (TFT)**: its correlation functions depend only on the topology of the underlying manifold.

### BRST Operator and Cohomological TFT

The scalar supercharge, denoted Q, is nilpotent: Q$^2$ = 0 (up to a gauge transformation). It plays the role of a **BRST operator**. Physical observables in a cohomological TFT are the Q-cohomology classes: O ∼ O + {Q, $\Lambda$}. The stress-energy tensor is Q-exact, T_$\mu \nu$ = {Q, G_$\mu \nu$}, which ensures metric-independence of correlation functions.

### The GL Twist

For the application to geometric Langlands, Kapustin and Witten use the **GL twist** (named after Geometric Langlands). Starting with N = 4 SYM on a four-manifold M = $\Sigma \times$ C, where $\Sigma$ and C are Riemann surfaces, the GL twist produces **two** independent scalar supercharges, Q_ℓ and Q_r. This is a key technical point: having two nilpotent symmetries allows the theory to be analysed via a two-step reduction. In the limit where $\Sigma$ shrinks to zero size, the effective theory on C is a two-dimensional topological sigma model whose branes are precisely the Hecke eigensheaves of the geometric Langlands program.

## 5. Sheaves and D-Modules

### Presheaves and Sheaves

A **presheaf** F on a topological space X assigns to each open set U ⊂ X a set (or abelian group, or vector space) F(U), together with restriction maps $\rho$_VU: F(U) $\to$ F(V) for V ⊂ U, satisfying $\rho$_UU = id and $\rho$_WV ∘ $\rho$_VU = $\rho$_WU.

A presheaf is a **sheaf** if it satisfies two additional axioms:
- **Locality**: If an open cover {U_i} of U and sections s, t ∈ F(U) satisfy s|_{U_i} = t|_{U_i} for all i, then s = t.
- **Gluing**: If sections s_i ∈ F(U_i) agree on overlaps, s_i|_{U_i∩U_j} = s_j|_{U_i∩U_j}, there exists s ∈ F(U) with s|_{U_i} = s_i.

Think of a sheaf as a systematic assignment of local data that can be glued together — the natural language for local-to-global constructions in geometry.

### Key Examples

- **Constant sheaf** $\mathbb{C}$_X: F(U) = locally constant functions U $\to \mathbb{C}$.
- **Structure sheaf** 𝒪_X: F(U) = holomorphic (or regular) functions on U.
- **Sheaf of sections** of a vector bundle E: F(U) = $\Gamma$(U, E).

### D-Modules

Let X be a smooth algebraic variety. The sheaf of differential operators D_X is the sheaf of non-commutative $\mathbb{C}$-algebras generated by 𝒪_X (functions) and the tangent sheaf T_X (vector fields), subject to the commutation relation [$\xi$, f] = $\xi$(f) for a vector field $\xi$ and a function f.

A **D-module** is a sheaf M of left D_X-modules — that is, for each open U, M(U) is a module over the ring of differential operators D_X(U), compatibly with restrictions.

The fundamental example: given a vector bundle E with a **flat connection** $\nabla$, the sheaf of flat sections (i.e., sections s satisfying $\nabla$s = 0) is a D-module. Conversely, a D-module whose underlying 𝒪_X-module is coherent and locally free corresponds to a vector bundle with flat connection. This is the **Riemann–Hilbert correspondence**: the category of D-modules with regular singularities is equivalent to the category of perverse sheaves on X.

### Relevance to Geometric Langlands

In the geometric Langlands program, the objects associated to representations of the fundamental group (or Galois group) are not functions but **Hecke eigensheaves** — D-modules on the moduli stack Bun_G(X) of G-bundles on a curve X. Understanding D-modules is therefore essential; they are the geometric "automorphic forms" that the Langlands correspondence relates to Galois representations.

## 6. Derived Categories

### Motivation

Many geometric constructions — resolutions, deformations, cohomology — naturally involve **chain complexes** of sheaves rather than single sheaves. Two different resolutions of the same object are not isomorphic but are **quasi-isomorphic** (their cohomologies agree). The correct environment to work in is the **derived category**, where quasi-isomorphisms are formally inverted.

### Definition (Sketch)

Given an abelian category A (e.g., the category of sheaves of 𝒪_X-modules), the **derived category** D(A) is constructed in two steps:
1. Form the category of chain complexes Kom(A).
2. **Localize** at the class of quasi-isomorphisms — formally add inverses to every morphism that induces an isomorphism on cohomology.

The result is a triangulated category; the shift functor [1] corresponds to shifting a complex, and distinguished triangles replace short exact sequences as the fundamental structure. The derived category retains all the cohomological information: Hⁱ(K) ≅ Hom_{D(A)}(A, K[i]).

### D^b(Coh) and D^b(D-mod)

In the geometric Langlands context, two particular derived categories matter:

- **D^b(Coh(Y))** — the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves on a variety Y. Objects are complexes of sheaves whose cohomology sheaves are coherent (finitely generated) and vanish outside a bounded range.

- **D^b(D-mod_X)** — the bounded derived category of D-modules on X.

### Why Derived Categories Matter

The geometric Langlands correspondence is most naturally formulated as an equivalence of derived categories:

D^b(D-mod_{Bun_G(X)}) ≅ D^b(Coh_{Loc_{^LG}(X)})

The left-hand side involves D-modules on the moduli stack of G-bundles on a curve X; the right-hand side involves coherent sheaves on the moduli stack of ^LG-local systems on X. This is a **Fourier–Mukai-type** equivalence, analogous to T-duality in mirror symmetry. Working at the derived level is essential because the correspondence exchanges not just individual objects but entire complexes — the Hecke eigensheaf condition involves cohomological operations (Hecke correspondences) that are intrinsically derived.

## Further Reading

1. **Kapustin & Witten (2007).** *Electric-Magnetic Duality and the Geometric Langlands Program.* Comm. Number Theory Phys., 1(1):1–236. The primary source. Section 2 (gauge theory review) and Section 3 (topological twisting) are accessible entry points.

2. **Frenkel, E. (2007).** *Lectures on the Langlands Program and Conformal Field Theory.* Les Houches, 2005. A pedagogical overview connecting conformal field theory, affine Kac–Moody algebras, and the geometric Langlands program. Excellent for understanding the representation-theoretic side.

3. **Nakahara, M. (2003).** *Geometry, Topology and Physics.* Chapters 9–10 cover fibre bundles, connections, and characteristic classes at a level accessible to physics students.

4. **Gaiotto, D. (2014).** S-duality and the geometric Langlands program. Online lecture notes. A concise review of the physical arguments, focusing on the GL twist, surface operators, and brane constructions.

5. **Tong, D. (2018).** *Gauge Theory.* Lecture notes, Cambridge. Covers Yang–Mills theory, instantons, and supersymmetry in a clear, physical style.

6. **Hotta, R., Takeuchi, K., & Tanisaki, T. (2008).** *D-Modules, Perverse Sheaves, and Representation Theory.* The standard reference for D-modules. Chapters 1–3 provide the necessary algebraic geometry background.

7. **Gelfand, S. & Manin, Y. (2003).** *Methods of Homological Algebra.* The classic introduction to derived categories and triangulated categories, with motivation from algebraic geometry.

8. **Borel, A. (1979).** *Automorphic L-Functions.* Proc. Symp. Pure Math. 33(2):27–61. The classic exposition of the Langlands dual group from the automorphic perspective, including the root datum construction.
