# Irregular Sequences and the Distribution of Primes

Certain sequences of integers — the prime numbers are the canonical example — appear irregular when listed in order on the number line. The gaps between consecutive primes fluctuate in ways that resist simple closed‑form description. The sequence looks random: it passes many statistical tests for randomness, yet it is generated by a perfectly deterministic rule. A number is prime if it is not divisible by any smaller integer greater than 1. No dice are thrown; every entry follows from a precise combinatorial condition. The surface disorder therefore demands a different explanation.

The key is to change the stage. Instead of taking the additive number line as the fundamental backdrop, place the natural numbers in a tree whose branching reflects divisibility — a **divisibility tree**. In such a tree, numbers that share prime factors lie on nearby branches, while numbers with distinct factorisations diverge early. Two simple constructions make this precise.

The **prime‑factor tree** builds from the root \(1\). The children of a node \(m\) are all numbers \(m \cdot q\) where \(q \ge p_{\min}(m)\) (the smallest prime factor of \(m\); for \(m=1\) we take \(q\) all primes). In this tree primes are exactly the nodes at depth one — the immediate offspring of the unit. Their placement is as simple as could be: they are the first‑generation branching points that carry the entire multiplicative structure of the integers.

Another incarnation, deeper and more arithmetically charged, is the **Stern–Brocot tree**. It houses every positive rational \(\frac{a}{b}\) in lowest terms. The path from the root \(\frac{1}{1}\) to \(\frac{a}{b}\) records the sequence of quotients in the Euclidean algorithm for the pair \((a,b)\). This is a pure divisibility narrative: the tree distance between two fractions measures how many steps of the enlarged Euclidean algorithm they share, how closely their divisibility properties align. The positive integers appear as the extreme right branch \(\frac{1}{1} \to \frac{2}{1} \to \frac{3}{1} \to \frac{4}{1} \to \dots\). In this universe, primes are again the integers \(\frac{p}{1}\) that cannot be reached by a splitting path at any internal composite node. They occupy the boundary trunk that never sends off a proper fraction. The arrangement is utterly structured and deterministic.

Now look at the usual number line. It is an additive object: the distance between \(n\) and \(m\) is \(|n-m|\), a notion alien to the multiplicative tree. Listing the primes in increasing order amounts to a **projection** — a flattening of the tree onto a line that discards branching information. Two primes that are intimate in the tree (both are depth‑one children of \(1\)) can be far apart on the line because the line only hears differences, not shared parentage. The predictable tree positions become scrambled gaps.

The **Monna map** (and its inverse, the Minkowski question mark function \(?(x)\)) gives this projection an exquisitely precise arithmetic form. The map \(M\) sends the binary tree of dyadic intervals — a purely additive structure — to the Stern–Brocot tree, twisting the metric violently. Under \(M\), a smooth measure on the binary tree pushes forward to a fractal measure; the regular branching of the Stern–Brocot tree, when viewed through the binary lens, shatters into a singular devil’s staircase. If we embed the divisibility tree into the unit interval via this arithmetic projection, the primes’ deterministic positions in the tree become points whose pairwise distances are the scrambled values of \(M(p_n)\) or \(?(p_n)\). The irregular gaps on the ordinary number line are precisely the gaps between these projections. The irregularity is a **projection artifact**.

The Monna map is not a curiosity. It connects deeply to the modular group \(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\) and to Farey sequences, which govern the distribution of rational approximations. The map distorts distances in a way that mirrors the tension between additive and multiplicative number theory. The “randomness” of prime gaps is not a signal of fundamental lawlessness; it is a symptom of asking an additive question (how far away is the next prime?) about an object whose natural home is multiplicative.

This is not a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis nor a derivation of the Prime Number Theorem. It is a shift of perspective. The primes, so erratic on the line, become regular when placed where they belong — on a tree of divisibility. The surface features of fluctuation and apparent randomness are exactly what an arithmetic projection like the Monna map produces when it scrambles a deterministic tree. What we perceive as irregularity is, in the end, the shadow of a perfectly lawful reality cast onto a foreign coordinate system.
