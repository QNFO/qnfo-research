Morphological Type (Isolating vs. Synthetic): This is one of the clearest indicators. In highly isolating languages like Mandarin Chinese, each word typically represents a single morpheme. In contrast, synthetic languages (like many in the Indo-European family) pack multiple morphemes into a single word through inflection.

Impact on Frequency: This has a profound effect on Zipf's law. The canonical word-level Zipf's law is robust for synthetic Indo-European languages but does not hold for character-based isolating languages like Chinese, Japanese, and Korean. For these languages, the law applies more accurately at the character level, as characters are a better analogue to the "packed" word forms in synthetic languages. A study of Bible translations confirmed that different "distinct synthetic structures lead to different Zipf's exponents" across various language families.

Information Density (Trade-off): There is a well-documented trade-off between word-internal information (morphological complexity) and across-word information (reliance on word order). Languages that are morphologically rich (synthetic) tend to pack more information into a single word, allowing for freer word order. Conversely, isolating languages rely heavily on word order and auxiliary words to convey grammatical relationships.

Impact on Frequency: This trade-off is measurable. Cross-linguistic studies using metrics like Word Information Density confirm a systematic trade-off between word-internal and across-word distributions of information, directly linking word frequency patterns to a language's syntactic strategy.

Lexical Diversity: This can be measured by the Type-Token Ratio (TTR), which is the ratio of unique words (types) to the total number of words (tokens) in a text. A language with a rich morphological system will naturally generate many different word forms from a single root, thus having a higher TTR than a language with limited inflection.

Communicative Efficiency: Zipf's "Law of Abbreviation"—the principle that more frequent words tend to be shorter—holds true cross-linguistically, demonstrating a universal pressure for communicative efficiency. More frequent words are statistically shorter and often morphologically simpler than less frequent words in virtually every language.

Genealogical Relatedness and Historical Change: The similarity of word frequencies reflects language history. Related languages (e.g., English and German) tend to share concepts with more similar frequencies than non-related languages (e.g., English and Chinese). 🧠 What Is the Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis?
It’s the idea that the language we speak influences how we think. There are two classic versions:

Linguistic Determinism (strong version): Language determines thought. You cannot conceive of ideas that your language lacks words or grammar for. This is largely discredited in its absolute form.

Linguistic Relativity (weak version): Language influences habitual thought, attention, and memory. Speaking a language trains you to habitually pay attention to certain aspects of reality because its grammar and vocabulary require it. This has considerable empirical support today.
