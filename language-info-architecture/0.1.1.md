# A Bayesian Cross‑Linguistic Investigation of Word Frequency Distributions, Morphological Typology, and the Sapir‑Whorf Hypothesis

**An LLM‑orchestrated, simulation‑based research plan**

---

## Abstract

All languages obey statistical regularities such as Zipf’s law, but the exact shape of the word‑frequency distribution varies systematically with morphological type. At the same time, the weak form of the Sapir‑Whorf hypothesis (linguistic relativity) suggests that obligatorily encoded grammatical categories can shape habitual thought. This research plan proposes a fully simulation‑based, Bayesian hierarchical study that uses the parametric knowledge of a large language model (LLM) to generate realistic word‑frequency profiles for 20 typologically diverse languages and to recall published psycholinguistic findings. The plan integrates these strands to (1) estimate language‑ and family‑level Zipf exponents and cross‑ratios, (2) quantify the frequency load of obligatorily marked Whorfian categories (evidentiality, gender, classifiers, spatial frames), and (3) model the relationship between that frequency load and the magnitude of cognitive biases reported in the literature. All analyses are performed within a single LLM thread using internal knowledge and embedded Python code execution, with explicit Bayesian priors derived from linguistic typology. The result is a fully reproducible, self‑contained research pipeline that demonstrates how LLMs can serve as laboratories for computational linguistics and cognitive science.

## 1. Introduction and Background

### 1.1 Zipf’s Law and Word Frequency Distributions

For any large corpus of natural language, the frequency of the *r*‑th most common word is roughly proportional to \(1/r^\alpha\), with \(\alpha \approx 1\). This empirical relationship, known as Zipf’s law, holds remarkably well across languages (Zipf, 1949; Piantadosi, 2014). The ratio between the frequencies of the first and second most common words—the “cross‑ratio”—is therefore expected to be close to 2:1. However, systematic departures from this idealised pattern occur when languages differ in morphological structure.

- In **isolating** languages (e.g., Mandarin Chinese), each word tends to be a single morpheme, and the most frequent “words” are often grammatical particles or content morphemes. When analysed at the character level, the distribution is close to the canonical Zipfian form.
- In **agglutinative** languages (e.g., Turkish, Finnish), a single root may combine with many distinct suffixes, generating a large number of inflected word forms. This “morphological splintering” reduces the dominance of the top‑ranked word and flattens the frequency curve, often resulting in a lower effective Zipf exponent \(\alpha\).
- **Fusional** languages (e.g., Russian, Arabic) fuse several grammatical categories into a single affix, and their word‑frequency distributions typically exhibit exponents near 1.0.
- **Polysynthetic** languages (e.g., Greenlandic, Navajo) encode entire clauses within a single word, leading to an extremely flat distribution at high ranks followed by a steep drop‑off for lexical items.

These observations are robust cross‑linguistic generalisations, often clustering within language families and geographic areas (Bentz et al., 2014; Piantadosi, 2014). Consequently, Zipfian parameters such as the exponent \(\alpha\) and the cross‑ratio between consecutive ranks can serve as typological diagnostics.

### 1.2 The Sapir‑Whorf Hypothesis and Frequency of Encoding

The Sapir‑Whorf hypothesis, particularly in its “weak” or “thinking‑for‑speaking” form (Slobin, 1996), posits that the grammatical categories a language obligatorily marks can influence habitual patterns of attention and memory. If a language requires speakers to specify, for example, the source of evidence for every past event (evidentiality), speakers of that language may develop a heightened sensitivity to evidential sources compared to speakers of languages where such marking is optional or absent.

Key testable domains include:
- **Evidentiality** (Turkish, Quechua, Tibetan): improved source memory.
- **Grammatical gender** (French, Spanish, German): possible influences on object categorisation.
- **Numeral classifiers** (Mandarin, Japanese): effects on object individuation (or lack thereof).
- **Spatial frames of reference** (Guugu Yimithirr, Tzeltal): absolute vs. relative spatial cognition.

A crucial—but underexplored—link is the *frequency* with which such markers occur in natural speech. If a cognitive habit is to be reinforced, the relevant grammatical distinction must be encountered or used with high regularity. Therefore, the relative frequency load of a Whorfian category across languages should correlate with the size and reliability of any measured cognitive effect.

### 1.3 Why a Bayesian Simulation‑Based Approach?

A full cross‑linguistic corpus study with genuine psycholinguistic meta‑analysis would require enormous resources. However, modern large language models possess extensive parametric knowledge of word frequencies, linguistic typology, and published experimental results. This plan leverages that knowledge to simulate the entire research pipeline within a single LLM thread. Crucially, we adopt a **Bayesian framework** for several reasons:
- It allows us to formally incorporate prior typological knowledge (e.g., “agglutinative languages tend to have lower Zipf exponents”) rather than treating each language as an independent data point.
- Hierarchical modelling enables partial pooling, borrowing statistical strength across related languages and families.
- Uncertainty is propagated through all stages, from frequency estimates to effect‑size modelling.
- Hypotheses can be compared directly using Bayes factors.

The result is a proof‑of‑concept that an LLM can orchestrate a principled, theory‑driven study using only its internal knowledge and embedded code execution.

## 2. Research Questions and Hypotheses

**RQ1:** How do Zipf exponents and cross‑ratios vary across languages of different morphological types and language families?

- *H1a*: Agglutinative languages will exhibit significantly lower Zipf exponents (flatter distributions) than fusional and isolating languages.
- *H1b*: The cross‑ratio rank‑1/rank‑2 will be lower (<1.5) in agglutinative and polysynthetic languages and higher (>1.8) in fusional/isolating languages.
- *H1c*: Language family‑level groupings will explain substantial variance in these parameters, consistent with genealogical inheritance and areal diffusion.

**RQ2:** Does the relative frequency load (cumulative token percentage) of obligatorily marked cognitive categories predict the presence or size of Sapir‑Whorf effects reported in the literature?

- *H2a*: For evidentiality, languages with a frequency load >1.5% will show positive source‑memory effects; languages with lower loads will not.
- *H2b*: The correlation between frequency load and effect size (Cohen’s *d*) across languages and domains will be positive and substantially different from zero.
- *H2c*: A model that includes frequency load as a predictor of effect size will outperform a null model that assumes no relationship.

### 3.2 Data Generation via LLM Internal Knowledge

For each language, the LLM will generate:
- The 200 most frequent word‑forms (or the linguistically appropriate unit: characters for Chinese, morphemes if needed for polysynthetic languages) along with an **estimated percentage frequency** of all tokens in a balanced corpus.
- Each percentage is given as a **95% credible interval** (e.g., 5.5–6.5%) to reflect the LLM’s uncertainty about exact corpus figures.
- For a subset of languages, the LLM will generate multiple plausible rank‑frequency profiles (e.g., 5 samples) to better capture within‑language variability.

The LLM will also recall, from its training data, **published psycholinguistic findings** relevant to each Whorfian domain, including effect sizes (Cohen’s *d*), sample sizes, and whether the effect was statistically significant. These will be treated as “observed” data points for the meta‑analytic step.

### 3.3 Bayesian Framework Overview

All parameters are estimated using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) via custom Metropolis‑Hastings samplers implemented in Python within the LLM thread. Priors are chosen to reflect established typological knowledge (see Table 1). The likelihoods are based on multinomial or normal approximations, depending on the analysis.

**Table 1: Key Priors**

| Parameter | Prior |
|-----------|-------|
| Zipf exponent \(\alpha\) (language‑level) | \(\alpha \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_{\text{family}}, 0.15)\) truncated to [0.5, 1.5] |
| \(\mu_{\text{family}}\) | \(\mu_{\text{family}} \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_{\text{global}}, 0.1)\) |
| \(\mu_{\text{global}}\) | \(\text{Normal}(0.95, 0.1)\) |
| Cross‑ratio log(rank1/rank2) | \(\log(\text{CR}) \sim \text{Normal}(\log(1.8), 0.3)\) for fusional; shifted for agglutinative etc. |
| Frequency load \(p\) of Whorfian category (e.g., evidential) | \(p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_p, \beta_p)\) with \(\alpha_p=2, \beta_p=5\) if not obligatory; \(\alpha_p=8, \beta_p=2\) if obligatory |
| Effect size \(d\) (cognitive bias) | \(d \sim \text{Normal}(0, 0.5)\) (regularising prior) |

### 3.4 Analysis 1: Zipf Exponent and Cross‑Ratio Estimation

For each language, the synthetic frequency vector \(\mathbf{f} = (f_1, f_2, \dots, f_{200})\) is modelled as:
\[
f_i = C \cdot i^{-\alpha} + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \sim \text{Normal}(0, \sigma^2)
\]
with the constraint \(\sum f_i = 1\) (normalisation is done implicitly during MCMC by rescaling). The log‑likelihood is computed using a Dirichlet‑multinomial approximation (or log‑normal for simplification). Metropolis‑Hastings sampling yields posterior distributions for \(\alpha\) and for the cross‑ratios \(\text{CR}_{1/2} = f_1/f_2\) and \(\text{CR}_{1/10}\).

**Prior for \(\alpha\)**: language‑level prior centred on family mean, which itself is drawn from a global distribution (see hierarchical model below).

### 3.5 Analysis 2: Hierarchical Model for Typological Clustering

We fit a partial‑pooling model:
\[
\alpha_j \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_{\text{fam}[j]}, \tau_{\text{fam}}) \quad \text{for language } j
\]
\[
\mu_k \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_0, \tau_0) \quad \text{for family } k
\]
with hyperpriors \(\tau_{\text{fam}}, \tau_0 \sim \text{HalfNormal}(0.1)\). This model is fit to the posterior modes (or the entire posterior samples) from the language‑level analyses, using a second MCMC step.

We then compute:
- Posterior probability that \(\mu_{\text{agglutinative}} < \mu_{\text{fusional}}\).
- Posterior predictive checks: how well the model reproduces the observed cross‑ratios.
- Bayes factor comparing a model with family‑specific means to a single global mean.

### 3.6 Analysis 3: Frequency Load of Whorfian Categories

For each language and each domain (evidentiality, gender, classifiers, spatial reference), the LLM identifies the relevant morphemes/words in the top‑200 list and sums their estimated frequencies to obtain a “frequency load” \(L\). We model the true load \(p\) as:
\[
\text{observed count of tokens} \sim \text{Binomial}(N, p)
\]
where \(N\) is the total number of tokens represented by the top‑200 list (e.g., sum of 200 percentages rescaled to 1).

The posterior of \(p\) is Beta‑binomial updated from the prior described in Table 1.

### 3.7 Meta‑Analysis: Linking Frequency Load to Whorfian Effect Sizes

The LLM retrieves from memory the following:
- For each language–domain pair, the reported effect size \(d_i\) (Cohen’s *d*) and its standard error \(se_i\) (if available) from a key psycholinguistic experiment.
- If a language did not participate in a known experiment, the domain is omitted or attributed a “null” effect with a diffuse prior.

We then fit a Bayesian meta‑regression:
\[
d_i \sim \text{Normal}(\theta_i, se_i^2)
\]
\[
\theta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{logit}(p_i)
\]
with priors \(\beta_0 \sim \text{Normal}(0, 0.5)\), \(\beta_1 \sim \text{Normal}(0, 0.3)\).

The key test is against the null model \(\beta_1 = 0\). We compute the Bayes factor \(\text{BF}_{10}\) using the Savage–Dickey density ratio. A \(\text{BF}_{10} > 10\) would be considered strong evidence for a positive relationship.

### 3.8 Implementation and Reproducibility

All code is written and executed stepwise in the LLM thread. The LLM documents each prior choice, outputs trace plots (ASCII or basic matplotlib), and reports posterior summaries (mean, 95% HDI, R‑hat). The entire research pipeline—from prior elicitation to final Bayes factor—is contained within a single conversation.

## 4. Expected Results and Interpretation

We anticipate the following outcomes based on known typological patterns:

- **Zipf exponents and cross‑ratios**: Agglutinative (Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian) and polysynthetic (Greenlandic, Navajo) languages will show posterior mean \(\alpha\) below 0.95 and cross‑ratios rank‑1/2 below 1.4. Fusional and isolating languages will cluster near \(\alpha = 1.0\) and cross‑ratios near 2.0. The hierarchical model will shrink individual language estimates toward family means, revealing clear genealogical signals.
- **Whorfian frequency loads**: Turkish, Quechua, and Tibetan will have high posterior loads (>1.5%) for evidential markers, while evidential load in English or Spanish will be negligible. Gender‑marking loads will be highest in German and Slavic languages.
- **Meta‑regression**: The Bayes factor will favour a positive association between evidential load and source‑memory effect size. For gender, the evidence will be mixed, consistent with the contested literature. The model will demonstrate that frequency of obligation is a useful predictor of cognitive habit strength, supporting a quantitative formulation of the thinking‑for‑speaking hypothesis.

## 5. Limitations

- **Synthetic data**: All frequency data and literature summaries are generated from the LLM’s stored knowledge, not from actual corpora or systematic review. The numbers may be imprecise or biased toward well‑resourced languages.
- **LLM training biases**: The model may over‑represent European languages and certain psycholinguistic paradigms, potentially inflating effect sizes or missing null results.
- **No genuine psychometric data**: The meta‑analysis relies on the LLM’s recollection of published results, which might be incomplete or inaccurate.
- **Simplified likelihoods**: True corpus frequencies follow complex distributions (e.g., Poisson‑lognormal), but we use normal approximations for tractability.
- **Domain scope**: Only a handful of Whorfian domains are examined; many others (e.g., colour, time, causality) could be added.
- **LLM as researcher**: The entire study is confined to a single thread without web access, so it cannot validate its own outputs against ground truth.

Despite these limitations, the Bayesian simulation framework provides a transparent and rigorous way to explore the connections between word‑frequency distributions, typology, and linguistic relativity, and it serves as a template for future LLM‑assisted linguistic research.

---

## 6. Conclusion

This research plan outlines a completely self‑contained, LLM‑orchestrated study that unites Zipf’s law, morphological typology, and the Sapir‑Whorf hypothesis under a Bayesian hierarchical model. By treating the LLM as both a data source and a statistical computing platform, the plan produces a reproducible, probabilistic analysis that can be executed without external resources. The expected findings will reinforce the diagnostic power of cross‑ratios for typological classification and quantify the role of frequency in linguistic relativity, thereby advancing our understanding of how language structure interfaces with cognition.
