# Language as Information Architecture: A Cross-Linguistic Investigation of Mandatory Encoding, Information Density, and the Design Space of Human Communication

**A reframed research plan — from Sapir-Whorf to Jakobson, Shannon, Grice, and Greenberg**

**Version:** 0.5.0 — Complete reframing following assumption audit (0.4.md review)
**Status:** Ready for execution

---

## Abstract

All languages can express roughly the same range of ideas. But they differ radically in what information *must* be included in the signal and what can be left implicit. This plan reframes the Word Cross-Ratio project away from the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (psychology: "does language shape thought?") and toward an information-theoretic investigation of language architecture (communication: "what information do different languages force into the channel?"). Drawing on Roman Jakobson's insight that "languages differ essentially in what they *must* convey," we repurpose the existing Bayesian measurement apparatus to quantify: (1) Shannon information density per word across 22 typologically diverse languages, (2) the mandatory epistemic, ontological, spatial, and categorical metadata load of each language, (3) the Gricean surplus — how much each language forces speakers to exceed cooperative norms, (4) Greenbergian correlations between mandatory categories, and (5) the position of each language in the design space of possible human communication systems, testing for empty regions that may reveal universal information-theoretic constraints.

## 1. Introduction: The Wrong Question and the Right One

### 1.1 What the Original Project Asked

The original framing (0.1.1.md, 0.1.2.md) asked: "Does the frequency with which a Whorfian category is obligatorily marked predict the magnitude of cognitive effects?" This was always problematic:

1. **It reversed the causal arrow.** Frequency of marking is a symptom of grammatical obligatoriness, not an independent cause of cognitive habits.
2. **It violated Zipf's own logic.** Zipf's law states that information content is inversely proportional to frequency. High-frequency words carry the *least* information. The project assumed the opposite — that frequent markers have the greatest cognitive impact.
3. **It required cross-domain commensurability that doesn't exist.** Evidentiality effects (source memory, $d \approx 0.45$–$0.72$) and gender effects (object categorization, $d \approx 0.05$–$0.30$) measure different cognitive constructs and are not comparable on a single scale.
4. **The null result was predetermined.** The synthetic effect sizes were generated independently of frequency loads, guaranteeing $\beta_1 \approx 0$.

### 1.2 The Right Question

Roman Jakobson (1959) gave us the correct framing:

> **"Languages differ essentially in what they *must* convey and not in what they *may* convey."**

This is not psychology. This is **information theory** applied to communication systems. Every language is an information channel. Every utterance must transmit certain metadata — and different languages mandate different metadata. Some languages require epistemic marking (how do you know this?). Some require ontological categorization (what kind of thing is this?). Some require spatial coordinate tracking (where is this relative to absolute directions?). Some require all three. Some require almost none.

The question is not "does this shape thought?" — it is: **"What does the distribution of mandatory information across languages reveal about the architecture of human communication? Are there universal constraints on how much metadata a language can force into the signal? Does the design space of possible languages have uninhabited regions, and if so, why?"**

### 1.3 The Theoretical Framework

This reframed project draws on four theoretical traditions:

| Tradition | Key insight | Application |
|:----------|:------------|:------------|
| **Jakobson (1959)** | Languages differ in mandatory vs. optional encoding | Quantify the mandatory load per language |
| **Shannon (1948)** | Information is measurable in bits; entropy = $-\sum p_i \log_2 p_i$ | Compute information density per word across languages |
| **Grice (1975)** | Cooperative communication requires optimal informativeness | Measure the Gricean surplus — forced over-informativeness |
| **Greenberg (1963)** | Implicational universals constrain possible language types | Test whether mandatory loads cluster as universals predict |

Together, these form an **information architecture** framework: languages are communication systems with different mandatory metadata schemas, and the space of possible schemas may be constrained by universal information-theoretic limits.

## 2. Research Questions

### RQ1: Information Density

**How do languages differ in the Shannon entropy of their word-frequency distributions?**

For each language, compute: $H = -\sum_{i=1}^{200} p_i \log_2 p_i$, where $p_i$ is the frequency of the $i$-th most common word-form. This measures the average information content (in bits) per word token. A language with high entropy distributes its information payload across many distinct word-forms. A language with low entropy concentrates information in a few high-frequency words.

**Prediction:** Polysynthetic languages (Greenlandic, Navajo) will have the highest entropy ($H > 6.5$ bits/word) because each word-form is unique — morphological splintering creates many distinct surface forms. Isolating languages (Mandarin, Cantonese) will have lower entropy ($H < 5.5$) because a few grammatical particles dominate.

### RQ2: Mandatory Information Architecture

**For each language, what proportion of tokens carries mandatory epistemic, ontological, spatial, and categorical metadata?**

Extend the "Whorfian load" analysis to four fundamental types of mandatory information:

| Information type | Domain | What the grammar forces speakers to encode | Example languages |
|:-----------------|:-------|:------------------------------------------|:------------------|
| **Epistemic** | Evidentiality, mood, modality | Source and certainty of knowledge | Turkish, Quechua, Tibetan, Korean |
| **Ontological** | Gender, noun class, animacy | What kind of entity is being referred to | German, Arabic, Russian, French |
| **Categorical (dynamic)** | Classifiers, measure words | Shape, animacy, function of counted objects | Mandarin, Japanese, Cantonese |
| **Spatial** | Absolute frames, directional verbs | Position in absolute spatial coordinates | Navajo, Greenlandic |

**Prediction:** Total mandatory load (sum across all four types) will show an upper bound — no language forces more than $\sim$10–12% of its tokens to carry mandatory metadata. Beyond this, the communication channel may become overloaded.

### RQ3: The Compression-Tax Trade-Off

**Do languages that compress information into fewer high-frequency words impose higher mandatory ontological/epistemic taxes?**

The hypothesis: languages have two strategies for managing information. They can either (a) spread information across many distinct word-forms through rich morphology (agglutinative/polysynthetic strategy) or (b) concentrate grammatical information in a few high-frequency function words and compensate by requiring explicit category marking elsewhere (isolating/fusional strategy). If true, there should be a negative correlation between Shannon entropy per word and total mandatory load.

**Prediction:** $r(H, \text{total mandatory load}) < -0.3$ — higher-entropy languages have lower mandatory loads.

### RQ4: The Design Space of Possible Languages

**When languages are plotted in the multidimensional space of (information density, epistemic load, ontological load, morphological complexity), are there empty regions? What constrains the habitable zone?**

This is the central theoretical contribution: mapping the actual design space of human languages and testing whether it is constrained by information-theoretic limits. If certain regions are empty (e.g., "high ontological load + high epistemic load + low entropy"), this suggests a universal constraint on how human communication systems can be configured.

**Prediction:** The habitable zone will be bounded — extreme values on multiple dimensions simultaneously will be absent. The boundary of the habitable zone represents the information-theoretic limits of human language.

### RQ5: Greenbergian Correlations

**Do mandatory category loads cluster as Greenberg's implicational universals predict?**

Test specific predictions:
- If a language has high gender load, it should also have number-marking load (Universal 36)
- If the dominant word order is SOV, case-marking load should be higher (Universal 41)
- Classifier languages should have lower number-marking load (not directly a Greenberg universal, but a known typological correlation)

**Prediction:** Cluster analysis of mandatory loads will recover known typological groupings, validating the measurement approach.

### RQ6: The Gricean Surplus

**For each language-domain pair, what is the "Gricean surplus" — the amount of information the grammar forces the speaker to provide beyond what would be cooperatively optimal?**

Define the Gricean surplus $S_{G}$ for a language-domain pair as: $S_{G} = L_{\text{mandatory}} - L_{\text{optional-minimum}}$, where $L_{\text{optional-minimum}}$ is the minimum load for that domain across all languages (representing what is cooperatively necessary). Languages with high $S_{G}$ force speakers to provide far more information than Gricean norms would require.

**Prediction:** Languages with higher Gricean surplus will have more restricted information-encoding options — the grammar preempts pragmatic choice.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Repurposing the Measurement Apparatus

The existing simulation pipeline (0.3.py) generated synthetic word-frequency profiles for 22 languages and estimated Zipf exponents, cross-ratios, and Whorfian frequency loads. We repurpose this apparatus with the following extensions:

| Existing measure | Reframed measure | New computation |
|:-----------------|:-----------------|:----------------|
| Zipf exponent $\alpha$ | Information density (Shannon entropy $H$) | $H = -\sum p_i \log_2 p_i$ from frequency vector |
| Cross-ratio $\text{CR}_{1/2}$ | Information concentration (dominance of top word) | Existing — reinterpreted |
| Whorfian load (per domain) | Mandatory information load by type | Existing — reclassified into 4 types |
| Morphological type | Morphological complexity index | Ordinal: 1=isolating, 2=fusional, 3=mixed, 4=agglutinative, 5=polysynthetic |
| — | Gricean surplus | $S_G = L_i - L_{\min}$ per domain |
| — | Design space coordinates | PCA on ($H$, loads, morph-complexity) |
| — | Greenbergian clusters | Correlation matrix of mandatory loads |

### 3.2 Information-Theoretic Measures

#### Shannon Entropy

For each language, from the mean frequency vector $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, \dots, p_{200})$ (rescaled to sum to 1):

$$H = -\sum_{i=1}^{200} p_i \log_2 p_i$$

The maximum possible entropy for 200 categories is $\log_2(200) \approx 7.64$ bits (uniform distribution). The minimum approaches 0 bits (one word dominates completely). Real languages fall somewhere in between.

We also compute:
- **Normalized entropy:** $H_{\text{norm}} = H / \log_2(200)$ — what fraction of maximum possible information is used
- **Information concentration:** $C = p_1$ (the proportion of tokens accounted for by the single most frequent word)
- **Effective vocabulary size:** $N_{\text{eff}} = 2^{H}$ — the number of equally-frequent words that would produce the same entropy

#### Information per Word

Combining entropy with morphological type, we estimate the **information payload per word-form**:

| Morphological type | Typical morphemes per word | Information per word |
|:-------------------|:--------------------------|:---------------------|
| Isolating | ~1.0 | Low: each word carries ~1 semantic unit |
| Fusional | ~1.5–2.0 | Medium: fused morphemes pack multiple units |
| Agglutinative | ~2.0–3.0 | High: stacked morphemes each carry a unit |
| Polysynthetic | ~4.0+ | Very high: clause-level information per word |

Multiplying entropy per word-form by morphemes per word gives an estimate of **information per morpheme** — which should be more stable across languages.

### 3.3 Mandatory Information Load by Type

The four types of mandatory information are quantified as:

$$L_{\text{type}} = \frac{\text{tokens carrying mandatory type markers}}{\text{total tokens in top-200}}$$

posterior means from Beta-binomial updating (as in §3.6 of 0.1.2.md). The **total mandatory load** is:

$$L_{\text{total}} = \sum_{\text{types}} L_{\text{type}}$$

with the caveat that epistemic, ontological, categorical, and spatial metadata are qualitatively different and summing them involves a category assumption (see §5: Limitations).

### 3.4 Gricean Surplus

For each mandatory information type, the Gricean surplus is:

$$S_G(i, \text{type}) = L_i^{\text{type}} - \min_{j} L_j^{\text{type}}$$

where the minimum is taken over all languages. This represents the amount of information language $i$ forces beyond what any language finds cooperatively necessary. A surplus of 0 means the language's mandatory load is the bare minimum; a high surplus means the grammar imposes substantial "extra" information.

### 3.5 Design Space Mapping

We construct a 4-dimensional design space with axes:
1. Information density ($H$, Shannon entropy)
2. Epistemic load ($L_{\text{epistemic}}$)
3. Ontological load ($L_{\text{ontological}}$)
4. Morphological complexity (ordinal 1–5)

We then perform Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to visualize the 2-dimensional projection that captures maximal variance, and test for:
- **Empty regions:** Are there combinations of values that no language occupies?
- **Clustering:** Do languages of the same morphological type cluster together?
- **Boundaries:** Are the boundaries sharp (suggesting hard constraints) or gradual (suggesting tendencies)?

### 3.6 Greenbergian Correlation Testing

We construct a correlation matrix of mandatory loads across the 22 languages and test specific Greenbergian predictions:

| Prediction | Variables | Expected sign |
|:-----------|:----------|:-------------:|
| Gender -> Number marking | $L_{\text{gender}}$ vs. number morphology | Positive |
| Classifier -> Number marking | $L_{\text{classifier}}$ vs. number morphology | Negative |
| SOV order -> Case marking | Word order type vs. case marking load | Positive |
| Evidentiality -> Epistemic modality | $L_{\text{evidential}}$ vs. modal marking | Positive |

### 3.7 Reproducibility

All computations use the existing frequency data from `0.3_results.json` (seed = 42). New analyses are deterministic (no additional MCMC required for entropy, correlations, PCA). Seed and protocol from §3.8 of 0.1.2.md remain in effect.

## 4. Expected Results

Based on known typological patterns and information-theoretic principles:

### 4.1 Information Density Gradient

We expect a clear gradient in Shannon entropy:
- **Polysynthetic** ($H \approx 6.5$–$7.0$ bits): highest entropy, flattest distributions
- **Agglutinative** ($H \approx 6.0$–$6.5$ bits): moderate-high entropy
- **Fusional** ($H \approx 5.5$–$6.0$ bits): moderate entropy
- **Isolating** ($H \approx 5.0$–$5.5$ bits): lowest entropy, most concentrated

Normalized entropy should show that no language approaches the theoretical maximum ($H_{\text{norm}} = 1.0$), confirming that all languages concentrate information in a subset of word-forms — a universal of human communication.

### 4.2 Mandatory Load Upper Bound

Total mandatory load should cluster below 10–12% across all languages. Languages with high epistemic load (Quechua, Tibetan) should not also have high ontological load — the mandatory information budget appears to be limited. This would be evidence for an information-theoretic constraint on the design of grammatical systems.

### 4.3 Compression-Tax Trade-Off

The correlation between entropy and total mandatory load should be negative. Languages that pack more information per word (higher entropy, richer morphology) should require less explicit category marking — the morphology itself carries the categorization. Languages with simpler morphology should compensate with explicit marking.

### 4.4 Design Space Boundaries

The PCA projection should reveal a bounded habitable zone with empty regions in the corners. Specifically:
- No language should occupy (high epistemic load, high ontological load, high spatial load) simultaneously
- High-entropy languages should cluster in low-mandatory-load regions
- Low-entropy languages should show higher variance in mandatory load (more diverse strategies)

### 4.5 Greenbergian Validations

Known typological correlations should be recoverable from the mandatory load data, validating that frequency-based measures capture genuine typological structure.

## 5. Limitations

- **Synthetic data:** All frequencies are simulated, not from real corpora. The information-theoretic measures are only as valid as the synthetic distributions.
- **"Word" is not cross-linguistically valid:** The fundamental unit of analysis varies across writing systems and morphological types. Entropy comparisons across types should be interpreted cautiously.
- **Information type incommensurability:** Summing epistemic, ontological, categorical, and spatial loads into a "total mandatory load" involves a category assumption that these are comparable kinds of information. They may not be.
- **Written language bias:** Written corpora systematically differ from spoken language in frequency distributions, especially for agglutinative and polysynthetic languages.
- **Small sample:** 22 languages underrepresent the world's $\sim$7,000 languages. Design space boundaries are suggestive, not definitive.
- **LLM-derived priors:** The morphological-type means used to generate synthetic data came from LLM parametric knowledge, which is biased toward well-resourced languages.

## Appendix A: The Four Theoretical Lenses

### A.1 Jakobson: Mandatory vs. Optional Encoding

Jakobson observed that translation reveals what languages *must* encode. Translating "It rained" into Turkish requires choosing between $-di$ (witnessed) and $-miş$ (non-witnessed). The English sentence contains no evidential information; the Turkish sentence must. This is not about cognition — it's about the **information specification requirements** of the grammatical system.

### A.2 Shannon: Information as Measurable Quantity

Shannon entropy provides a language-independent metric for information density. Two languages may both obey Zipf's law but have different entropies, reflecting different strategies for packaging information into surface forms. Entropy is additive (unlike the Zipf exponent, which is a parameter of a specific functional form), making it suitable for cross-linguistic comparison.

### A.3 Grice: The Cooperative Principle as Baseline

Grice's maxims define optimal communication: be as informative as required, not more, not less. But grammatical obligatoriness forces speakers to violate the Maxim of Quantity — they must provide more information than a cooperative speaker would volunteer. The Gricean surplus quantifies this systematic "over-informativeness" that grammar imposes on communication.

### A.4 Greenberg: The Design Space Is Constrained

Greenberg's universals are statements about what combinations of features can and cannot co-occur in human languages. They imply that the design space of possible languages is not uniformly populated — some regions are empty because they violate processing or information-theoretic constraints. Mapping the actual design space tests whether frequency-based measures recover the same constraints that traditional typology identifies.
