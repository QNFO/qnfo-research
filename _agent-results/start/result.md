## Result

## Energy-Efficiency Benchmarking of Quantum Computers: Corpus and Current External Work

**1. The QNFO corpus defines the joules-per-solution (JPCUB) metric as the universal, physics-grounded benchmark.** The P0 paper (`joules-per-solution-metric`, DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.21637028) defines computational advantage as the ratio of total system-level energy required to produce a correct solution at a specified correctness threshold, covering computation, cooling, power conversion, networking, and amortized manufacturing. It argues that gate-model quantum computing operates at roughly 10¹²–10¹⁵ times the Landauer limit per logical operation, and that existing domain-specific benchmarks (quantum volume, FLOPs, PUE) provide no cross-domain comparability.

**2. The corpus prices quantum error correction thermodynamically.** `jpcub-qec-landauer` (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.22117282) derives a Landauer floor of (n−k)/k · kT·ln2 per logical qubit per round for active (measurement-based) QEC, verified across repetition, surface, and constant-rate code families. The floor scales linearly in n for repetition codes, quadratically in distance for surface codes, and is constant for constant-rate families — correction overhead does not converge to zero as codes improve.

**3. The corpus's competitive landscape ranks 17 platforms by estimated joules-per-solution.** `jpcub-competitive-landscape` (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.21821767) applies a consistent system-level power model to the task of factoring N=15 via Shor's algorithm. Gate speed — not cooling cost, qubit count, or fidelity — dominates the ranking: trapped-ion platforms rank lowest despite room-temperature operation because microsecond-scale gate times overwhelm their power advantage; neutral atoms emerge as the most balanced architecture. IBM Eagle is the only platform with a published measurement at 0.89 J/solution; all other values are model-derived estimates, not empirical measurements.

**4. External 2025–2026 work is converging on energy-efficiency frameworks for quantum hardware.** Carrasco-Codina et al. (arXiv:2605.15090, May 2026) define energy efficiency as the ratio of algorithms performed per unit time over energy consumed, analyze five physical platforms (superconducting, silicon spin, trapped ions, neutral atoms, photonic), and propose a benchmarking framework for future architectures. Calero et al. (Nature Reviews Physics 7:352–353, 2025, DOI: 10.1038/s42254-025-00846-0) argue that quantum solutions are evaluated on performance and qubit count but not energy, and call for energy-aware circuit design and execution.

**5. The external literature still lacks an agreed metric, though theoretical foundations are emerging.** Chen (Nature Computational Science 3:457–460, 2023, DOI: 10.1038/s43588-023-00459-6) notes that experts have not agreed on metrics for quantum energy consumption. Meier and Yamasaki (arXiv:2305.11212) provide a theoretical framework linking energy consumption with quantum computational complexity, proving conditions for energy-consumption advantage. Alves, Pezzutto, and Omar (arXiv:2601.03141, Jan 2026) extend this to Rydberg-atom platforms, and Fellous-Asiani et al. (arXiv:2209.05469) propose the Metric-Noise-Resource framework for full-stack quantum resource optimization.
