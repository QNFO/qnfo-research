# Convergence, Consilience, and the Hierarchical Architecture of Reality: A Corrected Meta-Analysis with Worked Examples from Interdisciplinary Physics

## Abstract

Scientists in disconnected fields repeatedly rediscover the same mathematical structures — gauge symmetry, effective field theory, universal critical exponents — and each rediscovery costs duplicated effort while suggesting that the space of viable theories is tightly constrained. Whether that suggestion is strong enough to guide research strategy depends on how carefully the convergences are characterized and how honestly their evidential status is stated. This paper is a corrected and extended version of a prior meta-analysis [5] that argued convergence (nature independently producing similar forms across lineages) and consilience (independent lines of knowledge converging on the same truths) are symmetric faces of a hierarchically organized reality shaped by attractors in possibility space. Three errata are remediated: an unsupported claim of a systematic scan of over four hundred prior releases is withdrawn; a claim that gallery cases share an "exact" mathematical identity is corrected to distinguish exact structural identity from shared universality class; and a status table resting on the retracted scan is replaced by explicitly labeled assessments. As a new quantitative contribution, the paper derives, with full arithmetic, the extremal bounds for degree-based topological indices of trees [1] and shows that distinct optimization problems converge on the same internal-degree-4 architecture: for $n$ pendent vertices, $M_1 \geq 9n-16$ (even $n$) and $M_2 \geq 11n-27$ ($n \geq 8$), with extremal internal-vertex counts $m = (n-2)/2$ and $m = (n-1)/2$ derived and verified by degree-sum checks; for $n = 10$ the bounds evaluate to $M_1 \geq 74$ and $M_2 \geq 83$. The result changes practice by replacing an unverifiable self-audit with a reproducible one and by sharpening what counts as evidence for a constrained possibility space.

## 1. Introduction

A working condensed-matter physicist who rediscovers in graphene the renormalization-group machinery that particle physicists built for quantum electrodynamics has lost months to duplicated labor and gained a piece of evidence: the same mathematics keeps appearing wherever the physics is similar. Multiply this across gauge theory, effective field theory, critical phenomena, holography, and non-equilibrium growth, and a pattern emerges that individual researchers feel but rarely formalize. The pattern has a practical stake. If convergent rediscovery reflects genuine attractors in the space of possible theories, then a researcher choosing where to invest effort should treat structures that have already been reached from several independent directions as likely stable — and should treat a framework that has not been reached from multiple directions with more suspicion. If instead the convergences are artifacts of shared ancestry, diffusion of ideas, or selective attention, that investment heuristic fails and duplicated effort is simply waste to be minimized by better communication. The decision that changes if this paper's corrected argument holds is: when is a convergence strong enough to bet on?

The prior version of this analysis [5] argued that convergence and consilience are symmetric — convergence is the ontological signature of attractors in possibility space, consilience the epistemological program that reconstructs them — and supported the argument with a gallery of five interdisciplinary physics cases. That version, however, contained defects that undermine its own evidential standards, and this paper remediates them. Two readings of the remediation task are possible: a narrower reading under which the corpus-scan claim and the status table that depends on it are corrected as a single erratum, and a broader reading under which each independently unsupported claim is remediated as its own erratum. We take the broader reading, under the convention that each claim lacking reproducible support is a distinct defect requiring its own corrective operation; this yields three errata:

1. **The retracted corpus-scan claim.** Section 1 of [5] asserted that "a systematic scan of over four hundred prior releases in the author's research corpus confirmed" that the paper's vocabulary is shared across a coherent multi-year program. No reproducible audit methodology was given, and the claim cannot be verified by a reader. This paper withdraws the claim and replaces it with an explicitly labeled assumption and a proposal for a reproducible audit (Sections 3 and 7). We take the retracted status of this claim under the convention that only a reproducible audit can support a corpus-level assertion; the alternative reading, under which the scan is treated as confirmed without a stated methodology, is rejected because it would reintroduce the very defect being corrected.

2. **The overstrong identity claim.** Section 2.3 of [5] promised that every gallery case involves "the mathematical identity of the converged-upon structure" being "exact," yet its own Section 4.5 describes the KPZ interface-growth equation and the complex Ginzburg-Landau equation as sharing a "universality class" and "the same universal scaling laws" — a weaker and correct relation. Sharing a universality class means flowing to the same renormalization-group fixed point and hence sharing universal exponents, not sharing an identical equation. This paper introduces an explicit three-tier taxonomy — exact identity, shared universality class, and structural analogy — and reclassifies each gallery case under it (Section 3).

3. **The unsupported status table.** Section 8 of [5] presented a table with entries labeled "Status (from systematic prior-release review)," including "polygenesis confirmed absent from all prior releases." Since the underlying scan is retracted, these status entries are unsupported. This paper replaces the table with assessments labeled as working judgments, not audit results (Section 3).

Beyond remediation, the paper adds a quantitative worked example that the prior version lacked. The extremal theory of degree-based topological indices [1] supplies theorems with explicit numerical bounds whose derivations can be reproduced line by line, and whose structure — distinct optimization problems converging on the same internal-degree-4 tree architecture — is itself a small, checkable instance of the convergence phenomenon the paper analyzes. The derivations appear in Section 4 and the computed values in Section 5.

**Contributions.**

- A remediation of the three errata of [5], with the retracted systematic-scan claim withdrawn and replaced by an explicit assumption and an audit proposal (Sections 1, 3, and 7).
- A three-tier taxonomy separating exact mathematical identity from shared universality class from structural analogy, with each gallery case of [5] reclassified under it (Section 3).
- Full arithmetic derivations of the Zagreb-index lower bounds $M_1 \geq 9n-16$ (even $n$), $M_1 \geq 9n-15$ (odd $n$), and $M_2 \geq 11n-27$ ($n \geq 8$) for trees with $n$ pendent vertices, including the internal-vertex counts $m = (n-2)/2$ and $m = (n-1)/2$ of the extremal trees, verified by degree-sum checks, with the $n = 10$ values $M_1 \geq 74$ and $M_2 \geq 83$ evaluated explicitly (Sections 4 and 5).
- A demonstration that the $M_1$- and $M_2$-optimization problems, though differently posed, converge on the same qualitative extremal architecture, offered as a worked instance of structural convergence (Sections 4 and 6).

## 2. Background and Related Work

This section situates the paper in the literature available to it. The bibliography contains five works; all five are discussed, and the consequence of this small base for the paper's claims is addressed in Section 6.

**[5] Convergence, Consilience, and the Hierarchical Architecture of Reality.** The document under revision defines convergence as the independent origin of similar traits, forms, or ideas in distinct lineages, cultures, or minds, and consilience as the convergence of independent lines of evidence on the same truths, and argues the two are symmetric faces of a hierarchically organized reality shaped by attractors in possibility space as modeled by the renormalization group. Its gallery of five cases spans gauge theory (independent invention by Weyl, by Yang and Mills, and by Shaw), effective field theory (Fermi's four-fermion interaction and Landau-Ginzburg theory as precursors of the modern EFT framework), universality (identical critical exponents $\beta \approx 0.33$, $\gamma \approx 1.24$, $\nu \approx 0.63$ across magnets, fluids, alloys, and superfluids), quantum geometry (black-hole entropy, the holographic principle, and AdS/CFT), and non-equilibrium dynamics (KPZ interface growth and the complex Ginzburg-Landau equation). It also treats the superdeterministic challenge: if all variables are pre-correlated from the initial singularity, the "independent" lines of evidence were never independent, and consilience risks becoming a closed loop of mutual reinforcement; the document's resolution is pragmatist — the attractor landscape remains mathematically real and we proceed as though consilience tracks causal structure. The present paper keeps this framework and argument structure but corrects the three evidential defects listed in Section 1, and adds the quantitative worked example that [5] lacks.

**[1] Minimizing Degree-based Topological Indices for Trees with Given Number of Pendent Vertices + Erratum.** This work provides the paper's quantitative core. In mathematical chemistry, topological indices such as the first and second Zagreb indices, $M_1(G) := \sum_{v \in V(G)} d_G(v)^2$ and $M_2(G) := \sum_{uv \in E(G)} d_G(u)\, d_G(v)$, are used to predict properties of chemical compounds, and a standard problem is to find lower bounds of an index over a set of graphs and characterize the extremal graphs. For trees with a fixed number $n$ of pendent vertices (vertices of degree 1), [1] proves Theorem 1: $M_1(T) \geq 9n - 16$ for even $n$, with equality for a "4-tree" in which every internal vertex has degree 4, and $M_1(T) \geq 9n - 15$ for odd $n$, with equality for a tree whose internal vertices all have degree 4 except one of degree 3. It proves Theorem 3: for $n \geq 8$ pendent vertices, $M_2(T) \geq 11n - 27$, with equality when each stem vertex (an internal vertex incident to pendent vertices) has degree 4 or 5 while other internal vertices have degree 3. The introduction of [1] also records the background fact that over trees of fixed order the chain minimizes the Zagreb indices while the star maximizes them, and that extremal graphs typically sit on the "boundary" of the admissible set. The paper uses these theorems in Section 4 as a worked, checkable instance of structural convergence: two differently posed optimization problems reach the same qualitative architecture.

**[2] The Decryption Key: Cognitive Architecture as the Hidden Radix of Physical Measurement.** This work argues that every SI base unit traces to a feature of human cognitive architecture, formalized as a measurement cross-ratio $(A, B; C, D)$ of physical phenomenon, mathematical divisor, metrological artifact, and human observer, which is invariant only conditional on the observer point $D$ being held fixed; its conclusion is that we have "mistaken anthropocentric invariance for observer-independent invariance," and it proposes the adelic product formula as the genuinely observer-independent invariant, invoking Ostrowski's theorem that the only non-trivial absolute values on $\mathbb{Q}$ are the real and the $p$-adic ones. The paper cites [2] as a worked example of systematic bias across independent measurement traditions; the inference that this illustrates convergence tracking a shared observer feature is the paper's own interpretation. Second, its cross-ratio formalism gives a concrete model of what "independence of viewpoints" must mean before consilience claims can be evaluated.

**[3] The Substrate Is the Algorithm: A Categorical Formalization of the Strongest Consilience Thread.** This work formalizes the claim that substrate determines capability, setting physical substrates as objects of compact closed monoidal categories following the Baez–Stay correspondence, and defining a property as *intrinsically protected* on a substrate $S$ iff it is invariant under the full endomorphism monoid $\mathrm{End}(S)$, with Lemma 1 establishing that protection equals endomorphism-invariance and error correction being active exactly when the protected property is not in the fixed-point set of $\mathrm{End}(S)$. It reports that the thread passed a red-team audit "verbatim across all five pillars" on 2026-07-25, with instances spanning problem-substrate mapping, silent-radix notation, adelic quantum error correction using the $\mathbb{Q}_p$ topology, a $v_p^{\max}$ classification distinguishing valuations (with the figures $v_p^{\max} = 28$ versus $4$), and pattern-process inseparability. The paper cites [3] as a model of what a *verified* consilience claim looks like: a cross-pillar convergence subjected to an explicit adversarial procedure, in contrast to the unreproducible corpus scan retracted here. Its fixed-point language also connects directly to the attractor ontology of [5]: an intrinsically protected property is one that survives all free processes of the substrate, which is the categorical analogue of an RG fixed point surviving coarse-graining.

**[4] The Computable Real Boundary: Where Physics Ends and Cognitive Fiction Begins.** This work argues that the boundary between physics and what lies beyond it coincides with the computable reals $\mathbb{R}_{\text{comp}}$, building on Spencer-Brown's calculus of indications (the mark of distinction and the enclosure, with the reduction rules of Calling and Crossing) and constructing number systems from finite sequences of distinctions; its conclusion defines physics as "the fixed point of Re-entry under finite enclosure operations," identifying that fixed point with $\mathbb{R}_{\text{comp}}$ and classifying everything beyond it — non-computable reals, non-measurable sets, unfalsifiable formal systems — as Cognitive Fiction: internally consistent but externally unverifiable. The paper cites [4] for a boundary condition on the consilience program: a convergence of formalisms counts as evidence of world-structure only if the converged-upon structure is reachable from finite acts of distinction; a "convergence" on an unfalsifiable formal system has, by [4]'s own criterion, no external verifiability and so cannot serve as the kind of evidence [5] claims for its gallery. This gives the superdeterminism discussion of Section 6 a sharper edge: the pragmatist resolution must operate within the computable boundary to retain any operational content.

**Scope of the literature base.** These five works are the entire bibliography available to this paper. Section 6 states explicitly what this limitation means for the strength of the conclusions.

## 3. Methods

The paper's method is conceptual analysis plus erratum remediation plus worked arithmetic derivation. No experiment, survey, corpus audit, or measurement was performed; every quantitative claim is either derived with shown arithmetic in Section 4 or attributed to a cited work.

**3.1 Remediation method.** Each erratum is handled by one of three operations. (i) *Retraction with replacement*: a claim with no reproducible support is withdrawn and replaced either by an explicitly labeled assumption or by a proposal for how support could be generated. The retracted claim is the systematic scan of "over four hundred prior releases" in [5]; we replace it with the working assumption that the author's research corpus shares a common vocabulary, labeled as an assumption and not as an audit finding, and we propose a reproducible audit in Section 7. (ii) *Weakening to the correct relation*: a claim of exact identity is replaced by the weakest relation the evidence supports. The gallery cases are reclassified under the taxonomy of Section 3.2. (iii) *Relabeling*: status entries that depended on the retracted scan are relabeled as working judgments. The table of Section 8 of [5] is therefore not reproduced as an audit result; its content survives only as labeled judgment, and the specific entry "polygenesis confirmed absent from all prior releases" is withdrawn outright, since absence claims require exactly the audit that cannot be cited.

**3.2 The three-tier taxonomy.** We take, under the convention that the weakest supportable relation is the correct one to claim, the following classification of convergence strength:

- **Tier 1 — Exact mathematical identity.** The independently derived structures are the same mathematical object: same defining equations, same symmetry group, same solution space. Example from the gallery of [5]: the non-abelian gauge structures of Yang–Mills and of Shaw, which [5] describes as a "mathematically identical structure."
- **Tier 2 — Shared universality class.** The structures are distinct mathematical objects that flow to the same renormalization-group fixed point, and therefore share universal quantities (critical exponents, scaling functions) but not their microscopic or even their equation-level form. Example: KPZ interface growth and the complex Ginzburg-Landau equation, which [5] itself describes as sharing "the same universal scaling laws" — the correct relation, and the one this paper now claims for that case.
- **Tier 3 — Structural analogy.** The structures share a pattern of organization (e.g., "write down the most general functional consistent with symmetries and keep the relevant operators") without a theorem guaranteeing shared fixed points or identity. Example: Fermi's four-fermion contact interaction and Landau-Ginzburg free-energy functionals as precursors of effective field theory, where the shared element is the organizing principle rather than a proven equivalence.

Each gallery case of [5] is assigned the weakest tier its own description supports: gauge theory, Tier 1; effective field theory, Tier 3 at the historical-convergence stage (with Tier 1 status only after the modern EFT framework subsumed both); universality and holography, Tier 1 at the level of the unifying framework (the RG explanation; the AdS/CFT duality); non-equilibrium dynamics, Tier 2. The erratum is thereby resolved: the promise of exact identity in [5] Section 2.3 is replaced by the requirement that each case state its tier, and the Section 4.5 case is stated at Tier 2.

**3.3 Derivation method for the worked example.** The quantitative core uses only the definitions and theorems of [1]. Inputs: the definitions $M_1(T) := \sum_{v} d_T(v)^2$ and $M_2(T) := \sum_{uv \in E(T)} d_T(u)\, d_T(v)$; the fact that a tree with $N$ vertices has $N - 1$ edges and hence degree sum $\sum_v d_T(v) = 2(N-1)$; and Theorems 1 and 3 of [1]. From these we derive, in Section 4, the internal-vertex counts of the extremal trees and verify the $M_1$ bounds by direct computation of $\sum_v d_v^2$ on the extremal degree sequences. All arithmetic is shown step by step. The extension of these results to the convergence thesis (that distinct optimization problems converge on the same architecture) is an interpretation, labeled as such, not a theorem.

**3.4 Assumptions.** We assume: (A1) the author's research corpus shares a common vocabulary across releases — an assumption replacing the retracted scan claim, carrying no quantitative content; (A2) the gallery cases of [5] are historically independent in the sense [5] describes (documented separate research traditions), which we take over from [5] rather than re-verify; (A3) the theorems of [1] hold as stated there; we re-derive their consequences but do not re-prove them.

## 4. Analysis

This section carries out the derivations. Every input number is stated with its source; every arithmetic step is shown.

**4.1 Setup and inputs.** From [1]: a tree $T$ has $N$ vertices and $N - 1$ edges; a pendent vertex has $d_T(v) = 1$; the first Zagreb index is $M_1(T) = \sum_{v \in V(T)} d_T(v)^2$ and the second is $M_2(T) = \sum_{uv \in E(T)} d_T(u)\, d_T(v)$. Theorem 1 of [1]: for any tree with $n \geq 2$ pendent vertices, $M_1(T) \geq 9n - 16$ if $n$ is even (equality for a 4-tree, all internal vertices of degree 4) and $M_1(T) \geq 9n - 15$ if $n$ is odd (equality when all internal vertices have degree 4 except one of degree 3). Theorem 3 of [1]: for $n \geq 8$, $M_2(T) \geq 11n - 27$, with equality when each stem vertex has degree 4 or 5 and other internal vertices have degree 3.

**4.2 Internal-vertex count, even case.** Let $m$ be the number of internal vertices, so $N = m + n$. The degree sum of a tree with $N$ vertices is $2(N-1)$, since $\sum_v d_v = 2|E| = 2(N-1)$. For a 4-tree, each of the $m$ internal vertices has degree 4 and each of the $n$ pendent vertices has degree 1:

$$4m + n = 2(m + n - 1).$$

Expanding the right side: $2m + 2n - 2$. So

$$4m + n = 2m + 2n - 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2m = n - 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m = \frac{n-2}{2}.$$

A solution in integers requires $n$ even, consistent with the theorem's even-case hypothesis. For $n = 10$: $m = (10-2)/2 = 4$, $N = 14$.

**4.3 Verification of the even-case bound by direct computation.** For the 4-tree with $n = 10$, $m = 4$: the degree sequence is four vertices of degree 4 and ten of degree 1. Then

$$M_1 = \sum_v d_v^2 = 4 \cdot 4^2 + 10 \cdot 1^2 = 4 \cdot 16 + 10 = 64 + 10 = 74.$$

The bound of Theorem 1 gives $9n - 16 = 9 \cdot 10 - 16 = 90 - 16 = 74$. Equality holds, as the theorem states for the 4-tree. As a further check, the degree sum: $4 \cdot 4 + 10 \cdot 1 = 16 + 10 = 26$, and $2(N-1) = 2 \cdot 13 = 26$. Consistent.

**4.4 Internal-vertex count, odd case.** For odd $n$, the extremal tree has $m$ internal vertices of which $m - 1$ have degree 4 and one has degree 3. The degree sum:

$$4(m-1) + 3 + n = 2(m + n - 1).$$

Left side: $4m - 4 + 3 + n = 4m + n - 1$. Right side: $2m + 2n - 2$. So

$$4m + n - 1 = 2m + 2n - 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2m = n - 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m = \frac{n-1}{2}.$$

Integer solutions require $n$ odd, consistent with the hypothesis. For $n = 9$: $m = (9-1)/2 = 4$, $N = 13$.

**4.5 Verification of the odd-case bound.** For $n = 9$, $m = 4$: three internal vertices of degree 4, one of degree 3, nine pendent vertices of degree 1.

$$M_1 = 3 \cdot 4^2 + 1 \cdot 3^2 + 9 \cdot 1^2 = 3 \cdot 16 + 9 + 9 = 48 + 18 = 66.$$

The bound: $9n - 15 = 81 - 15 = 66$. Equality holds. Degree-sum check: $3 \cdot 4 + 3 + 9 = 12 + 12 = 24$, and $2(N-1) = 2 \cdot 12 = 24$. Consistent.

**4.6 The $M_2$ bound.** Theorem 3 of [1] states $M_2(T) \geq 11n - 27$ for $n \geq 8$; we compute the bound values and verify the theorem's structural claim is compatible with the degree-sum identity, without re-proving the induction on which [1] relies. For $n = 8$: $11 \cdot 8 - 27 = 88 - 27 = 61$. For $n = 9$: $11 \cdot 9 - 27 = 99 - 27 = 72$. For $n = 10$: $11 \cdot 10 - 27 = 110 - 27 = 83$. For $n = 12$: $11 \cdot 12 - 27 = 132 - 27 = 105$. Compatibility check for $n = 9$: the extremal structure has each stem vertex of degree 4 or 5 and other internal vertices of degree 3. Let there be $s$ stem vertices and $r$ other internal vertices, $m = s + r$. A stem of degree 4 has 3 pendent neighbors and 1 internal neighbor; a stem of degree 5 has 4 pendent neighbors and 1 internal neighbor. Counting pendent vertices through stem edges: $n = \sum_{\text{stems}} (d_s - 1)$. With all stems of degree 4, $n = 3s$, so $s = n/3$ when $n$ is divisible by 3; for $n = 9$, $s = 3$ stems of degree 4, and the remaining internal vertices have degree 3. Degree sum: $3 \cdot 4 + r \cdot 3 + 9 = 2(m + 9 - 1)$ with $m = 3 + r$: left side $12 + 3r + 9 = 21 + 3r$; right side $2r + 22$. So $21 + 3r = 2r + 22$, giving $r = 1$, $m = 4$, $N = 13$ — the same vertex count as the odd-case $M_1$-extremal tree of Section 4.4. This coincidence is the observation Section 4.7 interprets.

**4.7 Convergence of extremal architectures.** The two optimization problems differ: $M_1$ penalizes squared degrees summed over vertices; $M_2$ penalizes products of degrees summed over edges; their constraints differ (even/odd $n$ for $M_1$; $n \geq 8$ for $M_2$). Yet their extremal trees share a qualitative architecture: internal vertices of degree 4 dominate (all internal vertices in the even $M_1$ case; all but one in the odd case; all stems in the $M_2$ case), and for $n = 9$ both problems are optimized by trees with $m = 4$ internal vertices and $N = 13$ vertices. We interpret this as a small worked instance of the paper's thesis: the space of degree-sequence optimization problems has an attractor at internal degree 4, reached independently by two different cost functionals. The interpretation is labeled as interpretation, not as a theorem: nothing in [1] asserts that the two optimization problems are related, and the coincidence at $n = 9$ is established here only for the degree-sum counts, not by exhibiting a single tree that simultaneously attains both bounds. The claim that a simultaneous extremal tree exists for $n = 9$ is therefore a hypothesis that the enumeration proposed in Section 7 would test.

**4.8 Summary of derived quantities.** The derivations above yield, for the record: $m = (n-2)/2$ (even-$n$ $M_1$-extremal 4-tree), $m = (n-1)/2$ (odd-$n$ $M_1$-extremal tree), $M_1 = 74$ attained at $n = 10$, $M_1 = 66$ attained at $n = 9$, $M_2 \geq 61, 72, 83, 105$ at $n = 8, 9, 10, 12$, and the structural coincidence $m = 4$, $N = 13$ for both problems at $n = 9$. Each value follows from the inputs of Section 4.1 by the arithmetic shown; none is measured, simulated, or imported from outside [1] and the degree-sum identity.

## 5. Results

The results are exactly the quantities derived in Section 4, restated with their derivations attached.

- **Result R1.** For any tree with $n = 10$ pendent vertices, $M_1(T) \geq 74$ (Theorem 1 of [1], even case: $9 \cdot 10 - 16 = 74$), and the bound is attained by the 4-tree with $m = 4$ internal vertices, whose degree sequence gives $M_1 = 4 \cdot 16 + 10 = 74$ (Section 4.3).
- **Result R2.** For any tree with $n = 9$ pendent vertices, $M_1(T) \geq 66$ (Theorem 1 of [1], odd case: $9 \cdot 9 - 15 = 66$), attained by the tree with three internal vertices of degree 4, one of degree 3, and nine pendent vertices, giving $M_1 = 48 + 9 + 9 = 66$ (Section 4.5).
- **Result R3.** For any tree with $n \geq 8$ pendent vertices, $M_2(T) \geq 11n - 27$ (Theorem 3 of [1]); evaluated: $M_2 \geq 61$ at $n = 8$, $M_2 \geq 72$ at $n = 9$, $M_2 \geq 83$ at $n = 10$, $M_2 \geq 105$ at $n = 12$ (Section 4.6).
- **Result R4.** The extremal internal-vertex counts are $m = (n-2)/2$ for even $n$ and $m = (n-1)/2$ for odd $n$, derived from the degree-sum identity $4m + n = 2(m+n-1)$ and its degree-3 variant (Sections 4.2 and 4.4).
- **Result R5.** At $n = 9$, both the $M_1$- and $M_2$-optimization problems are compatible with extremal trees having $m = 4$ internal vertices and $N = 13$ vertices (Sections 4.4 and 4.6); whether a single tree attains both bounds simultaneously is unresolved here and is the target of the proposed enumeration (Section 7).
- **Result R6.** Under the three-tier taxonomy of Section 3.2, the gallery cases of [5] reclassify as: gauge theory, Tier 1; effective field theory, Tier 3 historically; universality and holography, Tier 1 at the framework level; non-equilibrium dynamics (KPZ and the complex Ginzburg-Landau equation), Tier 2. No case requires the withdrawn blanket claim of exact identity.

No result in this section reports a measurement, simulation, or survey; all numbers trace to the arithmetic of Section 4 or to the theorems of [1] as cited.

## 6. Discussion

The paper's central interpretive claim is that the extremal architectures of the $M_1$- and $M_2$-optimization problems of [1] constitute a small, checkable instance of structural convergence, and that the three-tier taxonomy of Section 3.2 sharpens what the gallery of [5] can claim. Both claims carry limitations that this section states explicitly.

**6.1 What the worked example does and does not establish.** The convergence observation of Section 4.7 rests on two facts: both optimization problems favor internal degree 4, and at $n = 9$ both are compatible with trees having $m = 4$ internal vertices and $N = 13$ vertices. What it does not establish is a shared mechanism. In [1], the $M_1$ bound is proved by an optimization over degree sequences subject to the degree-sum identity, and the $M_2$ bound is proved by induction on $n$ using a generalized notion of an attached tree whose cost is interrelated with $M_2$; the two proofs use different techniques, and nothing in [1] asserts a relation between the problems. The shared architecture could therefore reflect a common underlying constraint — the degree-sum identity $4m + n = 2(m + n - 1)$, which forces the average internal degree toward 4 as $n$ grows — rather than two independent "discoveries" of the same attractor. Under the taxonomy of Section 3.2, this is the analogue of common descent: the two problems share the constraint set, and the shared extremal form may be inherited from it rather than independently reached. The convergence interpretation is strongest if the shared architecture is shown to persist under cost functionals that do not share the degree-sum route to degree 4; the enumeration proposed in Section 7 is designed to test this.

**6.2 The simultaneous-extremal hypothesis.** Section 4.7 leaves open whether a single tree with $n = 9$ pendent vertices attains both $M_1 = 66$ and $M_2 = 72$ simultaneously. The compatibility established in Section 4.6 is only at the level of vertex counts: the $M_2$-extremal structure at $n = 9$ has $s = 3$ stem vertices of degree 4 and $r = 1$ further internal vertex of degree 3, matching the $M_1$-extremal count. But the degree sequences must also be realizable by the same tree, and the stem-edge structure required for $M_2$-minimality (each stem incident to exactly 3 pendent vertices and 1 internal vertex, per the equality condition of Theorem 3 of [1]) constrains the adjacency in ways the count alone does not fix. If no single tree attains both bounds, the convergence claim weakens from "the same architecture" to "the same vertex-count profile," a strictly weaker observation; if such a tree exists, the claim strengthens to a shared extremal object. This is a decidable question by finite enumeration, and its outcome is the cleanest falsifier of the paper's interpretive claim.

**6.3 The taxonomy's dependence on [5]'s own descriptions.** The tier assignments of Section 3.2 are derived from the descriptions [5] gives of its own cases, not from independent verification of the history or the mathematics. We assume (A2 of Section 3.4) that the cases are historically independent as [5] describes. If any case fails that independence — for instance, if diffusion between research traditions can be documented where [5] claims none — the tier assignment collapses to the homology branch of Section 2.3 of [5], and the case ceases to be evidence for attractors at all. The taxonomy also inherits a subtler limitation: Tier 1 ("exact mathematical identity") is claimed for gauge theory on the strength of [5]'s description of the Yang–Mills and Shaw structures as "mathematically identical," but identity of the defining equations does not by itself guarantee identity of the physical content; the interpretation of a gauge structure depends on the sector in which it is deployed. The taxonomy classifies the mathematical objects, not their physical readings, and this restriction should be kept in view when the classifications are used.

**6.4 The small literature base.** The bibliography available to this paper contains five works, all discussed in Section 2. This is a limitation in two directions. First, the convergence thesis of [5] is supported here only by [5]'s own gallery and by the single worked example of Section 4; no independent literature on convergence in scientific theory choice, on universality in statistical mechanics beyond what [5] reports, or on the history of gauge theory is available to corroborate or challenge the case histories. Second, the remediation of the errata is checked only against the texts of [5] and [1]; a broader literature could supply the reproducible-audit methodology that Section 7 proposes, or precedents for the three-tier taxonomy, that this paper cannot cite. Every conclusion of the paper should therefore be read as conditional on the five-work base; the quantitative results (R1–R5) are unaffected, since they derive from [1] alone, but the interpretive claims (R6 and the convergence reading of Section 4.7) are not independently corroborated.

**6.5 What would falsify the main result.** The main interpretive result is the convergence reading of Section 4.7. It would be falsified by either of two observations. First, an enumeration of all trees with $n = 9$ pendent vertices that shows no tree simultaneously attains $M_1 = 66$ and $M_2 = 72$ — this would reduce the claimed convergence to a coincidence of vertex counts explainable by the shared degree-sum constraint, i.e., the homology alternative of Section 6.1. Second, a demonstration that the degree-4 architecture is an artifact of the degree-sum identity alone, in the sense that any cost functional minimized subject to $\sum_v d_v = 2(N-1)$ with $n$ pendent vertices is forced toward internal degree 4 regardless of its form; under that demonstration, the two problems would not be independent discoverers of an attractor but two instances of a single constraint, and the worked example would illustrate constraint, not convergence. The quantitative results R1–R4 are theorems of [1] plus arithmetic and are not falsifiable by these observations; only the interpretation R5 and the taxonomy-based R6 are.

**Limitations.** The results of this paper rest on the following assumptions, with sensitivity noted for each:

- **(A1) Common corpus vocabulary.** This assumption replaces the retracted scan claim and carries no quantitative content; no result depends on it, so the sensitivity is zero. It matters only for the framing of the paper as continuous with a research program.
- **(A2) Historical independence of the gallery cases.** Taken over from [5] without re-verification. Result R6 and the convergence interpretation of Section 4.7 are fully sensitive to it: if independence fails for a case, that case drops out as evidence.
- **(A3) The theorems of [1] hold as stated.** Results R1–R5 are fully sensitive to this; an error in Theorem 1 or Theorem 3 of [1] would propagate to every bound. The degree-sum checks of Sections 4.3 and 4.5 verify internal consistency of the extremal degree sequences but do not re-prove the theorems' minimality claims.
- **(A4) The weakest-supportable-relation convention.** The taxonomy of Section 3.2 assigns each case the weakest tier its description supports. A different convention — assigning the strongest tier the description is consistent with — would raise the effective claims of [5] but reintroduce the overstatement the erratum corrects. R6 is sensitive to this convention choice.
- **(A5) The convergence interpretation is not a theorem.** Nothing in [1] relates the two optimization problems; the interpretation of Section 4.7 is labeled as interpretation and is falsifiable per Section 6.5.

## 7. Practical Implications and Engineering Pathways

**(a) Who would use the result and what changes.** The users are authors and editors of self-published or rapidly released research corpora who need to issue errata without an audit infrastructure, and researchers who use convergence arguments to allocate effort across fields. For the first group, the remediation method of Section 3.1 — retraction with replacement, weakening to the correct relation, relabeling — is a reusable procedure: it converts an unverifiable self-audit into an explicitly labeled assumption plus a proposal for reproducible support, and it replaces a blanket identity claim with a tiered one. What changes for them is the standard: a status claim in a release is either backed by a stated, reproducible methodology or labeled as a working judgment, with no intermediate category. For the second group, the taxonomy of Section 3.2 changes the decision of Section 1 ("when is a convergence strong enough to bet on?") into a checkable question: a convergence supports research investment at Tier 1 (exact identity), supports it more weakly at Tier 2 (shared universality class, where microscopic differences may still matter), and supports it least at Tier 3 (structural analogy, where no theorem guarantees the shared pattern extends). The worked example of Sections 4 and 6 supplies a template for how to test a convergence claim at the smallest scale: identify the shared constraint, ask whether the constraint alone explains the convergence, and design an enumeration or calculation that distinguishes the two.

**(b) Current readiness.** This work is purely analytical and has not been peer reviewed; under the NASA Technology Readiness Level definitions it sits at TRL 1–2: basic principles observed and reported (the theorems of [1], the taxonomy, the erratum method), with the convergence interpretation formulated but not yet subjected to the enumeration that would test it. No claim of applied or validated status is made; the remediation method has been exercised exactly once, on [5], and its generalization to other corpora is untested.

**(c) The smallest next test.** The smallest decisive calculation is an exhaustive enumeration of all trees with $n = 9$ pendent vertices ($N = 13$ vertices, 12 edges), computing $M_1$ and $M_2$ for each and checking whether any tree attains both $M_1 = 66$ and $M_2 = 72$. The enumeration is finite and small: the number of trees on 13 labeled vertices is large, but the restriction to trees with exactly 9 pendent vertices and the degree constraints (internal degrees in $\{3, 4\}$ for the $M_1$-extremal candidates; stems of degree 4 or 5, other internal vertices of degree 3, for the $M_2$-extremal candidates) reduces the candidate set to trees whose degree sequences are those enumerated in Sections 4.4 and 4.6. The outcome that refutes the convergence interpretation is: no degree sequence realizable as a tree attains both bounds simultaneously. A study performing this enumeration would need only a standard graph-enumeration library and the degree-sequence realizability test (the Erdős–Gallai condition); no hardware, dataset, or human subject is involved.

**(d) Binding constraints.** For the enumeration of (c), the binding constraint is combinatorial: the candidate set must be generated from degree sequences satisfying the constraints above, and each sequence must be tested for tree realizability and then for both index values; the compute cost is negligible at $n = 9$ but grows with $n$, so extending the simultaneous-extremal test to larger $n$ is limited by enumeration cost, not by any conceptual barrier. For the reproducible audit proposed to replace the retracted scan claim, the binding constraint is data access: the audit requires the full text of the corpus releases and a stated matching methodology (e.g., vocabulary-overlap thresholds fixed in advance), neither of which this paper can supply. For the taxonomy, the binding constraint is historical: verifying Tier 1 or Tier 2 assignments requires documentary evidence of independence of research traditions, which is outside the paper's scope and is inherited as assumption (A2).

**(e) Order-of-magnitude bounds.** No cost or performance projections are offered: the paper derives no cost inputs, and the enumeration of (c) is small enough that its compute cost is not a meaningful bound. The only quantitative projections are the bound values themselves, already derived in Section 4.6 and restated as Results R1–R4; extending the $M_2$ evaluation to other $n$ uses the linear formula $M_2 \geq 11n - 27$ directly, with no additional assumptions.

**(f) Risks and misuse paths.** The taxonomy can be misused in the opposite direction from [5]'s error: an author could label a convergence Tier 1 on the strength of a superficial similarity of equations, using the taxonomy's vocabulary to lend unearned rigor to a weak analogy. The mitigation is the requirement, built into Section 3.2, that the tier be the weakest the evidence supports and that the evidence be stated; a tier assignment without a stated evidential basis is not a valid use of the taxonomy. A second misuse is treating the convergence interpretation of Section 4.7 as established: it is labeled as interpretation and is falsifiable per Section 6.5, and citing it as a confirmed instance of attractors would repeat, at small scale, the overstatement the errata correct.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper remediated three errata in [5] — the retracted corpus-scan claim, the overstrong exact-identity claim, and the unsupported status table — by withdrawing what lacked reproducible support, replacing it with explicitly labeled assumptions and a three-tier taxonomy (exact identity, shared universality class, structural analogy), and relabeling the dependent status entries as working judgments. It added a quantitative worked example drawn from [1]: full derivations of the extremal internal-vertex counts $m = (n-2)/2$ (even $n$) and $m = (n-1)/2$ (odd $n$) for the $M_1$-minimal trees, verification of the bounds $M_1 \geq 9n - 16$ (even $n$) and $M_1 \geq 9n - 15$ (odd $n$) by direct computation at $n = 10$ ($M_1 = 74$) and $n = 9$ ($M_1 = 66$), evaluation of the $M_2$ bound $M_2 \geq 11n - 27$ at $n = 8, 9, 10, 12$ ($61, 72, 83, 105$), and the observation that at $n = 9$ both optimization problems are compatible with extremal trees of $m = 4$ internal vertices and $N = 13$ vertices. The convergence reading of that observation is offered as a falsifiable interpretation, with the simultaneous-extremal enumeration of Section 7 as its decisive test. The paper's lasting contribution is methodological: a convergence claim is only as strong as the tier it is honestly assigned, and a self-audit is only as good as the reproducible procedure behind it.
