Open Questions That Led Me
From Fault-Tolerant QEC
to p-Adic Metrology

I'm not presenting answers. I'm presenting puzzles I couldn't solve — and one experiment I tried as a result.
CWI Summer School on Quantum Algorithms & Quantum Error Correction · 24–28 August 2026 · Poster Session: Wed 26 Aug

Things I've been wondering about (and can't find clear answers to)

QUESTION ①
The Energy Wall
Landauer's principle says erasing one bit costs energy — kT ln 2 at minimum. Each round of active error correction — syndrome measurement, decoding, feedback — erases information. I'm struggling to see how the total energy cost doesn't scale with the number of rounds and qubits. Is there an accepted scaling law for the thermodynamic cost of fault-tolerant computation at utility scale? If so, where is it published? I've been looking and haven't found it.
QUESTION ②
The Classical Shadow
The decoder must process syndrome data and return corrective actions within the coherence time. As qubit numbers grow, this becomes a real-time processing task with its own energy and latency footprint. At what scale does the classical co-processor's own dissipation become a significant error source? I don't understand the full system-level energy budget — does anyone in this room?
QUESTION ③
The Geometry of Noise
In many physical platforms — spin systems, cold atoms, photonic clusters — noise correlations exhibit nested, hierarchical structure. This is an ultrametric property: errors within a branch look similar to each other and different from errors in other branches. Standard error models largely assume independent or short-range correlated noise. If noise already self-organizes hierarchically, are we leaving a source of passive resilience unused by not designing codes or sensors that match that geometry?
QUESTION ④
Why a Circuit?
The circuit model digitizes continuous quantum evolution into discrete gates. Active QEC then enforces that digital abstraction against physical drift. In other areas of physics, when a natural system requires intense external control just to stay in the model, it's often a sign the model isn't the best fit. Is there a deep reason the circuit model is forced upon us — or is it a historical choice we've stopped questioning?
QUESTION ⑤
What Would Falsify the Roadmap?
The timeline has been "roughly ten years" since the mid-1990s. I'm not arguing it's wrong. I'm asking: what empirical result would demonstrate that the fault-tolerant digital QC roadmap needs a fundamental revision rather than more time and money? What is the falsification condition? If we don't have one, how do we distinguish a research programme from a belief?

Where the questions led me

The Ultrametric Triangle Inequality
Noise organizes into nested clusters that a single global measurement washes out automatically.
No ancillas. No syndrome extraction. No feedback loop. Error resilience by geometry.

The Experiment (Not an Answer)

These questions made me wonder: what if we stopped fighting noise and started reading it in its native geometry? In a p-adic (ultrametric) framework, noise clusters hierarchically. A carefully chosen global measurement averages local fluctuations automatically.

This is a sensor architecture, not a computer. It exploits exactly the noise structure that active QEC treats as an enemy — using ~10 entangled photons at room temperature, no dilution fridge, no error correction.

Preliminary data: beating the standard quantum limit in a room-temperature photonic platform. I'm not claiming this scales to universal computation. I'm claiming it already beats classical sensors with hardware that exists today.

What a Toy Model Suggested — and Why I Don't Trust It Yet

The geometry-of-noise question led me to try something small: a back-of-envelope model of an error-correcting code living on a tree-like (ultrametric) structure. The model suggested something surprising: redundancy might not grow smoothly with fidelity — it seemed to grow in discrete steps.

But here's the part I'd rather tell you than hide: when I tested my model's most interesting-looking feature — a gap where redundancy vanishes — it didn't survive the test. It looks like an artifact of how I built the model, not real physics.

I'm not claiming this is real. I'm asking: has anyone here seen something like this — or a reason it must be wrong? That's the kind of question I came to have answered.

If noise has structure we can exploit passively, why is the entire field betting on active correction?
I don't know the answer. I'm hoping someone in this room does.
