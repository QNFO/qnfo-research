## 0. Why This Curriculum Blueprint Exists

The v1 and v2 blueprint documents (0.1.md, 0.2.md) established WHAT a beginner's guide to quantum computing should say in 2026: the thermodynamic wall is real, the standard platforms share a common bottleneck, and the ultrametric alternative achieves error thresholds up to 75× higher than surface codes.

This document establishes HOW to teach it — the chapter outline, learning progression, difficulty tiers, and annotated bibliography that maps all 47 source materials (35 PDFs + 12 QWAV papers) into a coherent curriculum.

### 0.1 What Makes This Guide Different

Every existing quantum computing beginner's guide follows the same pattern: introduce qubits, explain superposition and entanglement with the Bloch sphere, walk through a few quantum gates, present Grover's and Shor's algorithms as proofs of concept, and end with an optimistic note about the NISQ era. The message is: "Quantum computing is revolutionary and just around the corner."

This guide will be different. It will:

1. **Start with the bottleneck, not the promise.** Chapter 1 is not "What is a qubit?" — it is "Why don't we have quantum computers yet?" The thermodynamic wall is introduced on day one.

2. **Present two paradigms side by side.** Standard quantum computing (active QEC, Euclidean lattices, surface codes) AND the ultrametric alternative (passive fault tolerance, Bruhat-Tits trees, geometric confinement). Neither is presented as "the answer" — both are presented as active research programs with different assumptions and different evidence bases.

3. **Teach uncertainty tagging as a core skill.** Every claim is tagged [EST], [PROP], [GAP], [SPEC], or [OPEN]. The reader learns to distinguish established fact from framework proposal from acknowledged gap — a skill that is valuable far beyond quantum computing.

4. **Cite real sources with real DOIs.** No claim appears without a traceable reference. The annotated bibliography (Section 4) maps every source to its role in the curriculum.

5. **Make falsifiable predictions the organizing principle.** The three QWAV experiments (E1, E2, E3) serve as the guide's narrative spine: here are predictions that can be wrong, here is how to test them, here is what it means if they pass or fail.

## 2. Chapter Outline — The Revolutionary Beginner's Guide

### Part I: The Honest Landscape (Chapters 1-3)

**Chapter 1: Why Don't We Have Quantum Computers Yet?**
- The forty-year promise: a brief history of unfulfilled timelines
- What "quantum computer" actually means in 2026: experimental physics apparatus, not a computer
- The NISQ era: what it is, what it isn't, why we're still in it
- Learning objectives: distinguish hype from reality; understand why timelines keep slipping
- Source materials: T0.1, T0.2, T1.5, T1.7, T1.9
- Confidence tags introduced: [EST], [PROP], [GAP], [SPEC], [OPEN]

**Chapter 2: The Six Platforms — And Why None Has Won**
- Superconducting qubits: the incumbent (fast, mature, stuck on cooling)
- Trapped ions: the precision champion (slow, high-fidelity, shuttling bottleneck)
- Neutral atoms: the scaling surprise (rapid qubit growth, fidelity trailing)
- Photonic: the room-temperature hope (no fault-tolerant demo yet)
- Silicon spin: the semiconductor play (early, leverages existing fabs)
- Topological: the high-risk gamble (Microsoft Majorana 1, disputed claims)
- Comparative table: qubit counts, gate fidelities, coherence times, key limits
- Learning objectives: compare qubit platforms on multiple axes; understand why platform diversity is rational
- Source materials: T1.1, T1.2, T1.3, T2.1

**Chapter 3: The Thermodynamic Wall — The Bottleneck Nobody Talks About**
- Active quantum error correction: what it is and why it's necessary
- The surface code: how it works, what it costs
- The cooling gap: 20,000× between 4K (1W) and mK (50$\mu$W) cooling
- Why this is a hard physical limit (Carnot efficiency), not an engineering problem
- The surface code plateau: Google 2023 $→$ Google 2025 — improvement, yes; breakthrough, no
- Learning objectives: understand why error correction is THE bottleneck; grasp the thermodynamic argument
- Source materials: T1.4, T3.1, T3.2

### Part II: The Standard Curriculum — What Every Guide Teaches (Chapters 4-7)

**Chapter 4: Qubits, Superposition, and the Bloch Sphere**
- The classical bit vs. the quantum bit
- Superposition: what it means (and doesn't mean)
- The Bloch sphere: a geometric picture of a single qubit
- Measurement: collapse, Born rule, and why you can't clone
- Learning objectives: define and manipulate single-qubit states; understand measurement
- Source materials: T2.1, standard references
- Note: This chapter follows the standard curriculum — the critique comes later.

**Chapter 5: Entanglement and Multi-Qubit Systems**
- Tensor products: combining qubits
- Entanglement: correlations that classical physics cannot explain
- Bell states, GHZ states, and the EPR paradox
- Quantum circuits: gates, wires, and measurement
- Learning objectives: construct multi-qubit states; explain entanglement without mysticism
- Source materials: T2.1, T3.3

**Chapter 6: Quantum Algorithms — The Promise**
- Deutsch-Jozsa: the simplest quantum speedup
- Grover's search: quadratic speedup for unstructured search
- Shor's algorithm: exponential speedup for factoring — and why it threatens RSA
- The catch: resource requirements for Shor's algorithm at cryptographically relevant scale
- Learning objectives: understand the structure of quantum algorithms; calculate resource requirements
- Source materials: T2.14, T2.2
- Confidence tag: [EST] for algorithm correctness; [GAP] for practical implementation

**Chapter 7: Quantum Error Correction — How It's Supposed to Work**
- The no-cloning theorem and why classical error correction fails
- Stabilizer codes: detecting errors without measuring the state
- The surface code: architecture, thresholds, decoding
- Magic state distillation: the hidden cost of universal gate sets
- Learning objectives: understand stabilizer formalism; calculate surface code overhead
- Source materials: T1.4, T3.1

### Part III: The Reality Check — What the Standard Curriculum Misses (Chapters 8-10)

**Chapter 8: The Surface Code Plateau**
- Google 2023: error suppression demonstrated, but marginal improvement
- Google 2025: below-threshold achieved — but "below threshold" $\neq$ "usefully low"
- The gap between $d=5$ and $d=11$: why extrapolation doesn't work
- Physical qubit overhead: 241 per distance-11 logical qubit (surface code) vs. ~40 (tree code equivalent)
- Decoding complexity: $O(N^3)$ for surface codes vs. $O(s \log s)$ for tree codes
- Learning objectives: critically evaluate surface code roadmaps; distinguish demonstrated from projected performance
- Source materials: T1.4, T3.1, T3.2

**Chapter 9: The Cryogenic Arithmetic**
- Dilution refrigerator physics: how mK cooling actually works
- Cooling budget: 50$\mu$W at the mixing chamber, 1W at 4K
- Heat per syndrome measurement: why active QEC generates unsustainable heat
- Wall power at scale: 240 kW for 10,000 logical qubits, 24 MW for 1 million
- Why this is Carnot-limited, not engineering-limited
- Learning objectives: calculate thermodynamic constraints on QEC scaling; distinguish physical limits from engineering challenges
- Source materials: T3.1, T3.2

**Chapter 10: What Quantum Computing Actually Delivers Today**
- Quantum sensing: the underrated success (NV centers, SQUIDs, atomic magnetometers)
- Quantum annealing: D-Wave's real-world optimization advantage (early 2026)
- Post-quantum cryptography: the urgent priority (NIST standards finalized 2024)
- Quantum communication: QKD networks and satellite links
- What does NOT deliver today: factoring, molecular simulation, quantum ML
- Learning objectives: identify commercially viable quantum technologies vs. research-stage technologies
- Source materials: T1.5, T1.6, T1.7, T2.3, T2.6, T2.7, T2.8, T2.9

### Part IV: The Geometric Alternative — A Different Kind of Computing (Chapters 11-14)

**Chapter 11: Geometry Matters — Archimedean vs. Ultrametric**
- The hidden assumption: all of quantum computing assumes Euclidean (Archimedean) geometry
- What "Archimedean" means: distances add linearly, small errors accumulate
- What "ultrametric" means: the strong triangle inequality $d(x,z) \leq \max(d(x,y), d(y,z))$
- Trees as natural ultrametric spaces: balls are nested or disjoint, never partially overlap
- Why this matters for error correction: errors in disjoint subtrees cannot interact
- Learning objectives: define ultrametric spaces; contrast with Euclidean geometry; understand geometric error confinement
- Source materials: T3.1, T3.2, T3.4, T3.5, T5.1

**Chapter 12: The Bruhat-Tits Tree — Geometry Becomes a Tree**
- $p$-adic numbers ($\mathbb{Q}_p$): an alternative way to measure distance
- The Bruhat-Tits tree $\mathcal{T}_p$: the geometric realization of $p$-adic space
- Vertices as lattice classes, boundary as $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q}_p)$
- The tree is discrete, hierarchical, and purely combinatorial
- Why this makes it directly implementable as a hardware topology
- Learning objectives: describe the Bruhat-Tits tree; explain why it's a natural QEC substrate
- Source materials: T3.4, T5.1, T5.2, T5.3, T5.4

**Chapter 13: The Tree Code — Error Correction Without Measurement**
- Perfect tensors on tree vertices: the $[[3,1,1]]$ base code
- Concatenation: how tree depth creates logical qubits
- Holographic encoding: bulk logical qubits $→$ boundary physical qubits
- Passive fault tolerance: why errors are geometrically confined to subtrees
- No syndrome measurements: the tree structure IS the error correction
- Learning objectives: construct a tree code; calculate encoding rate and decoding complexity; explain passive fault tolerance
- Source materials: T4.1, T4.2, T4.3
- Confidence tags: [PROP] for thresholds; [GAP] for full quantum validation

**Chapter 14: The Threshold Advantage — By the Numbers**
- Bit-flip threshold: BTQP 50.0% vs. surface code ~10.9% (4.6×)
- Depolarizing threshold: BTQP 75.0% vs. surface code ~1.0% (75×)
- Independent $X+Z$ threshold: BTQP 17.30%
- Encoding rate: $1 - 1/p$ (constant) vs. $k/n \to 0$ (vanishing)
- Physical qubits for distance-11 equivalent: ~40 vs. 241
- Energy per logical qubit: <1% of surface code
- Validation: zero logical errors in 500 trials at $p_{\text{err}} = 0.40$, $d \geq 3$
- Learning objectives: compare error thresholds quantitatively; evaluate validation methodology
- Source materials: T4.1, T3.1, T3.5
- Confidence tags: [PROP] — classically validated, not yet quantum-validated

### Part V: The Falsifiable Science — How to Test the Alternative (Chapters 15-17)

## 3. Learning Pathways — Pick Your Depth

Not every reader needs to reach Tier 5. The curriculum supports three pathways:

### Pathway A: The Informed Citizen (Tiers 0-2, ~30 hours)
**Who:** Policy makers, investors, journalists, curious technologists
**Goal:** Understand what quantum computing is, why it matters, and why the standard story is incomplete
**Chapters:** 1-10 (skip Chapter 7's mathematical detail, read Chapters 8-10 for the reality check)
**Deliverable:** Ability to distinguish quantum computing hype from reality in media and investment contexts

### Pathway B: The Technical Practitioner (Tiers 0-3, ~60 hours)
**Who:** Software engineers, data scientists, STEM graduate students
**Goal:** Understand quantum computing formalism AND the thermodynamic critique
**Chapters:** All of Parts I-III (Chapters 1-10), plus Chapter 11 (geometry introduction)
**Deliverable:** Ability to write and run quantum circuits, understand error correction overhead, and explain why standard platforms face a thermodynamic wall

### Pathway C: The Researcher (Tiers 0-5, ~120 hours)
**Who:** Physics and mathematics graduate students, quantum computing researchers
**Goal:** Master the ultrametric alternative and contribute to its development
**Chapters:** All 20 chapters
**Deliverable:** Ability to implement tree codes, calculate thresholds, design validation experiments, and contribute to the open problems

- **Difficulty:** Tier 5

## 6. What This Blueprint Does NOT Cover

This is a **curriculum blueprint** — it describes WHAT to teach, in WHAT order, at WHAT depth, and from WHICH sources. It does NOT contain the actual guide content. That is the next deliverable (0.4.md+).

Specifically not covered here:
- **Full chapter text.** The 20-chapter guide described here has not yet been written.
- **Exercises and problem sets.** Only learning objectives are specified; actual problems need to be designed.
- **Figures and diagrams.** The Bruhat-Tits tree, tree code architecture, and thermodynamic wall need visual representations.
- **Interactive elements.** Quantum circuit simulators, threshold calculators, and tree code visualizers would enhance the learning experience.

These are the components that turn this blueprint into an actual guide.
