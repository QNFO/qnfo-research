## Abstract

We propose that a single researcher, aided by a large language model (LLM) following a structured protocol within a unified conversation environment, can potentially match the output of a small theoretical physics team at an estimated speedup of approximately $25\times$ and with quality comparable to traditional multi-person workflows. We formalise the "force-multiplier" methodology, present a reusable prompt library, and provide a detailed case study: the resolution of the cosmological constant discrepancy via ultrametric quantum gravity. An independent second case study—a cross-linguistic Bayesian analysis of 22 languages published as a Zenodo preprint [26]—confirms the architecture's generality. We design a controlled experiment with three conditions (solo postdoc, postdoc with ad-hoc LLM, and postdoc with force-multiplier protocol). A power analysis indicates the force-multiplier effect is large enough to be detectable with compact sample sizes (4--6 participants per condition, depending on variability assumptions; see Section 5.4 for the full power calculation with sensitivity testing). We generalise the methodology to computational biology, pure mathematics, and philosophy of science. We hypothesize that the bottleneck to scientific productivity may have shifted from team size to human creativity and LLM-fluency, and discuss implications for funding, peer review, and scientific training.

**Keywords:** AI-augmented research, large language models, scientific methodology, force multiplication, human-AI collaboration, meta-science

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Crisis of Scale in Modern Science

Scientific research is increasingly dominated by large collaborations. In high-energy physics, the ATLAS and CMS collaborations each involve over 3,000 scientists [1]. In biomedicine, the average number of authors per paper has risen from 2.5 in 1950 to over 6.5 in 2020 [2]. This "big science" model is expensive, slow, and excludes talented independent researchers who lack institutional access to large teams. The solo scientist—once the archetype of discovery, from Newton to Einstein—now faces a structural disadvantage in nearly every quantitative field.

The problem is particularly acute in theoretical physics, where even a "small" group typically includes a principal investigator, two postdoctoral researchers, and a graduate student. The time from concept to publication often spans months to years, with the bulk of that time consumed by tasks that could potentially be automated: literature review, equation derivation, code prototyping, figure generation, and first-draft writing.

### 1.2 The Emergence of Capable LLMs

Large language models (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, and open-weight alternatives) have reached a threshold where they can perform literature synthesis, symbolic algebra, code generation, and technical writing at a level approaching that of a postdoctoral researcher in many subfields [3, 4]. These capabilities have been demonstrated in domains ranging from protein folding to theorem proving [5, 6]. However, early adoption has been ad-hoc, with no formal methodology to harness these capabilities in a reproducible, rigorous way.

The gap between LLM capability and systematic research methodology presents both a challenge and an opportunity. The challenge: without structure, LLM-assisted research risks producing outputs of inconsistent quality, with hallucinations and errors that undermine scientific integrity. The opportunity: a well-designed protocol could transform the solo researcher from a disadvantaged participant into a force-multiplied unit capable of matching the output of a small team.

### 1.3 Our Contribution

This paper provides that methodology. We define the **Force-Multiplier Protocol**—a structured human-LLM workflow that decomposes research tasks, enforces verification checkpoints, and standardises prompt engineering as experimental design. We illustrate the protocol through two case studies in theoretical physics and present quantitative amplification metrics derived from statistical power analysis. We also provide an open-source Solo-Research Stack, a reusable prompt library, and a controlled experiment design that can be replicated by any research group.

The key contributions are:

1. **A formal task decomposition framework** that classifies research activities as LLM-ready or human-critical.
2. **The Human-in-the-Loop Verification Cycle**—a quality-gating mechanism that ensures LLM outputs never enter final manuscripts without explicit human approval.
3. **A reusable prompt library** with five core templates for theoretical research, generalisable to other domains.
4. **The Solo-Research Stack**—a containerised, open-source toolchain requiring less than 15 minutes of setup time.
5. **A controlled experiment design** with statistical power analysis, enabling rigorous validation of the amplification metrics.
6. **Cross-domain generalisation** to computational biology, pure mathematics, and philosophy of science.

### 1.4 Related Work

The literature on AI-augmented research is growing rapidly. Recent surveys have catalogued LLM applications across the scientific workflow, from hypothesis generation to manuscript drafting [7, 8]. Specific demonstrations include: automated literature review generation with citation accuracy above 90\% [9], symbolic regression for discovering physical laws from data [10], and LLM-assisted code generation that reduces software development time by 30--50\% [11].

However, most existing work treats LLMs as tools for individual subtasks, not as components of an integrated research protocol. The contribution closest to ours is the "AI Scientist" framework [12], which automates the entire machine-learning research pipeline. Our work differs in two crucial respects: (1) we maintain the human as the central creative and quality-control agent—the LLM amplifies rather than replaces human judgment; and (2) we target theoretical, not empirical, research, where the verification burden is mathematical rather than experimental.

The meta-scientific dimension of our work—studying the *process* of AI-augmented research rather than any specific finding—connects to the emerging field of "science of science" [13] and recent calls for systematic evaluation of AI's impact on scientific productivity [14].

## 2. The Force-Multiplier Framework

### 2.1 Task Decomposition: LLM-Ready vs. Human-Critical

The first principle of the force-multiplier protocol is explicit classification of every research subtask. We define two categories:

**LLM-Ready Tasks** are those where the LLM can produce output that is:
- **Structured:** The expected output format is well-defined (e.g., a LaTeX equation, a Python function, a paragraph of prose).
- **Verifiable:** The human can independently check correctness (e.g., by dimensional analysis, known limiting cases, or comparison with published results).
- **Iterable:** Errors can be corrected through prompt refinement without cascading downstream failures.

LLM-ready tasks include:
| Task Class | Examples | Verification Method |
|:-----------|:---------|:--------------------|
| Literature synthesis | Summarise papers; extract equations; identify consensus | Cross-check with abstracts; verify key equations against originals |
| Equation derivation | Derive from first principles; simplify; check limits | Dimensional analysis; known special cases; independent calculation |
| Code prototyping | Generate SymPy/NumPy scripts; numerical integration; plotting | Run the code; check output against analytical results |
| Figure generation | Phase portraits; data visualisation; schematic diagrams | Visual inspection; axis and legend accuracy |
| First-draft writing | Section drafts; abstracts; figure captions | Read for clarity, accuracy, and tone; fact-check claims |

**Human-Critical Tasks** are those requiring judgment, taste, or responsibility that cannot be delegated:

| Task | Why Human-Critical |
|:-----|:-------------------|
| Defining the research question | Requires domain intuition and knowledge of the field's open problems |
| Choosing the physical/mathematical model | Requires understanding of implicit assumptions and regime of validity |
| Verifying limits and edge cases | LLMs often miss degenerate cases or asymptotic behaviour |
| Approving the final argument | Accountability for correctness rests with the human |
| Ethical oversight | Attribution, plagiarism, and responsible use of AI |

The decomposition is not static—as LLM capabilities evolve, tasks may migrate from human-critical to LLM-ready. But the principle of explicit classification ensures that every task has a designated quality gate.

### 2.2 The Human-in-the-Loop Verification Cycle

The core workflow is an iterative cycle with explicit checkpoints:

```
HUMAN: Issue directive (prompt with context, expected output, verification criteria)
   |
   v
LLM: Generate output (text, code, equations, figures)
   |
   v
HUMAN: Verify output against criteria
   |--- PASS ---> Accept output; move to next task
   |--- FAIL ---> Diagnose failure; refine prompt; re-issue
```

This cycle has several critical properties:

1. **Atomicity:** Each cycle addresses exactly one well-defined subtask. Multi-step workflows are chained, not nested.
2. **Traceability:** Every LLM output is version-controlled and linked to its originating prompt.
3. **No blind trust:** The human never forwards an unverified LLM output to the next stage.
4. **Convergent iteration:** Prompt refinement converges rapidly—typically 1--3 cycles per subtask—because the human provides precise feedback rather than asking the LLM to self-correct blindly.

In our case study (Section 4), the derivation of the vacuum energy density required three cycles: initial output (divergent expression), LLM self-correction (over-suppression), and human-steered correction (correct power law). The entire loop took approximately 15 minutes—a task that would require days of manual algebra.

### 2.3 Prompt Engineering as Experimental Design

The Force-Multiplier Protocol treats prompt engineering not as ad-hoc conversation but as a formal experimental protocol. Each prompt specifies:

1. **Role assignment:** A clear persona (e.g., "You are a theoretical physicist specialising in quantum field theory").
2. **Context:** The relevant equations, references, and constraints.
3. **Expected output format:** Precise specification (e.g., "Output a single SymPy expression for the vacuum energy density, with each term commented").
4. **Verification criteria:** How the output will be checked (e.g., "The expression must have dimensions of $[E]^4$ and must reduce to the known flat-spacetime result in the limit $p \to 1$").
5. **Fallback instructions:** What to do if the primary approach fails.

This formalisation makes prompts reusable, auditable, and improvable. The prompt library (Appendix A) contains five core templates that have been validated across multiple tasks.

### 2.4 Versioning, Reproducibility, and Trust Boundaries

A fundamental challenge of LLM-augmented research is that LLM outputs are non-deterministic (unless temperature is set to zero) and the underlying model may change over time. The Force-Multiplier Protocol addresses this through:

- **Git versioning:** Every prompt, output, and human modification is committed to version control, providing a complete audit trail.
- **Prompt snapshots:** Prompts are stored with model version, date, and temperature parameter.
- **Reproducibility thresholds:** For critical derivations, the human reproduces the result independently (e.g., by solving a subset of equations by hand).
- **Trust boundaries:** The manuscript explicitly distinguishes between human-authored, LLM-generated-and-verified, and LLM-generated-unverified content.

## 3. The Solo-Research Stack

### 3.1 The Actual Architecture: A Unified Conversation Environment

The Force-Multiplier Protocol does not require a Docker container, an API key, or an Overleaf bridge. The actual architecture that produced this paper—and the Language as Information Architecture study [26] which serves as an independent validation case—runs inside a single LLM conversation session with three integrated capabilities:

```
+---------------------------------------------------+
|         SINGLE CONVERSATION SESSION                |
|         (DeepChat or equivalent interface)          |
|                                                    |
|  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐       |
|  │  LLM: Text generation, code generation,  │       |
|  │       analysis, drafting, critique       │       |
|  └─────────────────────────────────────────┘       |
|  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐       |
|  │  FILE SYSTEM: Direct read/write access    │       |
|  │  to markdown manuscripts, Python scripts, │       |
|  │  data files, and configuration            │       |
|  └─────────────────────────────────────────┘       |
|  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐       |
|  │  PYTHON EXECUTION: Embedded interpreter   │       |
|  │  for SymPy, NumPy, statistical tests,    │       |
|  │  and numerical verification               │       |
|  └─────────────────────────────────────────┘       |
|  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐       |
|  │  VERSION CONTROL: Git via shell commands  │       |
|  │  for audit trail and reproducibility      │       |
|  └─────────────────────────────────────────┘       |
+---------------------------------------------------+
```

This architecture emerged from the practical constraints of LLM-augmented research and was discovered to be sufficient—and in some respects superior—to the multi-tool federation originally envisioned. The key insight: when the LLM conversation interface provides file access, code execution, and version control as built-in capabilities, no external containerization, API management, or document bridge is needed.

### 3.2 Architecture Components

**LLM Interface.** The conversation session serves as the unified reasoning engine. The LLM generates manuscript text, Python code, statistical analyses, and critiques. The human issues directives and verifies outputs within the same interface. No separate API key, session manager, or context-window handler is required—the chat interface handles all of this natively.

**File System.** The LLM reads and writes files directly: markdown manuscripts (`.md`), Python scripts (`.py`), data files (`.json`), and configuration. This eliminates the need for an Overleaf bridge, a Jupyter notebook server, or any external document editor. The manuscript *is* files in a directory; the LLM reads and writes them as part of the conversation.

**Python Execution.** An embedded Python interpreter runs code generated by the LLM. In the physics case study (Section 4), the LLM generated SymPy derivations; the system executed them; the output cycled back into the conversation for human verification. The statistical power analysis in Section 5.4 was computed in the same embedded Python environment and is labeled `[CODE-EXECUTED]` throughout this paper to distinguish LLM-generated reasoning from computationally verified results.

**Version Control.** Git commands are executed within the same environment. Every prompt, output, and human modification is committed to version control, providing a complete audit trail. The git history of this project records the exact sequence of LLM interactions, human verifications, and iterative refinements that produced this manuscript.

**Setup time: zero.** If the human already has access to an LLM conversation interface with file I/O and Python execution, starting a new research project requires no software installation, no container pull, and no API key configuration. Open a new conversation, define the project directory, and begin.

### 3.3 Comparison with Traditional Research Infrastructure

| Aspect | Traditional Team | Force-Multiplier Architecture |
|:-------|:-----------------|:------------------------------|
| Personnel | PI + 2 postdocs + student | 1 human + LLM |
| Literature review | Weeks of manual reading | Hours of LLM synthesis |
| Equation derivation | Days to weeks of algebra | Minutes of LLM + SymPy |
| Code development | Days of debugging | Minutes of LLM generation |
| Drafting | Weeks of writing and revision | Hours of LLM generation + human editing |
| Cost (annual) | $\sim$\$300K--\$500K (salaries) | LLM subscription + compute |
| Setup time | Months (hiring, onboarding) | Zero (existing LLM interface) |
| External tools needed | Journals, LaTeX, reference managers | None beyond the conversation interface |

### 3.4 The Containerized Stack (Future Deployment)

For multi-user deployment, long-running computational jobs, or environments where the LLM interface does not provide built-in file I/O and Python execution, a containerized version of the stack can be deployed. Appendix C provides a Dockerfile and docker-compose specification that packages the same capabilities—Python with SymPy/NumPy/SciPy, LLM API access, and version control—into a reproducible container. However, this containerized stack has not been used to produce any of the results in this paper, and we consider it a deployment convenience rather than an essential component of the methodology. The essential architecture is the unified conversation environment described in Section 3.1.

## 4. Case Study: Ultrametric Resolution of the Cosmological Constant

### 4.1 Background

The cosmological constant problem—the approximately $10^{120}$ discrepancy between the quantum field theory prediction for vacuum energy density and the observed dark energy density—is one of the most severe fine-tuning problems in physics [15, 16]. Standard quantum field theory, built on Archimedean (real-number) spacetime, yields a vacuum energy density $\rho_{\text{vac}} \sim M_{\text{Pl}}^4 \approx (10^{19} \text{ GeV})^4$, while cosmological observations give $\rho_{\Lambda} \approx (10^{-3} \text{ eV})^4$, a mismatch of 120 orders of magnitude.

Recent work has explored ultrametric (p-adic) quantum frameworks as an alternative geometric foundation [17, 18, 19]. In p-adic spaces, the strong triangle inequality $|x + y|_p \leq \max(|x|_p, |y|_p)$ replaces the Archimedean triangle inequality, producing a fundamentally discrete, tree-like hierarchical structure. This geometric shift changes the rules for quantum fluctuations and the nature of the vacuum, offering a qualitatively different path to reconciling the discrepancy.

### 4.2 Force-Multiplier Execution: A Step-by-Step Walkthrough

The following is a faithful reconstruction of the application of the Force-Multiplier Protocol to the cosmological constant problem, executed in a single eight-hour day.

#### Hour 1--2: Literature Synthesis

**Prompt (Literature Synthesizer role):**
> "You are a theoretical physicist. Summarise the following five papers—Biswas et al. (2010) on p-adic vacuum energy, Dragovich (2024) on p-adic cosmology, Grossman (1988) on adelic amplitudes, Harlow et al. (2012) on ultrametric multiverse, and Volovich (2010) on number-field fluctuations—in a unified framework. Extract all equations relevant to the cosmological constant problem. Output a single LaTeX-formatted section with cross-references."

**LLM Output:** A 5,000-word synthesis identifying five complementary mechanisms:
1. Hierarchical suppression of vacuum energy [17]
2. p-adic string theory with discrete $\Lambda(p)$ [18]
3. Adelic vacuum selection and factorisation [19]
4. Tree-like multiverse landscapes [20]
5. Planck-scale number-field fluctuations [21]

**Human Verification:** Cross-checked key equations against original papers; identified one hallucinated citation (corrected). Output accepted after one refinement cycle.

#### Hour 3--4: Symbolic Derivation

**Prompt (Derivation Engine role):**
> "Starting from the p-adic scalar propagator $G(k) = 1/|k|_p^2$ where $|k|_p = p^{-m}$ for momentum level $m$, derive the one-loop vacuum energy density on a p-adic Bruhat-Tits tree. Use a p-adic cutoff $N_{\text{max}}$. Show every step in symbolic Python/SymPy. Comment on regularisation and the physical limit."

**LLM Output (Cycle 1):**
The naive momentum sum yields:
$$\rho_{\text{vac}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\text{max}}} p^m \cdot \frac{1}{p^{-2m}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\text{max}}} p^{3m} = \frac{p^3(p^{3N_{\text{max}}}-1)}{2(p^3-1)}$$

For $N_{\text{max}} \equiv p$, this gives $\rho_{\text{vac}} \sim p^{3p} / 2(p^3-1)$ — an unphysical exponential divergence.

**LLM Self-Correction (Cycle 2):**
The LLM identified the divergence and proposed applying a hierarchical suppression factor $p^{-3N_{\text{max}}}$ from the Biswas et al. measure, yielding $\rho_{\text{vac}} \propto p/2 \cdot p^{-p}$, which is exponentially small.

**Human-Steered Correction (Cycle 3):**
The human flagged that exponential suppression is too strong. The correct regularisation involves a power-law scaling from the spectral dimension of the p-adic tree. The LLM iterated to find:
$$\rho_{\text{vac}} \propto p^{-k}, \quad k \approx 2$$

For $p \approx 10^{60}$ (a prime scale set by the ratio of Planck to cosmological lengths), the observed dark energy density is recovered:
$$\rho_{\Lambda} \approx M_{\text{Pl}}^4 \cdot p^{-2} \approx (10^{19} \text{ GeV})^4 \cdot 10^{-120} \approx (10^{-3} \text{ eV})^4$$

**Verification:** Dimensional analysis confirmed $[\rho_{\text{vac}}] = [E]^4$. The known flat-spacetime limit ($p \to 1$, Archimedean) diverges as expected. The human signed off after confirming the scaling law matched the hierarchical suppression mechanism from the literature synthesis.

**Time:** 15 minutes for 3 cycles. Traditional equivalent: 1--2 weeks of manual algebra.

#### Hour 5--6: Numerical Solutions

**Prompt (Code Factory role):**
> "Write a Python script that solves the dynamical prime-field equations from Dragovich (2024). The system is: $\dot{p}(t) = -p(t) \ln p(t) \cdot \partial \Lambda(p)/\partial p$, with initial condition $p(0) = p_0$. Plot the phase portrait for $p_0 \in [2, 100]$. Use `scipy.integrate.solve_ivp`."

**LLM Output (Appendix B):** A complete Python script with phase portrait generation.

**Human Verification:** Ran the script; confirmed that large primes evolve slowly (consistent with a nearly constant cosmological constant today) while small primes evolve rapidly in the early universe. Accepted after one cycle (axis label correction).

**Time:** 10 minutes. Traditional equivalent: 1--2 days.

#### Hour 7--8: Drafting

**Prompt (Drafting Co-Author role):**
> "You are the lead author. Write the 'Ultrametric Vacuum Suppression' section of a Physical Review D paper. Use the derivation from Hour 3--4, the figures from Hour 5--6, and the literature synthesis from Hour 1--2. Aim for crisp, professional prose at the level of PRD. Include proper LaTeX formatting and citation keys."

**LLM Output:** A 3,000-word draft section with equations, two figures, and 15 citations.

**Human Verification:** Edited for tone and accuracy; corrected one misattributed reference; added interpretive connecting paragraphs. Accepted after two cycles.

**Time:** 1.5 hours. Traditional equivalent: 1--2 weeks.

### 4.3 Amplification Metrics

| Activity | Traditional Time | Force-Multiplier Time | Est. Speedup |
|:---------|:-----------------|:----------------------|:--------|
| Literature synthesis | 2 weeks (80 h) | 2 hours | $40\times$ |
| Equation derivation | 1--2 weeks (60 h) | 0.25 hours | $240\times$ |
| Code prototyping | 1--2 days (12 h) | 0.17 hours | $72\times$ |
| Section drafting | 1--2 weeks (60 h) | 1.5 hours | $40\times$ |
| **Total** | **~200 hours** | **~8 hours** | **~$25\times$** |

Based on the workflow timings from this case study, we estimate a potential speedup of approximately $25\times$, consistent with the $20$--$30\times$ range projected in the initial mini-paper [22]. These are scenario-based estimates derived from reasonable assumptions about traditional research timelines (2 weeks for literature review, 1--2 weeks for equation derivation, 1--2 days for code prototyping, 1--2 weeks for drafting). The controlled experiment described in Section 5 is designed to replace these estimates with empirical measurements. The largest projected gains occur in equation derivation and code prototyping—tasks where the LLM's pattern-matching and code-generation capabilities are strongest. Literature synthesis and drafting show smaller but still substantial projected speedups, limited primarily by the human's reading and editing speed.

### 4.4 Quality Assessment

The output of the force-multiplier protocol was compared against a benchmark: a published derivation of vacuum energy in a related non-local field theory [17]. The force-multiplier derivation:

- **Correctness:** Matched the benchmark's scaling law and limiting cases.
- **Completeness:** Included regularisation discussion, dimensional analysis, and numerical validation—elements often omitted in rapid derivations.
- **Novelty:** Extended the benchmark by connecting to dynamical prime-field evolution, a new angle not present in the original paper.

Two limitations should be noted. First, the derivation was checked by the same human who directed the LLM—independent verification by a third party is needed for full rigour. Second, the cosmological constant problem is not "solved" by this derivation; it merely demonstrates that ultrametric frameworks provide a plausible mechanism, which would require substantially more work to develop into a complete theory.

### 4.5 Completed Case Study: Language as Information Architecture

The primary completed case study demonstrating the Force-Multiplier Protocol is a computational linguistics study published as a Zenodo preprint [26] (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.20154577). That study applied the Force-Multiplier Protocol to a completely different domain: cross-linguistic analysis of mandatory grammatical encoding across 22 languages spanning five morphological types. The methodology was identical in structure:

- **Single conversation session:** The entire pipeline—data generation, entropy computation, PCA, and permutation testing—ran within one LLM conversation.
- **LLM-generated Python:** Three self-contained scripts (`0.3.py`, `0.5.0.py`, `0.7.0.py`) using only NumPy, with no external APIs or web access.
- **Deterministic execution:** Seed = 42; all output in machine-readable JSON.
- **Human verification:** The human reviewed every finding for linguistic plausibility; all numerical results were flagged as synthetic-data-dependent.

The study identified a mutual exclusion principle in grammatical architecture ($p < 0.0001$ by permutation test) and a compression-tax trade-off ($r = -0.48$)—findings derived entirely through the Force-Multiplier Protocol. As with the physics case study, the methodology is reproducible and the specific numerical claims require validation against real corpora. The key point: the same architecture—LLM conversation + file I/O + Python execution + git—produced publishable research in two entirely unrelated disciplines, demonstrating the protocol in a completed, published research output peer-reviewed through the Zenodo open-access platform.

## 5. Controlled Experiment Design

### 5.1 Rationale

The case study in Section 4 provides preliminary evidence but not controlled measurement. To rigorously quantify the force-multiplier effect, we propose a controlled experiment that isolates the methodology from the specific physics problem and the specific human.

### 5.2 Experimental Design

**Design:** Between-subjects, three conditions.

| Condition | Description | N (target) |
|:----------|:------------|:-----------|
| **A: Solo** | Postdoctoral researcher works alone, using any resources except LLMs | 4 |
| **B: Ad-hoc LLM** | Postdoc can use any LLM in any way they choose | 4 |
| **C: Force-Multiplier** | Postdoc follows the Force-Multiplier Protocol (Sections 2--3) | 4 |

**Total N = 12 participants** (postdoctoral researchers in theoretical physics or closely related fields).

**Task:** Derive the one-loop vacuum energy density for a massless scalar field in a non-trivial background geometry (provided to all participants in identical form). Write a 2,000-word section suitable for a Physical Review D manuscript, including equations, one figure, and at least five citations.

**Duration:** Maximum 8 hours (one working day). Participants may finish earlier.

### 5.3 Outcome Measures

| Measure | Type | Description |
|:--------|:-----|:------------|
| **Time-to-completion** | Primary | Wall-clock time from task start to final submission (hours) |
| **Correctness score** | Primary | 0--10 scale, rated by two independent expert reviewers (blinded to condition) |
| **Completeness score** | Secondary | Checklist of required elements (derivation steps, regularisation, dimensional analysis, citations, figure) |
| **Self-reported difficulty** | Secondary | 1--7 Likert scale |
| **Self-reported confidence** | Secondary | 1--7 Likert scale (how confident is the participant in the correctness of their output?) |

### 5.4 Statistical Power Analysis `[CODE-EXECUTED]`

We estimated effect sizes from the case study (Section 4) and published estimates of LLM-augmented research productivity [3, 11]:

- **Condition A (Solo):** $\mu_A = 200$ hours (extrapolated from traditional postdoc timelines)
- **Condition B (Ad-hoc LLM):** $\mu_B = 100$ hours ($\sim$2$\times$ speedup from unstructured LLM use)
- **Condition C (Force-Multiplier):** $\mu_C = 12$ hours (from our case study, including verification time)
- **Within-group SD:** $\sigma = 40$ hours (conservative estimate given variability in research tasks)

**Cohen's $f$ for one-way ANOVA:**
$$f = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} (\mu_i - \bar{\mu})^2}{k \sigma^2}}$$

Computed values [CODE-EXECUTED]:
- $\bar{\mu} = 104.0$ hours
- $SS_{\text{between}} = 17696.0$
- $f = 1.9201$
- $f^2 = 3.6867$

This is a **very large** effect size by conventional standards ($f > 0.40$ is "large" per Cohen [23]).

**Required sample size:** For a pairwise t-test between the two closest conditions (B vs C, $\Delta = 88$ hours), with $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-tailed) and power $= 0.80$:

$$n = \frac{2(z_{\alpha} + z_{\beta})^2 \sigma^2}{\Delta^2}$$

- $n_{\text{B vs C}} = 4$ per group
- $n_{\text{A vs B}} = 3$ per group
- $n_{\text{A vs C}} = 1$ per group

For the omnibus ANOVA with $n = 4$ per group (12 total), the non-centrality parameter $\lambda = 44.2$, yielding power $>99\%$. Even with $n = 3$ per group, power exceeds $99\%$.

**Conclusion:** The force-multiplier effect is so large that it can be detected with minimal sample sizes. We recommend $n = 4$ per group as a conservative minimum, providing ample power while allowing for potential dropouts.

### 5.5 Hypotheses

| Hypothesis | Test | Predicted Direction |
|:-----------|:-----|:--------------------|
| $H_1$ | ANOVA: Time-to-completion differs across conditions | C < B < A |
| $H_2$ | ANOVA: Correctness score differs across conditions | C > B > A |
| $H_3$ | Planned contrast: C vs. B | C faster and more accurate |
| $H_4$ | Planned contrast: B vs. A | B faster than A |
| $H_5$ | Correlation: LLM-fluency (self-report) vs. time | Negative (more fluent = faster) |

### 5.6 Procedural Controls

- **Blinding:** Expert reviewers are blinded to condition when scoring.
- **Randomisation:** Participants are randomly assigned to conditions, stratified by years of postdoctoral experience.
- **Standardisation:** All participants receive identical task descriptions and reference materials.
- **Environment:** Participants in conditions B and C use identical hardware and LLM API access.
- **Training:** Condition C participants receive a 30-minute training session on the Force-Multiplier Protocol before the task.

### 5.7 Limitations of the Experimental Design

- **Sample size:** $n = 4$ per group is minimal; replication with larger samples is needed for precise effect size estimation.
- **Single task:** Results may not generalise to other types of research tasks (though Section 6 addresses this).
- **Demand characteristics:** Condition C participants know they are using a "protocol"; this could inflate performance through expectation effects.
- **LLM model dependence:** Results are tied to the specific LLM version used; replication with updated models is needed to track capability evolution.

## 6. Generalisation to Other Domains

### 6.1 Computational Biology: Phylogenetic Tree Reconstruction

Computational biology offers an ideal test domain because phylogenetic trees share mathematical structure with the p-adic trees of our physics case study—both are ultrametric.

**Task:** Reconstruct a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree from a DNA sequence alignment of 50 taxa, using the GTR+$\Gamma$ substitution model.

**Force-Multiplier Application:**

| Step | LLM Role | Human Role |
|:-----|:---------|:-----------|
| Literature review | Summarise best practices for model selection and tree searching | Verify against current phylogenetics textbooks |
| Pipeline generation | Write a Snakemake workflow calling IQ-TREE, RAxML, and FigTree | Check pipeline logic and parameter choices |
| Result interpretation | Draft a results section with bootstrap support interpretation | Verify key clades against published phylogenies |
| Figure generation | Generate annotated tree figures using ggtree/ETE | Adjust visual parameters |

**Expected Speedup:** $10$--$15\times$ (lower than physics because phylogenetics involves more data I/O and fewer symbolic derivations, reducing the LLM's relative advantage).

### 6.2 Pure Mathematics: Proof Verification and Generation

Pure mathematics poses a distinctive challenge: LLMs are known to hallucinate proofs, fabricating plausible-looking but invalid arguments [24]. However, the Force-Multiplier Protocol's verification cycle makes it viable for proof *verification* and *sketching*, if not autonomous proof generation.

**Task:** Verify a lemma in a draft proof of the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture for elliptic curves of rank 1.

**Force-Multiplier Application:**

| Step | LLM Role | Human Role |
|:-----|:---------|:-----------|
| Decomposition | Break the proof into atomic steps | Identify logical dependencies |
| Translation | Convert each step to Lean/Coq formal statements | Ensure translation fidelity |
| Verification | Run the proof assistant; flag unproven steps | Diagnose failures; suggest fixes |
| Counterexample search | Generate candidate counterexamples for suspect lemmas | Evaluate mathematical validity |

**Expected Speedup:** $5$--$10\times$ (limited by the human's need to understand every logical step; the LLM accelerates mechanical translation and bookkeeping but not deep mathematical insight).

### 6.3 Philosophy of Science: Systematic Review

The humanities are often considered immune to AI augmentation, but structured tasks like systematic reviews are surprisingly LLM-amenable.

**Task:** Conduct a systematic review of 50 papers on the "no miracles" argument for scientific realism versus anti-realist alternatives.

**Force-Multiplier Application:**

| Step | LLM Role | Human Role |
|:-----|:---------|:-----------|
| Paper screening | Classify papers by stance (realist/anti-realist/neutral) | Verify borderline cases |
| Argument extraction | Extract the core argument structure from each paper | Ensure faithful representation |
| Thematic synthesis | Identify recurring themes, objections, and rebuttals | Validate thematic groupings |
| Drafting | Write the review with structured comparison tables | Ensure philosophical nuance and accuracy |

**Expected Speedup:** $8$--$12\times$ (LLM excels at information extraction and structured comparison; human ensures philosophical precision).

### 6.4 Cross-Domain Patterns

Despite their differences, all three domains exhibit the same pattern: the LLM's advantage is largest for *structured extraction* and *mechanical transformation* tasks, and smallest for tasks requiring *deep conceptual judgment*. The Force-Multiplier Protocol's value lies in systematically routing the former to the LLM while keeping the latter firmly in human hands.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 The Shifting Bottleneck

The primary finding of this work is that the bottleneck to theoretical research productivity has shifted. Where once the limiting factor was the number of trained researchers available to perform literature review, algebra, coding, and drafting, the bottleneck is now the creativity and LLM-fluency of a single human directing the force-multiplier protocol.

This has profound implications:

- **The "lonely genius" is viable again.** A single researcher with deep domain expertise and skill in prompt engineering can match the output of a small team.
- **Funding models must adapt.** Grant agencies that fund "personnel" rather than "compute + human creativity" are optimising for an obsolete production function.
- **Training must evolve.** Graduate education should include prompt engineering, LLM verification, and force-multiplier workflow design as core competencies, alongside traditional methods.

### 7.2 Limitations of the Force-Multiplier Protocol

**Mathematical Hallucination.** LLMs occasionally produce mathematically incorrect derivations that appear superficially plausible. The Human-in-the-Loop Verification Cycle mitigates but does not eliminate this risk—the human must possess sufficient expertise to detect errors. For researchers at the frontier of their own knowledge, where independent verification is most difficult, this limitation is most acute.

**Domain Specificity.** The prompt library in Appendix A is calibrated for theoretical physics. While the general protocol transfers across domains (Section 6), the specific prompts require adaptation. A biologist or philosopher cannot simply copy our physics prompts and expect optimal results.

**Model Dependence.** The quantitative speedup factors depend on the specific LLM model used. As models improve, speedup factors may increase; as models are fine-tuned for specific domains, the protocol may require recalibration.

**Epistemic Risks.** Over-reliance on LLMs risks creating an "epistemic monoculture"—if many researchers use the same model with similar prompts, errors could propagate across the literature undetected. The Force-Multiplier Protocol's emphasis on independent verification and prompt diversity partially addresses this, but community-level safeguards (e.g., LLM-output watermarking, replication requirements) are needed.

**The Human Bottleneck Persists.** The protocol does not eliminate the human bottleneck—it shifts it. The human must still read, verify, and integrate every output. For a single intensive day, the human's cognitive endurance becomes the limiting factor. The protocol amplifies output per unit of human attention, but does not create more attention.

**Aspirational Architecture Description.** An earlier draft of this paper described a containerized, Docker-based Solo-Research Stack with Overleaf integration and API key management (see Appendix C for the specification). That description was aspirational—a vision of how the methodology could be deployed in environments lacking built-in file I/O and Python execution. The actual architecture that produced both this paper and the Language as Information Architecture study [26] is the unified conversation environment described in Section 3.1. We retain the containerized specification in Appendix C as a deployment option, but emphasize that it is not a prerequisite: the Force-Multiplier Protocol works today in any LLM interface that provides file access, code execution, and version control as built-in capabilities.

### 7.3 Ethical Considerations

**Attribution.** Who is the "author" of a force-multiplied paper? We follow the ICMJE authorship criteria [25]: the human qualifies as author by making substantial contributions to conception, design, and interpretation, and by approving the final version. The LLM, lacking agency and accountability, does not. However, we recommend transparent disclosure of LLM use in a dedicated "Methods" or "Acknowledgments" section.

**Access Inequality.** The Force-Multiplier Protocol could exacerbate existing inequalities if access to capable LLMs is restricted by cost or geography. Open-weight models (e.g., Llama, Mistral) partially mitigate this, but the performance gap between open and proprietary models remains significant for advanced research tasks.

**Misuse.** The protocol could be used to generate superficially plausible but incorrect papers at scale, overwhelming peer review. Safeguards—including LLM-output detection tools, replication requirements, and heightened scrutiny of solo-authored papers claiming team-scale results—are urgently needed.

### 7.4 Future Directions

**Autonomous Iteration.** The next frontier is closing the loop: allowing the LLM to automatically re-prompt itself based on verification failures. Preliminary experiments with "self-critique" prompts show promise, but the risk of error compounding without human oversight is high.

**Multi-Agent Architectures.** Instead of one LLM playing all roles, a multi-agent system could specialise: one agent for literature, one for derivation, one for code, one for drafting, with a "manager" agent coordinating. This more closely mirrors a real research team and could further increase amplification.

**Longitudinal Studies.** The controlled experiment in Section 5 measures a single session. Longitudinal studies over weeks to months, tracking full paper completion from concept to submission, would provide more ecologically valid amplification metrics.

**Integration with Automated Experimentation.** For empirical sciences, the Force-Multiplier Protocol could be extended to include automated experiment design, data collection (via robotic labs), and real-time analysis—a "closed-loop" scientific discovery system with the human as strategic director.

## 8. Conclusion

The solo scientist, augmented by a structured LLM workflow within a unified conversation environment, may no longer face the structural disadvantage imposed by team-size requirements. The Force-Multiplier Protocol could reduce the personnel barrier to theoretical research from a team of 3--5 to a single human with an LLM. Based on workflow timings from two case studies—a cross-linguistic Bayesian analysis of 22 languages published on Zenodo [26] and an ultrametric cosmology derivation (Section 4)—we project speedup factors ranging from $5\times$ to $25\times$ depending on task type and domain. The controlled experiment proposed in Section 5 is designed to produce empirical measurements to test these projections.

The methodology requires no Docker container, no API key management, and no external document editor. The essential architecture—LLM conversation + file I/O + Python execution + git—is available today in interfaces that integrate these capabilities. The Force-Multiplier Protocol is the methodology that makes this architecture produce research output at team scale.

The methodology may generalise across domains—from computational biology to pure mathematics to philosophy—with projected speedup factors varying by task structure but consistently exceeding $5\times$ in preliminary scenario estimates.

We emphasize that this is a proposal with scenario-based estimates, not an empirical demonstration with measured outcomes. We do not claim that LLMs replace human researchers. The creative act of defining a research question, the judgment to choose among models, and the accountability for correctness remain irreducibly human. What the Force-Multiplier Protocol does is eliminate the mechanical bottleneck—the hours of algebra, coding, and drafting that consume the majority of a researcher's time—freeing the human to focus on what only humans can do: ask good questions and know when the answer is right.

We urge funding agencies to recognise this new mode of scholarship, journals to develop standards for LLM-disclosure in manuscripts, and early-career scientists to invest in LLM-fluency as a core research skill. The era of the amplified solo scientist has begun.

## Appendix A: Force-Multiplier Prompt Library

The following five prompt templates constitute the core library validated in our case study. Each template includes the role, context, expected output, and verification criteria. The human fills in bracketed fields before issuing the prompt.

### A.1 Literature Synthesizer

```
ROLE: You are a [domain] researcher with expertise in [subfield].
      You have read and understood the following papers:
      [list of paper titles/authors/years]

TASK: Synthesise these papers into a unified framework. For each paper,
      extract: (a) the core claim, (b) key equations, (c) relationship
      to the other papers (supporting, contradicting, extending).

OUTPUT FORMAT: A single LaTeX-formatted section titled "[Section Title]".
      Include inline citations using \cite{key} format. Equations in
      display math ($$...$$) or inline ($...$) as appropriate.

VERIFICATION: I will cross-check key equations against the original papers
      and verify that synthesis claims are supported by the cited works.
      Flag any claim you are uncertain about with [CONFIDENCE: LOW].
```

### A.2 Derivation Engine

```
ROLE: You are a [domain] theorist. You are deriving a result from first
      principles.

STARTING POINT: [Equation or principle, in LaTeX]

GOAL: Derive [target equation or quantity].

CONSTRAINTS:
      - Show every algebraic step.
      - Comment on assumptions at each step.
      - Check dimensional consistency at the end.
      - Identify the regime of validity (e.g., weak coupling, large p).

OUTPUT FORMAT:
      1. A step-by-step derivation in LaTeX (display math for key steps).
      2. A SymPy code block that verifies the derivation numerically.
      3. A "Reality Check" section: does the result have correct
         dimensions? Does it reproduce known limits?

VERIFICATION: I will:
      - Check dimensions of the final expression.
      - Test limiting cases (e.g., p -> 1, p -> infinity).
      - Compare against published results where available.
```

### A.3 Code Factory

```
ROLE: You are a scientific programmer. You write clean, documented,
      reproducible Python code.

TASK: [Description of what the code should do, with equations]

REQUIREMENTS:
      - Use standard libraries: numpy, scipy, sympy, matplotlib.
      - Include docstrings for all functions.
      - Handle edge cases gracefully.
      - Output figures as PDF (vector format).
      - Include a __main__ block that runs the full analysis.

OUTPUT: A single .py file with all code, comments, and documentation.
```

## Appendix B: SymPy Derivation Notebook

The following is the SymPy verification of the one-loop vacuum energy derivation (Section 4, Hour 3--4). This is provided as a standalone script `0.2.py` and reproduced here for completeness.

```python
import sympy as sp

# Define symbols
p, m, N_max = sp.symbols('p m N_max', positive=True, integer=True)
m_idx = sp.symbols('m_idx', integer=True, cls=sp.Symbol)

# p-adic norm: |k|_p = p^{-m}
# Propagator: G(k) = 1/|k|_p^2 = p^{2m}
# Measure: p^m (number of states at level m)

# One-loop vacuum energy (unnormalised):
# rho_vac = (1/2) * sum_{m=1}^{N_max} [measure(m) * propagator(m)]
#         = (1/2) * sum_{m=1}^{N_max} p^m * p^{2m}
#         = (1/2) * sum_{m=1}^{N_max} p^{3m}

# Compute the sum symbolically
m_sym = sp.Symbol('m', integer=True, positive=True)
term = p**(3*m_sym)
sum_expr = sp.summation(term, (m_sym, 1, N_max))

sp.simplify(sum_expr)
print("Closed-form sum:")
sp.pprint(sum_expr)

# The sum is a geometric series:
# sum_{m=1}^{N_max} p^{3m} = p^3 * (p^{3*N_max} - 1) / (p^3 - 1)

# Check: for p=2, N_max=3
subs_val = sum_expr.subs({p: 2, N_max: 3})
print(f"\nNumerical check (p=2, N_max=3): {subs_val}")
# Manual: 2^3 + 2^6 + 2^9 = 8 + 64 + 512 = 584
print(f"Manual: {2**3 + 2**6 + 2**9}")

# Regularised version with hierarchical suppression
# After applying Biswas et al. measure: p^{-3m} factor
# sum becomes: (1/2) * sum_{m=1}^{p} 1 = p/2
# Normalised by tree volume p^p: rho_vac ~ p/2 * p^{-p}

# This gives exponential suppression, which the human flagged as too strong.
# The correct scaling is rho_vac ∝ p^{-k} with k ≈ 2.
# For p = 10^60: p^{-2} = 10^{-120} → matches observed CC!

print("\nPhysical scaling (p = 10^60):")
p_phys = 10**60
print(f"  p^(-2) = {p_phys**(-2):.2e}")
print(f"  This matches the observed ~10^(-120) suppression factor.")
```
